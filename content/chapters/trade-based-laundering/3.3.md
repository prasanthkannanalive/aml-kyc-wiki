## 3.3 Network and counterparty analysis

TBML is rarely a single transaction — it is a network of related entities, transactions and flows. Counterparty analysis reveals the network structure that individual transactions hide.

### Counterparty red flags

**New or recently-incorporated counterparties** — entities with no trading history, recently registered, or with minimal verifiable business presence.

**Counterparties in high-risk jurisdictions** — entities in jurisdictions with weak AML controls, high corruption, or sanctions exposure.

**Counterparties with no web presence** — companies that cannot be found online, have no physical address, or have no verifiable employees.

**Multiple transactions with same counterparty** — repeated transactions with the same counterparty at unusual intervals or volumes.

**Counterparties that are shells** — entities with no employees, no office, no verifiable business activity, or that share addresses/phone numbers with other entities.

### Network analysis techniques

**Ownership mapping** — identifying the ultimate beneficial owners of counterparties, especially through layers of holding companies or nominees.

**Address and contact analysis** — checking whether counterparties share addresses, phone numbers, email domains or registered agents with each other or with the customer.

**Transaction pattern analysis** — looking for circular flows (A pays B, B pays C, C pays A), rapid movement through multiple entities, or structuring across multiple transactions.

**Geographic analysis** — mapping the trade corridors and identifying unusual routing (goods shipped through multiple jurisdictions unnecessarily).

**Temporal analysis** — looking for transactions that cluster in time, precede or follow suspicious events, or follow patterns inconsistent with business cycles.

### The shell company problem

Shell companies are the primary vehicle for TBML counterparties. They have no real business activity but provide a legal entity for invoicing and payment.

**Shell company indicators**:
- Recently incorporated with no trading history
- No employees or verifiable business activity
- Registered at a virtual office or mail forwarding address
- Owned by nominees or through layers of holding companies
- Multiple shell companies sharing the same registered agent or address
- Companies that exist only to invoice and receive payments

**Detection requires**:
- **Corporate registry checks** — verifying company existence, directors, shareholders
- **OSINT** — searching for web presence, business activity, employee reviews
- **Beneficial ownership analysis** — identifying the ultimate owners through layers of ownership
- **Pattern analysis** — looking for networks of related shell companies

### The correspondent banking dimension

In correspondent banking, the correspondent bank processes payments on behalf of a respondent bank. The correspondent sees only the payment instruction — not the underlying trade, not the customer relationship, not the counterparty analysis.

This creates a blind spot where TBML networks can operate: the respondent bank may have weak controls, and the correspondent bank has no visibility into the trade network.

**Mitigation** — correspondent banks should:
- Conduct enhanced due diligence on respondent banks
- Require respondent banks to certify their AML controls
- Monitor for unusual transaction patterns that may indicate TBML
- Share information with respondent banks about suspicious activity
