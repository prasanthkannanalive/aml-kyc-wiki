## 4.1 Open-account TBML and the correspondent blind spot

Open-account trade finance and correspondent banking create the largest blind spots in TBML detection — places where illicit value moves with minimal bank visibility.

### Open-account trade finance

In open-account arrangements, the buyer pays the seller directly after shipment or delivery — no bank guarantee, no documentary credit, minimal bank involvement. The bank processes the payment but has no obligation to review trade documents.

**Why this creates TBML risk**:

- **No document review** — the bank sees only the payment instruction, not the invoice, bill of lading or other trade documents
- **No trade verification** — the bank cannot verify that goods actually shipped, that prices are fair, or that counterparties are legitimate
- **Minimal customer interaction** — open-account customers may have less ongoing monitoring than documentary credit customers
- **Higher volume** — open account is the most common trade finance method, so the blind spot affects the majority of trade transactions

**The control gap** — banks that offer open-account services often apply the same AML controls as for any corporate payment: customer due diligence, transaction monitoring, sanctions screening. But they lack the **trade-specific controls** needed for TBML: document review, price benchmarking, counterparty verification.

### The correspondent banking blind spot

Correspondent banking is the practice where one bank (correspondent) provides services to another bank (respondent) in a different jurisdiction. The correspondent processes payments on behalf of the respondent but has limited visibility into the respondent's customers or transactions.

**TBML in correspondent banking**:

- **Respondent bank with weak controls** — the respondent bank may have inadequate AML controls, allowing TBML to flow through its customers
- **Correspondent bank with no visibility** — the correspondent sees only payment instructions, not the underlying trade, not the customer relationship, not the counterparty
- **Nested correspondent relationships** — payments may pass through multiple correspondent banks, each seeing only its own transaction
- **High-risk jurisdictions** — correspondent relationships with banks in jurisdictions with weak AML controls or sanctions exposure

**The FATF Correspondent Banking Guidance** requires correspondent banks to:

- Conduct enhanced due diligence on respondent banks
- Understand the respondent's business, customers and risk profile
- Monitor respondent transactions for suspicious activity
- Terminate relationships with high-risk or uncooperative respondents

But even with these controls, the correspondent bank cannot see the underlying trade — only the payment flow.

### The combined blind spot

The most dangerous TBML scenario combines open-account trade finance with correspondent banking:

- A company in Country A (high-risk) imports goods from a company in Country B (low-risk)
- The payment flows through correspondent banks in Country C
- No bank in the chain sees the full picture: the respondent bank sees only its customer, the correspondent sees only the payment, and no one verifies the trade

This is where TBML networks operate — exploiting the gaps between institutions, jurisdictions and information systems.

### Mitigation strategies

**For open-account trade finance**:

- Implement trade-specific monitoring rules (pricing, documentation, counterparty)
- Require trade documents for high-risk transactions
- Conduct enhanced due diligence on trade finance customers
- Share information with trade finance teams and compliance

**For correspondent banking**:

- Conduct thorough due diligence on respondent banks
- Require respondents to certify their AML controls
- Monitor for unusual transaction patterns
- Share information about suspicious activity
- Consider exiting relationships with high-risk or uncooperative respondents
