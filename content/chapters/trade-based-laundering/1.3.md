## 1.3 The FATF/Egmont TBML reports and typologies

The FATF and Egmont Group have published the definitive research on TBML — two reports that every compliance professional should know.

### The 2006 report — "Trade-Based Money Laundering"

The foundational report identified the core techniques and red flags. It established that TBML typically involves:

- **Over-invoicing** — declaring goods worth more than their actual value to move money out of a country
- **Under-invoicing** — declaring goods worth less than their actual value to move money into a country or evade duties
- **Multiple invoicing** — issuing multiple invoices for the same goods to multiply the apparent value
- **Phantom shipments** — documenting trade that never occurs
- **Misdescription** — describing goods inaccurately to evade tariffs, sanctions or inspection

### The 2020 report — "TBML: Trends and Developments"

The updated report added:

- **Free-trade zones** as emerging TBML hubs
- **Digital trade documents** and the challenges of verifying electronic records
- **TBML-TF** — trade-based terrorist financing through over-invoicing and front companies
- **Services TBML** — using consulting, management fees and intellectual property to launder
- **E-commerce** as a new vector for small-value TBML

### The risk indicators

The FATF/Egmont "Trade-Based Money Laundering: Risk Indicators" document lists specific red flags:

**Documentation red flags**:
- Invoices that don't match shipping documents
- Missing or inconsistent certificates of origin
- Bills of lading that don't match commercial invoices
- Documents with obvious errors or inconsistencies
- Photocopied or low-quality documents where originals are expected

**Pricing red flags**:
- Prices significantly above or below market rates
- Round-number prices that don't reflect negotiation
- Prices that don't match the quantity or quality of goods
- Dramatic price changes between orders for the same goods

**Counterparty red flags**:
- New counterparties with no trading history
- Counterparties in high-risk jurisdictions
- Counterparties that are shell companies or recently incorporated
- Multiple transactions with the same counterparty at unusual intervals

**Transaction red flags**:
- Payments that don't match invoice amounts
- Payments to third parties not named on invoices
- Payments through multiple accounts or jurisdictions
- Payments that don't match the timing of shipment

### The typologies that matter

**Over-invoicing** — the most common technique. Company A "exports" goods worth $100,000 but invoices Company B for $1 million. The $900,000 difference is laundered value. Detection requires comparing invoice prices to market benchmarks.

**Under-invoicing** — the reverse. Company A "exports" goods worth $1 million but invoices for $100,000. The importer pays less duty and the $900,000 difference can be settled separately through informal channels.

**Phantom shipments** — documenting trade that never occurs. No goods move; only invoices and payments. Detection requires verifying actual shipment through shipping documents, customs records or inspection reports.

**Multiple invoicing** — issuing multiple invoices for the same goods. One shipment generates several payments, multiplying the apparent value. Detection requires cross-referencing invoice numbers, dates and quantities.

**Misdescription** — describing goods inaccurately to evade tariffs, sanctions or inspection. High-value goods declared as low-value; sanctioned goods disguised as permitted alternatives.

**Services TBML** — using consulting fees, management fees, intellectual property royalties or technical services to launder. No physical goods move; only invoices for intangible services. Increasingly common because it is harder to verify than goods.
