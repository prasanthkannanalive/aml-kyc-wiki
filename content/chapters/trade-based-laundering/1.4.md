## 1.4 The trade finance lifecycle and where it breaks

Understanding the trade finance lifecycle is essential for TBML detection — each stage has control points and vulnerabilities.

### The lifecycle stages

**Contract stage** — buyer and seller agree on goods, price, quantity, delivery terms and payment method. This is where the invoice price is set — the value transfer mechanism.

**Documentation stage** — seller prepares commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, certificate of origin and other documents. The buyer's bank may review these for documentary credit transactions.

**Shipment stage** — goods are transported from seller to buyer. The bill of lading serves as proof of shipment and title to goods.

**Payment stage** — buyer's bank pays seller's bank (or seller directly for open account). For documentary credits, payment is against documents; for open account, payment follows shipment or delivery terms.

**Settlement stage** — funds move through correspondent banks, currency conversion occurs, and the transaction is recorded.

### Where the controls break

**At the contract stage** — the bank has no visibility. The invoice price is agreed between buyer and seller; the bank only sees it later. There is no control point for verifying price fairness at this stage.

**At the documentation stage** — banks review documents for documentary credits, but the review is for *compliance with credit terms*, not *accuracy of trade*. A bank checks that documents are present and consistent with each other, not that they reflect reality. Forged documents that are internally consistent pass review.

**At the shipment stage** — banks have no visibility. They do not track goods; they rely on shipping documents. A bill of lading can be genuine while the goods described never existed.

**At the payment stage** — this is where the bank's control is strongest but still limited. For documentary credits, payment is made against documents — the bank's obligation is to pay if documents comply, regardless of whether the trade is real. For open account, the bank processes the payment with minimal trade-specific review.

**At the settlement stage** — funds move through correspondent banks, often in different jurisdictions. Each bank sees only its own transaction; none sees the full trade cycle.

### The documentary credit problem

Documentary credits (letters of credit) are supposed to provide security — the bank pays against documents, not against goods. This creates a fundamental vulnerability: **the bank's obligation is to documents, not to reality**. A fraudulent seller can present perfect documents for goods that don't exist, and the bank must pay.

This is why TBML thrives in trade finance: the system is designed to trust documents, and TBML exploits that trust.

### Open account versus documentary credit

**Open account** — buyer pays seller directly after shipment or delivery. The bank processes the payment but has no documentary review obligation. Lower bank involvement means lower control but also lower visibility — the bank sees only the payment, not the trade documents.

**Documentary credit** — bank pays against documents. Higher bank involvement means document review, but the review is for compliance with credit terms, not trade accuracy. The bank's obligation to pay against compliant documents creates the vulnerability.

**Collection** — documents are sent through banks for payment, but the bank does not guarantee payment. Intermediate control level.

### The correspondent banking blind spot

In correspondent banking, the correspondent bank processes payments on behalf of a respondent bank in another jurisdiction. The correspondent sees only the payment instruction — not the underlying trade documents, not the customer relationship, not the full transaction context. This creates a blind spot where TBML can operate: the respondent bank may have weak controls, and the correspondent bank has no visibility into the trade.
