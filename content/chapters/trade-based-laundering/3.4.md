## 3.4 Building TBML monitoring rules

TBML monitoring requires rules that look beyond transaction patterns to the trade context — invoices, shipments, counterparties and pricing. This chapter covers how to build effective TBML monitoring.

### Rule categories

**Pricing rules** — flag transactions where invoice prices deviate significantly from market benchmarks or historical patterns.

- Price deviation > X% from market average for product category
- Price deviation > X% from customer's historical average for same product
- Round-number pricing above threshold
- Price changes > X% between consecutive orders for identical goods

**Documentation rules** — flag inconsistencies across trade documents.

- Invoice amount doesn't match payment amount
- Invoice date after bill of lading date (impossible sequence)
- Missing or invalid certificates of origin
- Multiple invoices for same shipment (detected through invoice number patterns or dates)

**Counterparty rules** — flag transactions with high-risk counterparties.

- First-time counterparty above threshold
- Counterparty in high-risk jurisdiction
- Counterparty that is newly-incorporated or has no web presence
- Multiple transactions with same counterparty at unusual intervals

**Network rules** — flag patterns that suggest TBML networks.

- Circular payment flows (A→B→C→A)
- Rapid movement through multiple entities
- Payments to third parties not named on invoices
- Transactions that cluster in time or follow unusual patterns

**Geographic rules** — flag unusual trade corridors or routing.

- Goods shipped through multiple jurisdictions unnecessarily
- Trade corridors inconsistent with customer's business model
- Transactions involving free-trade zones or high-risk jurisdictions

### Data requirements

Effective TBML monitoring requires data that traditional AML systems may not have:

- **Trade documents** — invoices, bills of lading, certificates of origin, packing lists
- **Market pricing data** — benchmarks for product categories
- **Counterparty data** — corporate registry information, beneficial ownership, web presence
- **Shipping data** — tracking numbers, port records, customs declarations
- **Historical trade data** — customer's transaction history, pricing patterns

### Integration challenges

TBML monitoring is harder to implement than traditional AML monitoring because:

- **Data fragmentation** — trade documents may be in separate systems from payment data
- **Document processing** — invoices and bills of lading are often PDFs or images requiring OCR or manual review
- **Market data availability** — pricing benchmarks may not exist for specialized goods or services
- **Counterparty verification** — corporate registry data may be incomplete or unavailable for foreign entities

### Alert management

TBML alerts require different handling than traditional AML alerts:

- **Document review** — analysts must review trade documents, not just transaction records
- **Market research** — pricing anomalies require market knowledge to evaluate
- **Counterparty investigation** — shell company detection requires corporate registry checks and OSINT
- **Escalation** — TBML cases often require coordination with trade finance teams, compliance, and sometimes law enforcement

### Testing and validation

TBML monitoring rules must be tested against:

- **Known TBML cases** — historical cases used to validate rule effectiveness
- **False positive analysis** — tracking alert outcomes to tune thresholds
- **Regulatory expectations** — ensuring rules meet supervisory requirements
- **Real-world scenarios** — testing against actual trade patterns, not just theoretical cases
