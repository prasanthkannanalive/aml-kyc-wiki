## 2.1 Over-invoicing and under-invoicing

Over- and under-invoicing are the most common TBML techniques — simple price manipulation that moves value through the banking system while creating plausible trade cover.

### Over-invoicing — moving value out

**Mechanism** — Company A in Country X "exports" goods to Company B in Country Y. The goods are worth $100,000 on the open market, but the invoice declares $1 million. Company B pays $1 million; $900,000 of that is laundered value.

**Why it works** — the importer pays more than the goods are worth, but the excess is hidden in the invoice price. Customs may flag the high value, but if the goods are genuinely shipped (even if undervalued), the transaction looks like aggressive pricing rather than crime.

**Detection** — compare invoice prices to market benchmarks. If "industrial valves" are invoiced at 10x market price, that's a red flag. Requires market knowledge or third-party pricing databases.

**Variants**:
- **Gradual over-invoicing** — small overstatements across many transactions to avoid detection
- **Selective over-invoicing** — only certain product lines or customers
- **Currency manipulation** — invoicing in a currency that has appreciated, creating apparent value without real transfer

### Under-invoicing — moving value in or evading duties

**Mechanism** — Company A "exports" goods worth $1 million but invoices Company B for $100,000. The importer pays less duty and the $900,000 difference can be settled through informal channels (cash, crypto, third-party payments).

**Why it works** — the importer saves on duties and the seller receives less formal payment but may receive the difference through informal channels. The under-invoicing also facilitates **capital flight** — moving value out of a country without triggering exchange controls.

**Detection** — same as over-invoicing but reversed: prices significantly below market. Also watch for payments that don't match invoice amounts — the difference may be settled separately.

**The dual purpose** — under-invoicing serves both money laundering (moving value informally) and tax evasion (avoiding duties). The same technique can serve both purposes simultaneously.

### The market-price problem

The core detection challenge is establishing **market price**. For standardized commodities (oil, grain, metals), market prices are well-known. For specialized goods (custom machinery, intellectual property, services), market prices are opaque — there may be no comparable transactions.

This is why TBML detection requires:

- **Industry knowledge** — analysts who understand the products and markets
- **Third-party data** — pricing databases, trade data providers, industry associations
- **Historical analysis** — comparing prices over time for the same customer and product
- **Peer comparison** — comparing prices across customers for similar goods

### The documentation trail

Over- and under-invoicing leave documentary evidence:

- Invoices with prices that don't match market rates
- Inconsistent pricing between related transactions
- Prices that don't match the quantity or quality described
- Round-number prices that don't reflect negotiation
- Prices that change dramatically between orders for identical goods

The analyst's job is to **question the price** — not assume it's correct because the documents look professional.
