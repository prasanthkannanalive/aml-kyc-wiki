## 2.2 Phantom shipments and multiple invoicing

Phantom shipments and multiple invoicing are more sophisticated TBML techniques that create false trade documentation or multiply the apparent value of real trade.

### Phantom shipments — trade that never happens

**Mechanism** — Company A "exports" goods to Company B. Invoices, bills of lading and shipping documents are created, but no goods actually move. Company B pays for goods that don't exist; the payment is the laundered value.

**How it works**:
- **Forged documents** — bills of lading, certificates of origin and other documents are fabricated or altered
- **Complicit shipping agents** — corrupt freight forwarders or shipping companies create false documentation
- **Shell company networks** — both buyer and seller are controlled by the same criminal network; the "trade" is internal to the network
- **Free-trade zones** — goods may briefly enter a free-trade zone to create documentation, then never leave

**Detection** — phantom shipments are the hardest TBML technique to detect because the documents can be perfect. Red flags include:
- No evidence of actual shipment (no tracking numbers, no port records)
- Bills of lading from obscure or recently-registered shipping companies
- Inconsistencies between documents (dates, quantities, descriptions)
- Counterparties with no trading history or verifiable business presence
- Repeated transactions with the same counterparty at unusual intervals

**The verification problem** — banks cannot verify that goods actually shipped. They rely on documents, and documents can be forged. Customs authorities have more verification capability but focus on duties and tariffs, not AML.

### Multiple invoicing — multiplying the same goods

**Mechanism** — Company A ships $100,000 worth of goods to Company B but issues three separate invoices for $100,000 each, claiming the shipment was split into three consignments. Company B pays $300,000 for $100,000 worth of goods; the $200,000 difference is laundered value.

**Variants**:
- **Same goods, multiple invoices** — one shipment documented as several
- **Split shipments** — goods are actually split into multiple shipments, but each is invoiced separately to obscure the total value
- **Duplicate invoices** — the same invoice is submitted multiple times for payment

**Detection** — multiple invoicing leaves documentary evidence:
- Invoices with sequential numbers issued on the same date
- Invoices for identical goods with identical descriptions
- Bills of lading showing a single shipment but multiple invoices
- Payment patterns showing multiple payments for what appears to be one shipment

**The customs problem** — customs authorities see each shipment separately and may not connect multiple invoices to a single consignment, especially if shipments go through different ports or brokers.

### Combined techniques

Phantom shipments and multiple invoicing are often combined with over-invoicing: a phantom shipment is documented at an inflated price, multiplying the laundered value. Or a real shipment is invoiced multiple times at inflated prices, creating exponential value transfer.

The detection challenge is that each individual transaction may look plausible — it's the pattern across transactions that reveals the scheme.
