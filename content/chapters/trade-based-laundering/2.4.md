## 2.4 Goods-based laundering: commodities and free-trade zones

Goods-based laundering uses physical commodities to move value — exploiting price volatility, opaque markets and free-trade zones to obscure the true value of trade.

### Commodity TBML

**High-value, low-verification commodities** — gold, diamonds, art, antiques, rare earths — are attractive for TBML because:

- **Opaque pricing** — there is no single "market price" for a diamond or painting; value is subjective
- **Easy to transport** — high value-to-weight ratio makes physical transport efficient
- **Difficult to verify** — authenticity and value require expert appraisal
- **Weak documentation** — certificates of authenticity can be forged

**Gold TBML** — gold is the classic commodity for TBML because it is universally accepted, easily convertible to cash, and has relatively transparent pricing (though premiums and fabrication costs create manipulation room). Schemes involve over-invoicing gold shipments, smuggling gold through free-trade zones, or using gold as a settlement mechanism for other TBML.

**Precious stones** — diamonds and gemstones are even more opaque than gold. Kimberley Process certificates can be forged, and the "value" of a diamond is whatever the seller claims. TBML through precious stones often involves invoice manipulation where the declared value has no verifiable basis.

### Free-trade zones — the TBML hub

Free-trade zones (FTZs) are designated areas where goods can be stored, processed or transshipped without customs duties or inspections. They are legitimate economic tools — but also TBML havens.

**Why FTZs attract TBML**:

- **Weak oversight** — goods in FTZs are often exempt from customs inspection and documentation requirements
- **Transshipment** — goods can enter an FTZ, be repackaged or relabeled, and exit as different products
- **No customs visibility** — customs authorities may not know what goods are in the zone or where they came from
- **Shell company concentration** — FTZs attract trading companies, many of which are shells or fronts

**FTZ TBML techniques**:

- **Re-labeling** — goods enter as one product and exit as another (sanctioned goods become civilian products)
- **Value manipulation** — goods are "processed" in the FTZ and re-exported at inflated prices
- **Phantom trade** — documents show goods moving through the FTZ, but nothing actually moves
- **Transit trade** — goods pass through the FTZ without entering the domestic market, avoiding customs scrutiny

### The verification challenge

Goods-based TBML is the hardest to detect because:

- **Physical verification is expensive** — banks cannot inspect goods; they rely on documents
- **Appraisal is subjective** — the value of art, antiques or gemstones requires expert judgment
- **FTZ opacity** — goods in free-trade zones are often invisible to customs authorities
- **Transshipment complexity** — goods may pass through multiple jurisdictions, changing hands and documentation at each step

**Detection requires**:

- **Third-party inspection** — independent verification of goods, quantity and quality
- **Market benchmarking** — comparing prices to objective market data where available
- **Counterparty analysis** — investigating whether counterparties are legitimate trading companies or shells
- **Pattern analysis** — looking for unusual trading patterns, rapid transshipment, or repeated transactions with the same counterparties
