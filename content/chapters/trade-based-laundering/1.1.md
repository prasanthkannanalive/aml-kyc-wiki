## 1.1 What trade-based money laundering is

Trade-based money laundering (TBML) uses the global trade system — imports, exports, invoices and shipping documents — to move value across borders while disguising its origin or purpose. The trade may be real, partially real or entirely fictitious; what matters is that the documents create a plausible cover story for moving money.

### The basic mechanism

TBML exploits a structural feature of international trade: **the invoice price is the value transfer mechanism**. When Company A in Country X "exports" goods to Company B in Country Y for $1 million, the invoice says $1 million — but the actual goods may be worth $100,000, or may not exist at all. The $900,000 difference is the laundered value, moved through the banking system as a legitimate trade payment.

The bank sees: a corporate customer, a trade invoice, a payment to a foreign supplier. Everything looks commercial. What the bank cannot see: whether the goods actually shipped, whether the price reflects market value, or whether the "supplier" is a shell company controlled by the same criminal network.

### TBML versus trade-based terrorist financing

TBML and TBML-TF (trade-based terrorist financing) follow the same techniques but different purposes. TBML typically involves proceeds of crime being cleaned; TBML-TF involves legitimate trade being exploited to move value to terrorist organizations — often through over-invoicing where the "supplier" is a front company that skims the excess.

### The scale of the problem

FATF and Egmont estimates suggest TBML accounts for a significant portion of global money laundering — some estimates put it at 5-10% of all laundering, though precise figures are impossible because TBML is designed to be invisible. The 2020 FATF/Egmont report "Trade-Based Money Laundering: Trends and Developments" documented cases involving billions of dollars moved through invoice manipulation alone.

### Why it's hard to detect

Traditional AML monitoring looks for unusual transaction patterns: large amounts, rapid movement, structuring below thresholds. TBML transactions look completely normal — they are business payments for goods, processed through trade finance channels, with documentation that appears legitimate. The crime is in the *content* of the trade, not the pattern of the payment.

This is why TBML requires a different analytical approach: instead of asking "is this transaction suspicious?", the analyst must ask "is this trade real, and is the price real?" — questions that require documentary evidence, market knowledge and counterparty analysis.
