## 4.2 Documentary credits and LC fraud

Documentary credits (letters of credit) are supposed to provide security — the bank pays against documents, not against goods. This creates a fundamental vulnerability that TBML and LC fraud exploit.

### How documentary credits work

A documentary credit is a bank's undertaking to pay the seller (beneficiary) when the seller presents documents complying with the credit terms. The buyer's bank (issuing bank) pays against documents, not against goods.

**The process**:

1. Buyer and seller agree on trade terms and payment method (LC)
2. Buyer's bank issues LC to seller's bank (advising bank)
3. Seller ships goods and prepares documents (invoice, bill of lading, etc.)
4. Seller presents documents to advising bank
5. Advising bank reviews documents for compliance with LC terms
6. If documents comply, advising bank pays seller (or negotiates documents to issuing bank)
7. Issuing bank reimburses advising bank

**The critical feature** — the bank's obligation is to pay if documents comply, **regardless of whether the goods exist, whether they shipped, or whether the trade is real**. This is the "independence principle" — banks deal in documents, not goods.

### LC fraud and TBML

LC fraud exploits the independence principle:

**Fraudulent documents** — forged bills of lading, fake certificates of origin, altered invoices. The documents look compliant but are fabricated.

**Non-existent goods** — documents describe goods that don't exist. The seller presents perfect documents for phantom shipments.

**Complicit shipping agents** — corrupt freight forwarders or shipping companies create false documentation.

**Shell company networks** — buyer and seller are controlled by the same network; the "trade" is internal.

**TBML through LCs** — over-invoicing through inflated invoices, phantom shipments through forged documents, or multiple invoicing through multiple LCs for the same goods.

### The bank's dilemma

The bank faces a fundamental conflict:

- **Obligation to pay** — if documents comply with LC terms, the bank must pay, even if it suspects fraud
- **Obligation to prevent fraud** — banks have AML obligations to detect and report suspicious activity
- **Limited verification** — banks cannot verify that goods actually shipped or that prices are fair

This creates a situation where banks may be legally required to pay for fraudulent trade — the "fraud exception" is narrow and varies by jurisdiction.

### Red flags in LC transactions

**Document inconsistencies** — documents that don't match each other or that contain obvious errors.

**Unusual LC terms** — LCs with unusual requirements, excessive documentation, or terms that don't match industry norms.

**High-risk counterparties** — beneficiaries or applicants in high-risk jurisdictions or with no verifiable business presence.

**Unusual trade patterns** — LCs for goods that don't match the customer's business, unusually large LCs for new customers, or LCs that don't match shipping patterns.

**Payment patterns** — payments to third parties, payments that don't match LC amounts, or payments through multiple accounts.

### The compliance response

Banks should:

- **Screen all LC parties** — applicants, beneficiaries, and other parties against sanctions lists
- **Review documents critically** — look for inconsistencies, errors, or signs of forgery
- **Apply enhanced due diligence** — for high-risk transactions, customers, or jurisdictions
- **Monitor for patterns** — look for repeated LCs with the same counterparties or unusual trade patterns
- **Report suspicious activity** — file SARs when LCs appear to facilitate TBML or fraud
