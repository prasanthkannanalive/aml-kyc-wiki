Identity, application and first-party fraud Most of the frauds in this module attack an account that already exists. The frauds in this chapter attack the moment an account or loan is created, or abuse it from the start. They share one question: was the person who applied who they said they were, and did they mean to honour what they signed? The answers separate identity theft, where a real person’s identity is used without consent, from synthetic identity fraud, where a person is fabricated, and from first-party fraud, where the applicant is real and the deceit is their own. Each needs different evidence and a different response. 3.5.1  Identity theft and synthetic identity fraud Identity theft is the use of a real person’s identifying information, such as name, date of birth, address and identification numbers, to obtain credit, open accounts or take over services in that person’s name. There is a real victim whose credit file, statements or documents show the damage, and the victim will usually say “that was not me” once they find out. Synthetic identity fraud is different. The Federal Reserve-convened focus group on the subject described it as using a combination of personally identifiable information to fabricate a person or entity, in order to commit a dishonest act for personal or financial gain. The identity can be a blend of real and invented elements. No single person is the obvious victim, and the real owner of a borrowed number may not learn of the misuse for years. It has two forms that call for different responses. In third-party application fraud, someone applies using another person’s identity or a fabricated one. In first-party application fraud, the real applicant lies about their own circumstances. Cifas calls the second category false application, meaning false information or omitted details given by a genuine party. Third-party application fraud follows a clear sequence from the institution’s side. The fraudster applies, often online. The weakness is any check that tests whether data is consistent but not whether the applicant exists. Delivery of the card or credentials goes to an address or device the fraudster controls. The product is then used quickly, because the fraudster knows the lender will notice. The institution sees the loss only when the account goes into arrears with no payment ever made, or when the real owner learns of the account. 67 The warning signs cluster around the application itself. Contact details that are new or that appear on many applications. A delivery address that differs from the stated home address, or is a mail-forwarding point. Identification data that is internally inconsistent, for example an age that does not fit the credit history. Applications submitted at unusual speed, with data pasted rather than typed. Devices that have applied before under different names. An applicant who refuses or fails a live check. No single sign is proof, and the analyst should look for several that agree. 3.5.3  Bust-out and loan stacking A bust-out is a pattern in which an account or a group of accounts is built up to look reliable and then used to the limit with no intention to repay. The early period looks like a good customer: small purchases, on-time payments, requests for limit increases. Balances are pushed up to the limit, often through a short burst of high-value purchases or cash-like transactions, and payments stop. The account then defaults and the borrower cannot be found. Loan stacking is the taking of several loans from different lenders in a short window, before any of the lenders can see the others in the borrower’s credit file. Each lender sees a normal application, and only afterwards is the total debt visible. It may be a deliberate scheme or an act of desperation by a real borrower, and the response differs, but the exposure to the lender is the same. SCENARIO — THE CUSTOMERS WHO SHARED A FLAT Larchmont Bank opens credit card accounts for three new customers within two weeks. They have different names and credit histories of moderate length, but their applications give the same mobile number with one digit changed, and the delivery addresses are two flats in the same building. Over the next four months each account is used lightly and paid on time, and each requests a limit increase, which Larchmont grants. In month five all three accounts reach their limits within three days, mostly through purchases of high-value goods, and the payments stop. Larchmont’s collections team assumes three unrelated customers hit hard times. Question.  What does the pattern suggest, and what should the bank have done earlier? Answer.  The pattern suggests a bust-out, probably across linked identities that may be synthetic. The signs were present from the start: near-identical contact details, nearby delivery addresses and simultaneous applications. The quiet period of on-time payments built credibility so that limit increases would be granted, and the final burst used the limits fully before default. Treating the three accounts as unrelated customers in difficulty is the tempting and wrong reading. The earlier opportunities were at application, where linked-application checks on shared contact data would have flagged the group, and at limit increase, where a policy of caution for thin or new relationships would have capped the exposure. Now, Larchmont should treat the cases as fraud and not collections, review whether other accounts share the same links, and consider a report to the appropriate authority and, where permitted, to fraud-sharing databases. The decision rules for reporting are in Chapter 6.5. 3.5.4  First-party fraud and misrepresentation First-party fraud is the broad family of frauds in which the customer is the one who deceives. The customer is genuine, the identity is theirs, and the intent to cheat is formed before or during the relationship. As Chapter 3.4 noted, there is no single agreed regulatory definition of first-party fraud, so this guide uses it as a working term. The nearest published categories are the Cifas ones: misuse of facility, meaning misuse of an account or product by the genuine holder, and false application. 68 The hardest question is intent. A customer who forgets a small debt, who misunderstands a question or who is ashamed to disclose a difficulty has not necessarily committed fraud. A customer who invents an employer or fabricates payslips has made a false statement. In between lie exaggeration and omission, where the facts may be the same and the intent differs. A fraud analyst should therefore describe what the evidence shows (statements made, documents supplied, what was found when checked) and should avoid reaching a legal conclusion about guilt. 3.5.5  Consortium checks as a concept A consortium is a group of institutions that agree to share fraud-related information under agreed rules. The idea is simple: a fraudster who is caught by one lender will apply to another, and the second lender is better placed if it can see the first lender’s finding. A consortium turns the experience of every member into a check available to all. In the United Kingdom, the National Fraud Database run by Cifas is an example. Members record fraud risks and query the database when they take on a customer. Cifas relies on legitimate interests as its basis for processing, citing fraud prevention, and individuals can ask for access to their record, challenge it through the member that filed it, or escalate to Cifas or the regulator. The standard for filing is demanding: reasonable grounds to believe that fraud or financial crime was committed or attempted, clear, relevant and rigorous evidence, and conduct that meets a defined case type. The sharing rules for the United States and the European Union are in Chapters 6.5 and 7.2. SCENARIO — THE HIT THAT WAS NOT PROOF Merrow Lending screens a new loan application against a fraud-sharing database. The result shows a record filed two years ago by another lender, noting a false application. The applicant, Mr Adebayo, explains that the earlier record related to an application in which his employer’s name was entered wrongly by a broker, and that he has since asked the other lender to review it. Everything else in Mr Adebayo’s application checks out: identity, address, income, device and employer, which Merrow has confirmed by calling the employer’s payroll office. The junior analyst proposes to decline automatically because there is a database hit. Question.  Is an automatic decline the sound decision? Answer.  No. A database hit is evidence that another member held a concern, not proof that the present application is fraudulent. Although the filing standard is demanding, Mr Adebayo has raised a specific, credible challenge, and everything else in this application is verified. Declining automatically would make the database the decision-maker and would risk an unfair outcome. A sound analyst treats the hit as a flag that requires review. Merrow should assess the application on its own evidence, consider asking the filing lender whether the record is under review, apply a cautious approach such as extra verification, and document the reasoning. If Merrow does decline, the reason should be one it can explain and defend, and Mr Adebayo should know how to pursue the challenge with the filing member. 69 WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — Identity theft uses a real person’s identity without consent, while synthetic identity fraud fabricates a person, often by combining real and invented data, and so produces no complaining victim. — Application fraud has a third-party form, using another person’s or a fabricated identity, and a first-party form, in which the genuine applicant lies about their own circumstances. — A bust-out builds credibility and then uses the limits fully with no repayment, and loan stacking exploits the lag before other lenders can see new debt. — First-party fraud is a working term for deceit by the genuine customer, and the hardest question is intent, so reports should state what the evidence shows and not a conclusion about guilt. — A consortium hit is an input to a decision and not a verdict, because filing standards, accuracy and the right to challenge are part of how a shared database stays fair. 70
