Authentication, device and behavioural signals Rules and scores work on the data they are given. Some of the most useful data comes from the act of logging in and using an account: how the customer proved who they were, what device they used, and how they behaved in the session. This chapter explains authentication assurance using the US NIST digital identity guidelines, then covers device intelligence, behavioural analytics, behavioural biometrics, consortium data and the checks that guard identity proofing against deepfakes. 5.5.1  Authentication and assurance Authentication is the process of proving that a person is who they claim to be, usually by something they know, something they have or something they are. Assurance is the confidence the institution can place in the result. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) publishes digital identity guidelines that set out levels of assurance for identity proofing and for authentication. The box below outlines their principles. Principle What the guidelines say Graded levels Authentication is graded in levels: a single factor at the lowest level, two factors with a phishing-resistant option at the middle level, and a cryptographic authenticator with a non-exportable key at the highest Biometrics A biometric is acceptable only as one factor alongside a physical authenticator, with a non-biometric alternative, and face biometrics need defences against presentation attacks Fraud signals Signals such as unexpected location or IP address may inform a decision at any level but do not change the level Status Guidelines, not law: a teaching outline of the principles, not a compliance checklist Box: the NIST digital identity guidelines in principle. Two ideas matter for detection. First, phishing resistance. A phishing-resistant authenticator is bound to the genuine website or app, so a criminal who tricks the customer cannot reuse it. Codes sent by text or read from an app can be handed to a fraudster on a call, which is why account takeover can survive the use of two factors. Authorised scams are a different problem, because there the customer is the one authenticating. Second, fraud signals inform decisions but do not raise the assurance level. A trusted device does not turn a weak authenticator into a strong one. It is one input to the risk decision, alongside the others in this chapter. 5.5.2  Device intelligence Device intelligence means collecting and assessing information about the device a customer uses. This is practitioner method. A device can be given an identifier, built from attributes such as the operating system, browser or app version, screen settings, language and installed components, and the institution can then ask whether it has seen this device before and what it has done. Useful questions include: is the device new for this customer, how many different customers have used it, is it running in an emulator or a virtual machine, is remote-access software active during the session, has the device been tied to earlier fraud, and has the customer’s phone number recently moved to a new SIM? A device that is new for one customer is common, because people buy phones. A device that has opened or logged into many unrelated accounts is not. 115 5.5.3  Behavioural analytics and behavioural biometrics Behavioural analytics looks at how a customer uses the channel. It asks whether this session looks like this customer’s normal sessions. Features can include the time of day, the usual path through the app, how quickly forms are completed, whether the customer pastes details instead of typing them, and how the session compares with the customer’s own history and with other customers. Behavioural biometrics goes finer. It measures physical interaction patterns: typing rhythm, mouse movement, how a phone is held and swiped. Vendors describe these patterns as hard to copy and as collected passively, but this guide has no independent source to confirm either claim. Some firms use them to indicate that a different person is operating a legitimate customer’s account, and some use them as indicators of a scam in progress, such as unusual hesitation or switching between the app and a phone call, although how well that works is not established here. These signals have limits. Behaviour varies for honest reasons: a customer with an injured hand, a new phone, a tired evening, a shared tablet. Behavioural signals are therefore used to raise or lower friction, for instance with a warning or a call, and not as proof of wrongdoing. This guide gives no official position on behavioural biometrics, and treats them as one input among several. SCENARIO — THE PAYMENT MADE ON THE PHONE Anya Petrova, a long-standing customer of Lindenfield Bank, logs into her app from her usual phone with correct credentials. Over twelve minutes she adds a new payee and sets up a large payment. The session shows long pauses, repeated switching between the app and another application, and slow, careful typing of the payee’s details. Her usual sessions last under two minutes. The payment is not out of range for her account, and the payee does not match any known mule or fraud list. A rule based on device and credentials passes it as low risk. Question.  Which response is sound: pass the payment as low risk, block it, or add friction in proportion to the signal? Answer.  Add friction in proportion to the signal. The session behaviour is an illustrative signal of a possible authorised push payment scam, even though device and credentials are genuine. Anya is the person at the keyboard, so authentication cannot help, and the session is the only clue. A long session, switching to another application (possibly a call) and careful dictation-like typing are the kind of signs some firms treat as guidance by a third party. Because the signal is weak and its reliability is not established here, the response is an informative warning screen about the scam pattern, a short delay, or a call from the bank before release. There are two errors. The tempting one is to pass the payment because the device and login are good. The other is to block it outright: behavioural signals indicate risk without proving it, and the payment may be entirely hers. The action ladder from Chapter 5.1 fits: add friction that gives a genuine customer room to think and the bank a chance to ask questions, while letting a payment that is truly hers go ahead. 5.5.4  Consortium and shared data Consortium data means information about fraud that institutions pool, so that a fraudster identified by one is recognised by others. Shared signals can include reported mule accounts, compromised identities, device identifiers linked to fraud and confirmed fraud addresses. Arrangements differ widely by country and sector: some are run by industry bodies, some by commercial providers and some by law. Once a mule account reported by one bank is on a shared list, a second bank that sees the same account details at an application can refuse it or look closer. A shared record that is wrong, or not removed when it should be, can harm an innocent person by blocking their access to accounts. A sound filing standard, such as that of the UK Cifas National Fraud Database, requires reasonable grounds to believe fraud was committed or attempted, clear 116 and relevant evidence, and a way for individuals to challenge a record. Data protection law governs what can be shared (Chapter 6.5 and Module 7). A shared-data hit is a lead that needs verification against the institution’s own standard. 5.5.5  Identity proofing and deepfakes Identity proofing is the check made when a new customer is onboarded: is this a real person, and are they the person they claim to be? Customers are often proofed remotely, with a photo of an identity document and a selfie or a live video check. Generative artificial intelligence makes it easier to produce convincing fake faces, voices and documents. The box below sets out the principles of the official FinCEN alert on deepfake media, and Module 7 covers the regulatory frame. Item Principle Red flags Altered or inconsistent photos and identity documents; a photo found in galleries of AI-generated faces; flagging by deepfake detection software; a webcam plug-in or switching channels during a live check; refusal of multi-factor authentication or claims of technical glitches; new accounts with rapid payments to higher-risk payees; coordinated activity across similar accounts Mitigations Phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication; live audio or video verification; reverse-image search; metadata review; deepfake detection software; enhanced due diligence Caution No single red flag proves illicit activity; flags are read in context Reporting Filers are asked to mark suspicious activity reports on such schemes so that they can be found Box: the FinCEN alert on deepfake media in principle. It is guidance and carries the limits stated in the text. An injection attack is one in which fake or altered media is fed directly into the verification process, for instance through a virtual camera, instead of being held up to a real camera. A presentation attack is where something false, such as a mask or a screen, is shown to the camera. Controls against both are layered: live checks, detection software, document and metadata analysis, and monitoring of what the new account does next. The last layer is the most useful for fraud analysts, because a synthetic or stolen identity must eventually be used, and rapid movement of funds to higher-risk payees is a behavioural signal that a deepfake does not hide. SCENARIO — THE CAMERA THAT WOULD NOT COOPERATE Orchard Digital, an online lender, runs live video checks for new applicants. An applicant, “Lukas Brandt”, is asked to turn his head, but the video lags and freezes. He says his camera is faulty and asks to switch to a different application on his phone for the call. The identity document photo looks slightly smoother than usual around the edges, and his phone number and email were created recently. A supervisor has reminded the team that approvals are slow this week and applicants are dropping out, and the agent wonders whether to approve because the document data matches the application form. Question.  Which action is sound: approve because the document data matches, accuse the applicant of fraud, or decline to proceed on this session and use another verification route? Answer.  The third. The agent should treat this as a cluster of signals consistent with a possible injection or deepfake attempt, and should not approve on document data alone. Document data matching the form means little, because the information on a synthetic or stolen identity is internally consistent by design. The red flags are the freeze when asked to move, the request to change channel, the unusual photo quality and the recently created contact details. The FinCEN alert lists switching channels and technical glitches during live checks among its red flags. 117 Accusation is also wrong: no single flag proves fraud, as the alert itself cautions. The sound step is not to accuse the applicant but to decline to proceed on this session and require another verification route, such as a fresh live session in the lender’s own channel, further document checks or a higher-assurance method. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — Authentication proves identity, assurance is the confidence in the result, and the NIST digital identity guidelines grade authentication into levels of assurance. — Phishing-resistant authenticators resist being handed over to a criminal, which is why codes alone do not stop account takeover, while authorised scams are a different problem because the customer is the one authenticating. — Fraud signals such as location or device inform the risk decision but do not raise the authentication assurance level. — Device intelligence asks whether a device is new, shared, emulated or tied to earlier fraud, and it is one input and not a verdict. — Behavioural signals are used by some firms to suggest a different person at the keyboard or a customer being guided, their reliability is not established here, and they justify friction and not blocking on their own. — Consortium data finds criminals who move between institutions, but a shared hit must be verified and the sharing rules are in Chapter 6.5 and Module 7. — The FinCEN deepfake alert lists red flags such as webcam plug-ins and channel switching, and warns that red flags need context. 118
