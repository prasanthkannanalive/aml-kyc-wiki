She starts with fundamentals: what counts as fraud (Chapter 1.1), why people commit it (Chapter 1.2) and a shared language of loss (Chapter 1.5). She sets governance (Chapter 2.1), ownership across the three lines (Chapter 2.5) and a fraud risk assessment by scheme, product and channel (Chapter 2.2). Then she builds a loss map in the format of Chapter 7.5: for each scheme and jurisdiction, where does the loss land? UK authorised scams are reimbursed by the sending bank, with a share claimable from the receiving bank (Chapter 7.1). Euro-area unauthorised payments are refunded fast, with recovery only on proof (Chapter 7.2). US debit and card fraud follow Regulation E and Regulation Z, and authorised scams are generally outside them (Chapter 7.3). Ashgrove is large, so it falls within the failure-to-prevent offence (Chapter 2.6). The map goes into a source log with a review date for each row, and anything proposed is a watch item and not a design input. 7.7.2 Controls along the customer journey Layered preventive and detective controls (Chapter 2.3) are placed by stage. Beth walks the customer journey and asks, for each stage, which scheme is most likely there, which control sits there, and which rule decides who pays if the control fails. Ashgrove Bank sells a current account in the UK. On a Tuesday morning, a customer, Nadia Rahman, a woman in her seventies who lives alone and was bereaved a few months earlier, receives a call on her mobile. The caller says he is from Ashgrove’s fraud team. The number on her screen matches the one printed on her card. He tells her that criminals are about to empty her account and that she must move her money to a “safe account” while he secures it. He stays on the line for the next hour. Nadia opens her banking app, adds a new payee at Delmar Credit, another fictional bank, and sends a large payment. Ashgrove’s app shows a generic warning headed “Be careful who you pay”. The caller has told her to ignore it, and she taps through, then sends two further large payments to the same payee within forty minutes using Faster Payments. What did Ashgrove see? The payee was new, the three payments were large for the account, and the app session showed the account being used while a phone call was in progress. One alert went to a general queue reviewed at the end of the day. By then the money had left Delmar Credit. Nadia reports the next morning, after a neighbour tells her she may have been scammed. The call handler records a suspected authorised push payment scam and notes that Nadia is recently bereaved and lives alone. The claim is in scope: individual customer, covered system, covered receiving bank, no sign of first-party fraud. Colm Reeve, a fraud operations manager, wants to refuse the claim because Nadia ignored a warning. A colleague points out that the bank carries the burden of proving gross negligence, that the standard is a significant degree of carelessness, and that the exceptions do not apply to a vulnerable customer. Ashgrove reimburses Nadia in full within the decision period, applies no excess because she appears vulnerable, and claims the receiving bank’s share from Delmar Credit. Delmar Credit questions its share, citing the ignored warning. Ashgrove replies that the split is part of the scheme. Delmar Credit then reviews its own account and finds it was opened only weeks earlier on doubtful documents, with money arriving from several people and quick onward transfers after each. A mule review begins there (Chapter 3.3), and Ashgrove shares what it knows through proper channels (Chapter 6.5). 162 Ashgrove’s own review matters more than the claim. In every similar case it studies, the customer had a new payee, a high-value payment and an active phone call, and the generic warning was dismissed. A blanket extra step on all new-payee payments would catch these cases but would also touch thousands of ordinary payments, so the team must decide how wide the new control should be. Whatever the width, the bank makes three changes: warnings now name the specific scam and say the bank will never ask a customer to move money to a safe account; alerts of this type go to a priority queue with a time limit measured in minutes; and its source log records the scheme rules with review dates. The cost, in plain terms, was the reimbursement, staff time, the dispute with Delmar Credit and a distressed customer. It would have been larger had Ashgrove wrongly refused a claim the scheme required it to pay. The lessons: the scam was ordinary, and the decisive controls were speed and conversation, not technology; the classification and the vulnerability note decided the legal outcome; the receiving bank was both a victim of the mule and a cost-sharing partner; and a case review turned one loss into a targeted control. SCENARIO — TARGETED OR BLANKET After the case, Colm Reeve presents two options to Beth Marlow. Option one is a mandatory confirmation call for every new-payee payment above a low value. Option two holds a payment and triggers a call only where a new payee, a high-value payment and an active phone call coincide, with the warning rewritten to name the scam. All reviewed cases shared the three signals, and the contact centre has limited outbound capacity, so Colm warns that option one would delay ordinary payments and flood it. Question. Which option fits the evidence, and what else should Beth require? Answer. Option two. The harm is concentrated where three signals meet, and Chapter 5.1 and Chapter 5.4 favour targeted rules that catch the pattern without overwhelming staff and customers. Option one adds friction and false positives for little extra protection, and a flooded contact centre would slow the very calls that matter. Beth should require a time limit for the priority queue, a measure of how many held payments were true scams, a review date, a customer-facing explanation of why a payment was held, and monitoring for fraudsters who adapt. Calls must use a number held in the bank’s records, since the scam imitates the bank. The tempting error is to pick the broader option because it feels safer. Evaluators assess the curriculum against current regulatory expectations, the assessment design against whether it tests application or recall, and the issuer’s public claims against what the programme actually delivers. Accreditation is periodic rather than permanent. A programme accredited today is reassessed on a defined cycle, and material change brings the review forward: a new module, a revised assessment, or a substantive shift in the regulatory landscape the programme covers. That last point is why a programme has to be kept up to date after it is accredited. An accredited programme that has not been updated since its accreditation is not, in any sense that matters, still current. Blockchain verification accredited credentials are secured on a blockchain, which provides tamper-proof validation and continuous public verification. 174 It is worth being precise about what that does and does not establish, in the spirit of the rest of this guide. It establishes that a credential record has not been altered since issue, and that anybody can confirm this without depending on the issuer being contactable, solvent or willing. It does not establish that the holder is competent, and no verification technology can. What it removes is one specific failure: a credential that cannot be checked, or that can be checked only by asking the party with the strongest interest in the answer.
