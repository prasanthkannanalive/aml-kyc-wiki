Reading the numbers Fraud figures come from very different sources, which count different things in different places over different periods. Read carefully they inform; added together carelessly they mislead. This chapter quotes no figures. It names the kinds of dataset a fraud professional meets, explains what each measures, shows how to read a median against a mean and how to read loss data, and says why the time a scheme runs before it is found matters. Current numbers belong to their publishers: take them from the latest edition. 1.3.1  What each kind of dataset measures Five kinds of source appear again and again. The ACFE publishes a periodic study, Occupational Fraud: A Report to the Nations, built from a survey of fraud investigators about cases they handled. It describes fraud by staff, managers and owners against their own employer, in every industry, and is the main source on how insider schemes run, how they are found and which controls are linked to lower losses. Consumer-protection bodies, such as the United States Federal Trade Commission through its Consumer Sentinel network, collect reports from people who say they were defrauded. Law-enforcement complaint centres, such as the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3), record complaints about internet-enabled crime, including crime against businesses. Industry bodies, such as UK Finance, publish confirmed fraud cases and losses reported by member banks and payment firms. Regulators and central banks, such as the European Banking Authority and the European Central Bank in a joint report, publish payment fraud data collected from payment service providers. Each source answers a different question: ACFE data suits insider risk and detection methods, consumer and complaint datasets suit scam exposure, industry and regulator data suit benchmarking of payment fraud, and the firm’s own data suits any question about the firm. 1.3.2  Mean, median and datasets that cannot be added Loss data is skewed: many modest cases and a few very large ones. In a skewed set the mean (the total divided by the number of cases) is dragged upward by the largest cases and can be many times the median (the middle case when all are ranked). A typical case is far smaller than the average case. The median describes a typical case and is stable. The mean multiplies back to a total, but is sensitive to a handful of outliers. A firm has nine fraud cases of GBP 10,000 each and one of GBP 910,000: the median is GBP 10,000 and the mean is GBP 100,000. A board told only the mean would plan for the wrong kind of event, and a board told only the median would miss the case that matters. Good reporting gives both, with the count. Datasets also cannot be added together. They use different currencies, periods and definitions, and they overlap: a consumer scam loss may appear in a consumer report, a police complaint and a bank’s confirmed cases, all at once. Adding the totals counts the same loss several times. An occupational fraud study is likewise not a bank loss rate. It counts fraud against employers in all industries, and any “percentage of revenue lost to fraud” quoted from it comes from practitioners’ opinion, not from measurement. 20 SCENARIO — THE BOARD SLIDE A strategy analyst at Larchmont Bank prepares a slide for the board. It takes the latest annual loss total from a US consumer-protection body, a US complaint centre, a UK industry body and a European regulator, converts them to one currency, adds them up, and states that “global payment fraud is the sum of these”. The slide then applies an occupational-fraud study’s “percentage of revenue lost” estimate to Larchmont’s own revenue and presents the result as “our expected annual fraud loss”. The chief risk officer asks whether the slide can be used. Question.  What is wrong with the slide? Answer.  There are two separate errors. The totals measure different things over different periods, come from self-reported complaints, voluntary reports and confirmed bank cases, and overlap with each other, so their sum is neither a global figure nor any figure at all. The occupational-fraud estimate is a practitioner opinion about insider fraud in all industries, not a measured rate for external fraud against a bank, so it cannot give Larchmont’s expected loss. A sound slide presents each dataset separately with its source, period, unit and scope, says what each cannot show, and builds Larchmont’s own exposure from its internal loss data, volumes and typology. External totals give context and direction. They are not a substitute for a firm’s own numbers. 1.3.3  Self-reported and institutional data Self-reported data comes from victims. It captures losses that never reach a bank, but it is voluntary, unverified and shaped by who chooses to report. Institutional data comes from firms and has been confirmed by investigation, but it covers only what the firm saw, classified by the firm’s own definitions and often reduced by recoveries or reimbursement. Neither is a census. Each sees a different slice of the same events, so a gap between the two does not mean either is wrong. Figures are also recorded at different moments and on different bases. The same event can appear at a different size depending on whether the figure is gross (the full amount lost) or net (after recoveries and similar offsets, defined in Chapter 1.5). Consider an invented GBP 5,000 scam. The victim reports the full amount to a consumer survey and to a police complaint centre. The bank confirms the case two weeks later, recovers GBP 1,000 from the receiving bank and reimburses the rest. In the consumer dataset the loss is GBP 5,000; in the bank’s gross loss it is GBP 5,000; in its net loss it is GBP 4,000; and in a regulator’s return it may be counted once, in the period the case was confirmed. Four sources, one event, and no common total. 1.3.4  How to read loss data, and why detection time matters Direction of change needs care. A rise in reported fraud may reflect more crime, better reporting, or a change in definition. When cases rise while total value falls, the average loss per case has gone down, and a headline about either one alone is incomplete. Recent periods are also usually incomplete, because victims report late and cases take time to confirm. Every headline can be tested with the same short list of questions: — Who counted it, and was it self-reported or confirmed? — What was counted: attempts, confirmed cases, reported losses, or net losses after recovery? — What is the population and the period? — Is it a total, a median, a mean or a rate, and does a rate have a matching denominator? — Is it an estimate from opinion, or a measurement from records? 21 A vendor brochure that says “card fraud fell by a fifth” gives a change in a total, counted by the vendor from its own customers, with no denominator, so spend may simply have fallen. It supports no decision until each question has an answer. One finding in occupational fraud research is stable across editions and can be stated in words: the longer a scheme runs before it is found, the more it costs, because each month adds transactions, concealment and money moved. The ACFE also reports that tips are the most common single way schemes are found, and that proactive detection, such as data monitoring and surprise audits, finds fraud faster than waiting for it to surface. Speed of detection is therefore a measure of the control environment, not only a convenience. SCENARIO — TWO HEADLINES, ONE YEAR A newspaper reports that “fraud cases at UK banks jumped last year”, citing an industry fraud report. A colleague at Delmar Credit, quoting the same report, says “unauthorised fraud losses fell”. A third colleague says one of them must have misread the report. Neither has looked at the authorised push payment (APP) fraud section, where a victim is tricked into authorising the payment, and the newspaper did not say whether it meant cases or value. A product manager adds that the industry loss “must have gone down, since the bank’s own card losses did”. Question.  Can both statements be correct? Answer.  Yes. Cases and value are different measures, and a rise in many small cases with a fall in value is quite possible. Each is right about what they measured, but neither describes the whole. If APP losses rose while unauthorised losses fell, the total depends on their relative sizes, and “fraud losses fell” would be true only of the unauthorised part. The product manager’s inference from the bank’s own card losses is not evidence about the industry total. A sound reader names the measure each time and checks the split between unauthorised and APP fraud before drawing a view. A reader who says only “fraud fell” or “fraud rose” has not yet said which measure moved. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — Fraud datasets differ in source, scope, period and unit, overlap each other and cannot be added together. — Self-reported data captures what victims say they lost; institutional data captures what a firm has confirmed; neither is a census. — Loss data is skewed, so the mean sits far above the median and good reporting gives both with the count. — An occupational fraud study, or its opinion-based share-of-revenue estimate, is not a bank loss rate. — The longer a scheme runs before detection, the more it costs, and tips are the most common single way occupational fraud is found. — Before trusting a headline figure, ask who counted it, what was counted, over what period, and whether it is a total, an average or a rate. 22
