# Insider fraud in financial institutions

A bank or payments firm gives its staff something most employers do not: legitimate access to other people’s money and identities. The institution can suffer the usual schemes against an employer, and its staff can also be the tool through which customers and the wider financial system are defrauded. This chapter covers the insider risks that are particular to financial institutions, in branches, call centres and operations, in systems with privileged access, and in collusion with outsiders. It then looks at the signals in access logs and behaviour that give insiders away, and ends with a worked case.


**4.3.1** Why insiders in financial institutions are different In most businesses, an insider who commits fraud steals from the employer. In a financial institution there are three further kinds of harm. The customer may lose money from an account. The institution may become liable for a customer’s loss. And the institution may be used, knowingly or not, to move criminal funds for others, which makes insider fraud a money-laundering and regulatory matter as well as a loss event. Two features make financial institutions especially exposed. The first is delegated trust. Customers give staff their identity documents, their account details and sometimes their security answers, and a teller, agent or operations analyst can do things no outsider could, with the institution’s authority. The second is that outside criminals know this. Organised groups approach, recruit or coerce staff, because a single insider who can change a phone number on an account, or release a payment, is worth more than many external attempts. Chapters 3.2 and 3.3 describe what outsiders do. This chapter describes the insider who helps them, willingly or under pressure. Insider threats fall into rough groups, and the distinction guides the response. The malicious insider acts for personal gain or out of grievance. The recruited or coerced insider acts for an outside party, perhaps with an initial favour that grows into a dependence. The negligent insider causes loss by carelessness, for instance by sharing a password, ignoring a procedure or falling for a phishing call. The compromised insider is one whose credentials are stolen and used by someone else, so that the activity is not theirs at all. The log shows what happened, and the investigation establishes who did it and why.


**4.3.2** Call-centre and operations staff, and data theft Call-centre agents and operations staff sit between the customer and the system. They are trusted to verify identity, reset credentials, change details and release payments, and they work to targets under pressure. A recruited call-centre agent can help a fraudster pass identity checks, reveal verification answers, reset a password or add a payee, all of which look like normal service in the logs. Because the act is a normal action by an authorised person, it is the pattern, not the individual step, that gives the game away: one agent handling a disproportionate number of calls that end in credential resets, or an analyst whose overrides concentrate on the same few beneficiaries. Data theft is a second major insider risk. Customer records are valuable: names, addresses, dates of birth, account numbers and security answers allow impersonation of customers on a large scale. An employee may remove or disclose customer records without authority, including by passing lookups to an outsider who pays for them.


## Scenario — TOO MANY LOOKUPS

At Larchmont Bank’s contact centre, the weekly access analytics report shows that Danielle Moreau, an agent on the cards team, opened roughly four times as many customer profiles as the average agent in her team over the past month. Most of these profiles belong to high-value customers. Her call log shows far fewer completed calls than her profile views would suggest, and about a third of the profiles she viewed have no call recorded against them. Agents on her team are entitled to open any customer profile, so nothing was blocked. **Question.** What should the fraud and security team conclude, and what should they do? **Answer.** They should conclude that the pattern is unexplained and needs investigation, not that Danielle is guilty.

Profile views that do not match calls are a classic sign of browsing for a purpose other than service, which could be curiosity, data theft for sale, or lookups made for an outsider. The concentration on high-balance customers raises the concern from privacy breach to possible fraud preparation. A sound practitioner preserves the access logs, widens the check to see whether any of the viewed accounts has since had a change of contact details, new payee or takeover attempt, and escalates under the confidential insider-investigation process involving HR and legal. The practitioner does not question Danielle first, and does not rely on her supervisor’s opinion as an alibi.


**4.3.3** Privileged access and collusion with outsiders Privileged access means system rights above those of an ordinary user: the ability to change configurations, alter data, create accounts, bypass controls or see everything. System administrators, database administrators, developers with production access, and staff who administer payment systems all hold it. Abuse of privileged access is serious because the same rights that run the system can hide the abuse. An administrator may be able to change a record, and the log of the change. The controls are narrower access and stronger oversight. Privileged rights should be granted for a defined need and time, reviewed regularly, and removed when the need ends. Administrator accounts should be individual and named, with strong authentication, and use should be logged to a store that the administrators themselves cannot change. Sensitive actions should require two people. Production changes should be separate from development, with an independent approver. Collusion with outsiders deserves its own emphasis. In the largest schemes insiders often have accomplices, inside or outside. An outsider supplies the fake identities, the mule accounts, the victims and the cash-out route; the insider supplies access, approval or information. The ACFE reports that cases with several perpetrators are larger. Detection therefore depends on seeing links: staff who repeatedly handle the same outsider’s accounts, accounts opened in a branch by the same introducer, or approvals that always come from the same pair of colleagues.

**4.3.5** Case study: The overnight reset Composite case, fictional. Corvane Bank is a mid-sized retail bank with a contact centre of about two hundred agents in account services, cards and collections teams. Its fraud team reviews alerts and customer reports, its information security team holds the access logs, and a small confidential investigations unit sits under the head of compliance. This is how a recruited insider operated for several months and how the bank caught the scheme. A group of outsiders approached Marcus Teague, an agent on the account services team, who was in debt. They offered a small payment to confirm a customer’s address “for a delivery”, then larger payments for more. Within weeks he was resetting online banking credentials and adding mobile numbers on accounts when callers failed the identity questions. Each action was a routine service task. Each call was recorded, but the quality team sampled only calls that had ended in a complaint, so no one reviewed calls that ended in success. Over the following months the outsiders emptied several dozen accounts, mostly belonging to older customers with idle balances. A caller who failed the identity questions was put through to Marcus’s queue, and he reset the credentials and registered a new mobile number. Within a day the new number received the security codes, payees were added, and the balance was sent to newly opened accounts and moved on within hours, as Chapter 3.3 describes. From the bank’s side each payment looked like the customer’s own. The customers experienced a sudden and bewildering loss, and some were told, kindly but wrongly, that the payments must have followed a phishing message. The bank refunded under its rules and recorded each case as account takeover. Because the cases reached the fraud team through different channels, no one linked them. Three signals existed that the bank missed. First, Marcus’s credential-reset volume was well above that of his peers, and most of his resets followed failed identity checks that were then 90 overridden. An override report existed, but nobody owned it. Second, he was active during quiet night shifts when he was not rostered, using a colleague’s terminal. The shift system and the access logs were held by different teams and never compared. Third, a line manager had noticed that he had bought an expensive car and asked repeatedly for evening shifts, but felt that mentioning it without proof would be unfair, and there was no route to the fraud team that did not require proof. The scheme came to light when a fraud analyst, Hannah Brook, building a report for another purpose, joined the account-change history to the agents who had performed each change. For the compromised accounts, one agent’s name appeared far more often than chance would explain. She did not approach him. She preserved the logs and call recordings, passed the findings to the confidential investigations unit, and asked information security to restrict his access discreetly. Interviews followed under the bank’s procedures with HR and legal advisers present. Marcus left the bank, the matter was referred to law enforcement, and the bank reviewed every reset and contact-detail change he had made, contacted the customers concerned and placed protective checks on accounts where he had made changes that no customer could confirm. Decisions on legal action and reporting belong to Module 6. The cost was not only the refunded losses. The bank paid for the investigation, the review of every reset and the strengthened controls, and it lost the trust of older customers, some of whom moved their savings elsewhere. The lessons are clear. A control that reviews only failures misses the successful abuse of a routine action. Overrides of failed identity checks should be reported, reviewed and capped. Credential resets and contact-detail changes should need stronger verification and, for high-risk accounts, a second approver or a cooling-off period. Access analytics should join the agent to every account change as a standing report. Managers’ observations need a safe route to the fraud team that does not depend on proof. And case management should record who performed each action, not only who the victim was.


## Scenario — THE MANAGER’S OBSERVATION

After a briefing on the Corvane case, a team leader in the cards team, Joanna Pike, tells the fraud team that an agent in her team, Ismail, has bought an expensive car and has asked to move to evening shifts. She has no other concern: his calls are well handled, his customers are satisfied and she has seen nothing improper. She asks whether she should say anything to Ismail. The head of the contact centre says it is nothing without proof. A colleague in security says Ismail should be put under full investigation and his access restricted at once. **Question.** How should the fraud team handle the observation? **Answer.** Neither extreme is sound.

The observation is a behavioural signal, not evidence, and the lesson of the case is that such observations need a safe route to the fraud team that does not depend on proof. A car and a request for evening shifts have innocent explanations, so a full investigation and restricted access would be disproportionate and unfair. Dismissing it would repeat the Corvane error, where the same two observations were never passed on. The sound step is to log the observation, thank Joanna, and run a low-cost, confidential check of Ismail’s activity in the standing report against his team’s peers: resets, overrides of failed identity checks, contact-detail changes and out-of-hours access. If the figures sit within the norm, the matter closes with no action and no one else need know.

If they stand out, it moves to the confidential investigation function. Joanna should not raise it with Ismail or discuss it with the team, because that would warn a possible wrongdoer or embarrass an innocent person.


## What to take from this chapter
- Insider fraud in a financial institution can harm the customer, create liability for the institution and make the institution a channel for laundering, as well as taking money from the employer
- The main insider groups are the malicious, recruited or coerced, negligent and compromised insider, and each calls for a different response
- Call-centre and operations staff can help takeover or release payments through routine-looking actions, and data theft gives outsiders the means to commit many frauds
- Privileged access should be time-limited, named, logged to a store the user cannot change, and subject to two-person approval for sensitive actions
- The strongest insider signal is a joined-up one, such as a lookup followed by a contact-detail change followed by a payment, found by linking access logs to transaction records
- When an insider is suspected, preserve evidence and escalate confidentially before any conversation with the person.
