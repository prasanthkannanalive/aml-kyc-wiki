# From alert to case

An alert is only a signal. Most alerts are not fraud, and a few are urgent. The skill at this stage is sorting quickly without sorting carelessly: stopping money that can still be stopped, opening a case for matters that need real work, and closing the rest with a reason that someone else could read and agree with. This chapter covers the vocabulary, the first questions, how a case is opened and owned, and how to set the starting point. The method is practitioner method.


**6.1.1** Alerts, referrals, cases and dispositions Four words carry most of the weight in investigation work. An alert is a signal produced by a rule, a model or a control, such as a payment that tripped a threshold or a login from a new device. A referral is a concern raised by a person: a branch colleague, a call-centre agent, a customer, a tip through a hotline. A case is a record opened to investigate a matter that needs more than a quick look. A disposition is the recorded outcome of reviewing an alert or closing a case. Alerts and referrals are inputs. Not every input becomes a case. In a common design, an analyst first reviews the alert against the customer record and decides in minutes whether it is explained; a case is opened only when the matter cannot be explained, involves a loss or likely loss, or touches a person or pattern that needs a proper file. Dispositions should be a short, fixed list so that results can be counted: explained and closed as a false positive; confirmed fraud or attempted fraud; suspicious but not confirmed, with a reason; and referred to another team. Free-text notes add detail, but the fixed value lets the programme measure itself (Chapter 5.4 covers how the counts feed tuning). Record what was decided and why, in words a stranger could follow: an alert closed with “OK” protects no one if the decision is later questioned.


**6.1.2** Triage: the first questions Triage means sorting by urgency and importance. A good analyst asks the same questions every time, in the same order, because the answers decide the next hour. First, is money still moving or about to move? If a payment is pending, a card is still active or an account is still accessible to the wrong person, the clock is running. Second, can the loss still be stopped, recalled or held? Third, who is the likely victim: the customer, the institution, a third party, or a staff member? Fourth, are there signs that the customer is vulnerable, such as confusion or distress (age alone is not one; Chapter 6.3 explains)? Fifth, does the matter involve a colleague, a manager or a supplier, which changes who may see the file. Sixth, is there a pattern, meaning other accounts, other customers, or the same device or beneficiary across several alerts? Funds in motion, recoverability, the victim, any insider involvement, a pattern, and any legal or regulatory clock all raise priority; their opposites lower it. Levels and time targets are a practitioner convention, set by each institution.


**6.1.3** Opening and owning a case When triage says a matter needs work, a case is opened. A case record is the single place where everything about the matter lives: the trigger, the people and accounts involved, the evidence gathered, the decisions made, the contacts with the customer and the outcome. A sound case record has fixed parts. It names an owner, the person accountable for moving the case forward. It states the case type from a fixed list, such as the fraud types in Module 3, so that cases can be counted. It records the date and time of detection and of each later step, because deadlines inside and outside the institution run from those moments. And it links related cases, so that the fifth victim of one beneficiary account is not treated as a first. Ownership needs two safeguards. The first is conflict: an analyst must not work a case about a close colleague, a relative or a customer they know personally, and the system should make hand-over easy. The second is access: cases about insiders, or cases that reach senior people, should be restricted to those who need them. The ACFE Code of Professional Standards, which this guide uses as a reference for good practice, asks examiners to keep information confidential unless disclosure is authorised, required by statute or ordered by a court (III.F.1).


## Scenario — THE ALERT THAT LOOKED SMALL

At Larchmont Bank, first-line analyst Tomasz Reid reviews a queue of alerts. One is a new payee added to a retail account followed by a modest transfer, still marked pending. The customer, Mrs Adaeze Okafor, called the branch twice last week confused about a text message, and a note on her profile records both calls. The system ranks the alert low because of the amount. Tomasz notices that the same payee was added to two other accounts earlier that morning. Another analyst suggests clearing the queue in amount order and returning to the pattern after lunch. **Question.** How should Tomasz triage the alert? **Answer.** He should treat it as urgent.

A pending payment means money may still be stoppable, the profile note suggests the customer may be vulnerable, and the same beneficiary on three accounts is a pattern. Funds in motion, recoverability, victim and pattern all raise priority; only the amount points the other way. The sound steps are to hold the pending payment under procedure, contact Mrs Okafor through a verified channel, open a case linking the three accounts, and ask whether the payee account can be restricted or reported, noting the time of each step.


**6.1.4** Predication, scope and keeping an open mind Predication means the reasonable basis on which an examination starts: what is known or suspected, from which source, and why it justifies action. Scope is the boundary of the work: which accounts, which period, which people, which questions. The ACFE Code says the starting point and scope should be set at the outset and reassessed as the work proceeds (IV.A.2): write down why you are looking and at what, and revisit both when the evidence changes. Scope should be wide enough to answer the question and no wider. A finding that the customer made the payment, or that a colleague did nothing wrong, is a successful investigation. Look at what the question needs: the login history, the change-of-details events, the payments and the contacts around the incident. If the evidence points elsewhere, widen the scope and record why. Unneeded breadth costs time and creates a data protection problem of its own. The same Code asks investigators to guard against conjecture, unsubstantiated opinion and witness bias, and to weigh evidence that points away from the suspected explanation as well as evidence that points toward it (IV.A.3). Confirming evidence is gathered faster than contradicting evidence. The remedy is to write, for each case, the leading explanation and an alternative, and note what evidence would separate them.


## Scenario — THE THEORY THAT FITTED TOO WELL

Hannah Ellery, an investigator at Delmar Credit, opens a case on a card account after a series of overseas purchases the customer says he did not make. Her first theory is cloned card data, and she begins pulling merchant records. She then sees that each purchase was approved in the banking app from the family tablet, which the customer registered as a trusted device long ago and which his teenage son began using the week before the purchases started. The approvals came at times when the customer says he was at work. She decides the approvals are probably a system quirk and leaves them out of her notes so the file stays tidy. **Question.** What has gone wrong with Hannah’s approach, and what should she do? **Answer.** Hannah has let an early theory decide which facts count.

The app approvals from the registered tablet point away from card cloning, and tablet access is an explanation she has not tested. The Code’s requirement to weigh evidence that cuts against the leading theory (IV.A.3) means the approvals belong in the file, marked as unexplained. Leaving them out also makes the record misleading to the next reader. She should restate the case with two explanations, cloned data and use of a trusted device by someone with access, decide what would separate them (the timing of each approval against the tablet’s device log and who held the tablet), and record the change of scope.


## What to take from this chapter
- An alert is a signal, a referral is a person’s concern, a case is a record opened for real investigation, and a disposition is the recorded outcome
- Triage asks about harm first: is money in motion, can it be stopped, who is the victim, is anyone vulnerable, is an insider involved and is there a pattern
- A case record needs an owner, a type, timestamps, linked cases and enough detail that a colleague can pick it up cold
- Set predication and scope at the start and reassess them as evidence arrives, as the ACFE Code of Professional Standards asks
- Weigh evidence that points away from the leading theory, and record it even when it makes the file untidy.
