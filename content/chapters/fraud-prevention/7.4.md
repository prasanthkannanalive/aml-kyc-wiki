# Other regimes in brief

Singapore, Australia and India each took a different route to the same problem. This chapter gives them lighter coverage than the UK, the EU and the US. For each it teaches the design idea: who bears the loss, what triggers the duty and what kind of rule it is. The details come from regulator pages and secondary summaries, and should be checked against primary texts before anyone relies on them.


**7.4.1** Singapore: the Shared Responsibility Framework The Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) published a Shared Responsibility Framework for a defined phishing scam: impersonation through digital messaging, the victim enters credentials, and the transactions are “seemingly authorised”. Malware-based scams are excluded. Financial institutions must apply a cooling-off period before high-risk activity takes effect, send real-time alerts, provide a reporting channel and run real-time surveillance. Telecommunications operators must filter malicious links and send sender-identified messages only through authorised aggregators. Loss follows a waterfall that tests compliance with duties in order. The financial institution bears the loss if it breached a duty. If it complied, the telecommunications operator bears it if that operator breached a duty. If both complied, the consumer bears it. Loss is allocated by compliance with duties, not by payment type, so responsibility follows control: each party answers for the part of the chain it can fix.


**7.4.2** Australia: the Scams Prevention Framework The Scams Prevention Framework (SPF) is a statutory framework within Australian competition and consumer law. It applies to sectors brought in by designation: banks, telecommunications providers and digital platforms. The Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) regulates banks. Its design is duty-based. Regulated entities must prevent, detect, disrupt, report and respond to scams, with matching governance, and must belong to an external dispute-resolution scheme. The regulator’s page does not state a duty to reimburse victims, so the framework should not be mistaken for a refund scheme: it creates conduct duties, and compliance with them is what regulators examine. Reimbursement ideas have been reported as proposals only. A framework can regulate conduct across a whole chain without yet allocating loss to any link.


**7.4.3** India: customer liability limits and fraud risk directions India’s central bank, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), issued a circular limiting customer liability in unauthorised electronic transactions. Its current status should be checked. The design is a ladder. Where the fault is the bank’s, the customer has no liability whenever the report is made. Where a third party breached the system and the customer reports promptly, there is also none. Where the loss is due to the customer’s own negligence, the customer bears it until the report. A third-party breach reported after a short delay carries a fixed cap depending on account or card type, and after a longer delay the bank’s board-approved policy decides. The circular also expects prompt credit to the customer, resolution within a set period, and puts the burden of proof on the bank. Separate RBI directions on fraud risk management govern the 153 bank’s own conduct, including notice and a chance to reply before a fraud is classified, and contain no customer compensation. A compensation proposal is proposed only. The principle is that liability falls with speed of reporting and with whose fault the loss was.


## Scenario — THE PROMPT REPORT

A customer of an Indian bank, Kavita Rao, holds a standard savings account. A third party obtains her card details through a data breach at a merchant and makes unauthorised transactions. Kavita reports within the first few working days. The bank’s operations lead says the use of a one-time code shows customer negligence and declines to credit. Kavita says she did not share the code and is relying on that credit to pay her rent. **Question.** On the circular as described, what should the bank do? **Answer.** The bank should credit the loss. A third-party breach reported promptly carries no customer liability, and the burden of proving negligence rests on the bank. An unproven suspicion does not meet it.

The bank may investigate how the code was obtained and recover from others later, but should not withhold the credit on an assumption. The circular’s current status should be confirmed before relying on this answer in practice.


## Scenario — WHAT KIND OF REGIME IS IT?

Wen Hao, a risk analyst at a regional bank entering Singapore, Australia and India, drafts a board note: “All three countries now require banks to refund scam victims in full, so we should budget for that cost.” He relied on regulator summary pages and a law-firm newsletter, citing the Scams Prevention Framework, the Shared Responsibility Framework and a draft Indian compensation proposal. He has read no primary legal text, and the note goes to the board next week. **Question.** What is wrong with the draft? **Answer.** It treats three different kinds of rule as one. Singapore allocates loss by compliance with duties for a defined phishing scam. Australia’s framework sets duties and, per the regulator’s page, states no reimbursement duty.

India’s circular limits liability for unauthorised transactions, and the compensation draft is only proposed. The note should say what each regime does, mark proposals as proposals, and say the sources do not support full refunds in all three. The tempting error is to infer a rule from the existence of a framework.


## What to take from this chapter
- Singapore’s framework covers a defined phishing scam, excludes malware, and allocates loss by compliance with duties in order: the financial institution, then the telecommunications operator, then the consumer
- Responsibility follows control: each party in the chain answers for the part it can fix
- Australia’s Scams Prevention Framework imposes duties to prevent, detect, disrupt, report and respond across banks, telecoms and platforms, and the regulator’s page does not state a reimbursement duty
- India’s circular gives no customer liability for bank-side fault or prompt reports of third-party breaches, limits liability for later reports, and puts the burden of proof on the bank
- Draft proposals are not law, and a framework that regulates conduct is not necessarily a refund scheme.
