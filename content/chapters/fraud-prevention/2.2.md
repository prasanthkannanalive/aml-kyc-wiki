Fraud risk assessment A fraud risk assessment is the step that turns a general worry into a list of specific things that could go wrong, ranked, matched to controls and given an owner. The COSO and ACFE guide describes it as dynamic and iterative: it is repeated, not completed once. This chapter gives a practical six-step method built on the guide’s principle, with a worked example, so that an analyst can run an assessment on a product or process and produce a document others can challenge. 2.2.1  What an assessment is for, and the terms it uses The second principle of the guide asks for comprehensive fraud risk assessments that identify specific schemes, assess likelihood and significance, evaluate existing controls and act on residual risk. This guide breaks them into six working steps.  A fraud scheme is a specific way in which a loss could be caused, described from start to finish: who does what, to what, and for what gain. “Payment fraud” is a category. “A customer’s online banking session is taken over and a new payee is added before a large transfer” is a scheme. Assessments fail when they stay at the level of categories, because a category cannot be matched to a control. Inherent risk is the level of risk before any control is considered: if we had nothing in place, how likely is this scheme and how bad would it be? Residual risk is what remains after the controls that actually work are taken into account. Likelihood is how probable it is that the scheme will be tried and succeed in a given period. Impact, which the guide calls significance, is how much harm results, including direct loss, customer detriment, regulatory consequences, reputational damage and the cost of response. A fraud risk register is the document that holds the output: one line per scheme with its ratings, controls, owner and actions.  2.2.2  Steps one and two: identify schemes and rate inherent risk Step one is to identify the schemes. Good sources are varied: past incidents and near misses from case management, including those that were stopped; loss and dispute data, complaints and customer scam reports; internal audit findings, control test results and regulator or industry warnings; and process maps of the product, showing every point where money, data or authority moves. A team assessing a new online loan product might find that dispute data shows first-month defaults, process mapping shows income taken from an uploaded document, and an industry warning describes applications built on made-up identities. From those three it can write distinct schemes: false income on an upload, a made-up identity, and an application by a third party using stolen details. For each scheme, write one sentence of the form: who, doing what, exploiting which weakness, to gain what.  Step two is to rate inherent likelihood and impact, assuming no controls. A four-point scale of low, medium, high and very high is enough, provided each point is described in words that two people would interpret the same way. Rate on evidence where it exists, such as incident counts, and flag where a rating is a judgement. Resist rating everything medium: if every scheme sits in the middle, the register cannot guide any decision. 34 SCENARIO — THE REGISTER FULL OF MEDIUMS Fernlea Payments, a small payments firm, completes its first fraud risk assessment. The register has fourteen lines. Each is described by a category, such as “card fraud”, “account takeover” and “insider fraud”. Every inherent rating is medium for likelihood and medium for impact. Controls are listed as “fraud monitoring system” and “staff training” against every line. Residual ratings are all low, with the note “controls in place”. There are no owners, no test results and no actions. The head of compliance asks whether the register is ready to present to the board. Question.  Is it ready? Identify the main weaknesses against the method in this chapter. Answer.  It is not ready. The register stops at categories instead of schemes, so no control can be matched to a specific way of losing money. The uniform medium ratings suggest the ratings are not evidence-based and give the board nothing to prioritise. Residual ratings of low with “controls in place” are asserted, not reasoned. No owners or actions mean no risk response. A sound analyst sends it back with specific instructions: break each category into schemes, rate on evidence with written definitions, map and test controls per scheme, give reasons for residual ratings and add owners, responses and dates.  2.2.3  Steps three and four: map controls and rate residual risk Step three is to map the existing controls to each scheme: the preventive and detective controls that act on it, who runs them and what they depend on (Chapter 2.3 explains the control types). A scheme with no control listed against it is a finding in itself. Mapping is not enough. The guide expects the assessment to evaluate whether controls work as designed. Useful tests include sampling cases to see whether the control operated, checking that a rule fires on a known example, reviewing override logs, and asking the people who run the control how it behaves on a busy day. Consider a control meant to hold a payment when the payee name does not match the account. A test asks three things. Does the rule fire on a known mismatch? Does the hold reach a person, and how soon? Does the customer see a message that makes sense? If the rule fires but the hold sits unseen in a queue overnight, the control exists but does not work as designed, and the residual rating has to reflect that. A control that fires correctly and is then ignored is a common finding, and it is invisible in a document review.  Step four is to rate residual risk: judge how far the controls that actually work reduce likelihood, impact or both. Do not count controls that have not been tested as fully effective. And do not let one strong control justify a low rating if it has a single point of failure, such as a rule that relies on one data feed. 2.2.4  Steps five and six: respond and document, with a worked example Step five is to decide the response to residual risk. The usual options are four. Reduce the risk by adding or strengthening controls. Avoid it by changing or withdrawing the product or process. Transfer part of it, for example through insurance or contract terms, remembering that the organisation usually stays answerable for the outcome (Chapter 4.4). Or accept it, which is valid only if it is within appetite and approved by someone with the authority to accept it. Responses should be specific, owned and dated: “improve monitoring” is not a response; “add a rule for new payee followed by a large transfer, owned by the head of payment fraud, in place next quarter” is. Step six is to document: the boundary, the sources, the schemes, the ratings with reasons, the controls and their test results, the residual ratings, the responses with owners and dates, and who 35 approved any acceptance of risk. The assessment is refreshed on a schedule and also when something changes: a new product, channel or partner, a rise in a type of loss, or a new warning.  A worked example uses a fictional retail bank, Tarrant Bank, which has launched instant payments in its app. The scheme: a criminal persuades a customer by telephone that their account is under threat and that they must move money to a safe account, and the customer approves an instant payment to a new payee. Inherent likelihood is very high, because the scheme is common and easy to attempt, and inherent impact is high, because payments are irreversible. Four controls are mapped: a warning screen, a delay and extra questions for new payees on larger amounts, transaction monitoring, and customer education. Testing shows that the warning is often clicked through, the delay applies only above a set amount, and monitoring catches about half of test cases. Residual likelihood is high and residual impact medium, because smaller payments pass untouched. The response is to reduce: tighten the warning and extend the delay logic to customers whose behaviour changes sharply, owned by the head of payments with a target date. The executive committee records that it accepts the remainder for now, within appetite. SCENARIO — THE CONTROL THAT NEVER GOT TESTED Priya Nair, a risk analyst at Delmar Credit, is completing the assessment for the lender’s new online loan product. The scheme under review is synthetic identity applications, meaning applications built on a made-up identity assembled from real and false data. The product team says the risk is low because the application journey includes a document upload and a selfie check. Priya notes that no one has tested how the selfie check performs on poor-quality images, that the vendor supplied the pass rate in a sales document and that the document upload is not compared with any independent data. The product team asks her to enter the residual rating as low so that the launch date is kept. Question.  How should Priya rate residual risk, and what should she record? Answer.  She should not enter low. The controls exist, but the assessment’s job is to evaluate whether they work as designed, and none has been tested. The vendor’s figure is a claim, not a test result, and the document upload has no independent check behind it. On the evidence, the controls cannot be counted as fully effective, so residual risk should stay at the level the evidence supports, which on these facts is high, with a note saying why. A sound practitioner records the reasoning, proposes a response with an owner and date, such as testing the selfie check on realistic images and adding an independent data check, and escalates the request to keep the launch date to someone with authority to accept risk. If the business wants to launch, it can formally accept the residual risk, but that decision belongs to the right person and must be written down. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — The assessment identifies specific schemes, not categories: who does what, exploiting which weakness, to gain what. — Inherent risk is the level with no controls; residual risk is what remains after the controls that actually work. — The six steps are identify schemes, rate inherent likelihood and impact, map and test controls, rate residual risk, decide the response and document. — A control that has not been tested should not be counted as fully effective. — The responses to residual risk are reduce, avoid, transfer or accept, and acceptance must be within appetite and approved by someone with authority. — The assessment is dynamic and iterative: refresh it on a schedule and whenever the product, partner, channel or threat changes. 36
