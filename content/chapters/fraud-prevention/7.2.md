# The EU

EU payment rules rest on the second Payment Services Directive (PSD2), which deals firmly with unauthorised payments and says little about authorised scams. The Instant Payments Regulation adds a name check before many credit transfers. A newer package, a further directive and a Payment Services Regulation, has been proposed to extend protection to some authorised scams. Keep what is in force apart from what is proposed, and ask which of the two any statement describes.


**7.2.1** PSD2: strong customer authentication and liability Strong customer authentication (SCA) means checking identity with two or more independent elements, so that a breach of one does not compromise the others: knowledge (something only the customer knows), possession (something only the customer has) and inherence (something the customer is). PSD2 requires SCA for many electronic payments and, for remote payments, dynamic linking, which ties the authentication to the amount and payee so that a code cannot be reused (Chapter 5.5). SCA is the hinge of the liability model, and the principles can be stated briefly. The PSP bears the burden of proof on authentication and on gross negligence. It must refund an unauthorised payment immediately, withholding it only on a reasonable suspicion of customer fraud reported to the authority. The customer bears a small fixed amount for a lost or stolen instrument, nothing after notifying the PSP, and everything only if acting fraudulently, intentionally or with gross negligence. If the PSP did not require SCA where it should have, the customer bears a loss only if acting fraudulently, and a payee PSP that does not accept SCA compensates the payer’s PSP. The cost of weak authentication falls on the party that chose it. A payment the customer authorised to a fraudster is not an unauthorised payment, so it is not refundable under these rules. A payment to the correct account identifier was historically treated as correctly executed even if the name did not match, which is why authorised scams were left largely with the customer and why the name check below was introduced.


**7.2.2** The Court ruling: refund first, argue later The Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) has ruled on an unauthorised transaction involving a spoofed bank website. As reported from the Court’s own summary, a PSP must refund an unauthorised transaction immediately if the customer reported it without undue delay. An allegation of gross negligence does not justify withholding the refund. Only a reasonable suspicion of fraud by the customer, reported to the authority, does. Afterwards the PSP may recover the money, but only if it proves intent or gross negligence. The ruling does not say gross negligence can never be pleaded, only that it cannot justify delay. It concerns unauthorised transactions, so it creates no right for a customer persuaded to send money.


## Scenario — THE FAKE LOGIN PAGE

Alder Bank, in a euro-area member state, gets a call from Marta Kowal. She entered her login details and a one-time code on a page that looked like the bank’s and then found a transfer she did not make. She calls the same day. An Alder analyst notes that the logs show a correct one-time code, concludes Marta was careless, and wants to pause the refund to investigate her conduct. No one suspects fraud. **Question.** Is pausing the refund consistent with the Court’s ruling as described? **Answer.** No. Marta reported without undue delay and nothing suggests she acted fraudulently. The PSP must refund immediately, and a belief in gross negligence does not justify holding the money back. Only a reasonable suspicion of customer fraud, reported to the authority, would.

Alder should refund promptly. If it later holds evidence of intent or gross negligence, it may seek recovery from Marta, with the burden of proof on Alder. The correct code is evidence for that later question, not a reason to delay now.


**7.2.3** Verification of payee under the Instant Payments Regulation The Instant Payments Regulation introduced the EU’s verification of payee (VoP) duty: before the payment is authorised, the payee name given by the payer is compared with the name held against the account number (IBAN). The detail is from secondary summaries. The check returns match, close match, no match or not possible. A no-match result does not block the payment, and the payer may proceed. A PSP that ran the check and reported it correctly is not liable under the PSD2 identifier rule if the payer proceeds. A PSP that failed to provide a correct result, and so caused a defective payment, must refund the transfer in full without delay. VoP is a nudge, not a wall. It helps against misdirected payments and some impersonation, such as an invoice-redirection fraud where the new account name does not match the supplier (Chapter 3.6). It does not help where the account was opened in the name the victim was told to expect (Chapter 3.3).


**7.2.4** The proposed package A further payment services directive and a Payment Services Regulation have been proposed. This guide treats the package as proposed, not adopted. Check its current stage before relying on it, because the stage moves. The direction of the agreed text, as read from its explanatory preamble and not from operative articles, has three ideas. A victim of spoofing in which the fraudster impersonates the PSP’s staff would receive a full refund unless the payer acted fraudulently or with gross negligence. A PSP that fails to provide a correct VoP result would refund the defective transfer. Prompt refunds and a low user-liability limit would continue for unauthorised payments. Commentary also describes possible platform liability, but the operative wording was not confirmed. A recital explains a law’s purpose but creates no duty. Planning for a proposal is sensible; promising customers a right on its strength is not.


## Scenario — THE POLICY THAT QUOTED A PROPOSAL

Brightwater Payments has a draft customer policy: “If a scammer impersonates our staff, we will refund you in full.” The author, Luca Ferrante, who works in the compliance team of a firm serving several euro-area states, bases it on a recital in the proposed Payment Services Regulation. His manager asks whether it is accurate today. Luca says the text has been politically agreed, so it is effectively law, and proposes to launch the policy and build a refund process around it. **Question.** Is Luca’s reasoning sound? **Answer.** No. The package is proposed, not adopted. The agreed text was read only at recital level, so the operative article, the scope of “impersonation” and conditions such as police reporting are unconfirmed.

A policy promising a legal right on this basis misstates the law. Brightwater can still plan: track the legislative stage, map the evidence and operations a spoofing refund would need, and treat any voluntary refund policy as a separate commercial decision made through governance.


## What to take from this chapter
- Under PSD2 the PSP bears the burden of proof on authentication and gross negligence, and SCA with dynamic linking of amount and payee is the hinge of its liability model
- The Court’s ruling, as summarised, requires refund first for a reported unauthorised transaction, with delay only for reasonable suspicion of customer fraud and later recovery only on proof of intent or gross negligence
- A no-match name check does not block a payment, and a PSP that gives a correct result is not liable under the identifier rule if the payer proceeds
- Authorised payments to a fraudster are generally not refundable under PSD2, which is why the name check exists
- The proposed directive and Payment Services Regulation are not law until adopted, and recital-level reading must not be presented to customers as a right.
