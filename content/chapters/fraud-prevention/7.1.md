# Who bears the loss: the UK

The UK has two separate legal ideas that are easy to confuse. One is a mandatory scheme under which payment firms reimburse victims of authorised scams. The other is a corporate offence aimed at fraud that benefits an organisation, committed by someone acting for it. The first decides who repays a victim. The second decides when a large organisation can itself be prosecuted. This chapter teaches the first as a loss-allocation system and the second as a liability test.


**7.1.1** Unauthorised and authorised: the first triage question Every loss-allocation rule starts from one question: who initiated the payment? If someone else made the payment without the customer’s consent, it is unauthorised. If the customer made or approved it, even when deceived, it is authorised. Chapter 3.1 covers the authorised push payment (APP) scam, for which the UK reimbursement scheme is built. Unauthorised payments sit under a different law, the Payment Services Regulations. In broad terms, a firm must put an unauthorised payment right promptly, and the customer carries the loss only in narrow cases such as acting fraudulently or with gross negligence, with the firm having to prove it. The two routes have different rules, evidence and outcomes, and cases in between, such as remote-access software installed at the fraudster’s request, need careful fact-finding about who did what.


**7.1.2** The APP reimbursement scheme: scope and who pays The scheme was set by the Payment Systems Regulator (PSR), the UK regulator of payment systems. It protects individuals, microenterprises (very small businesses) and charities. It binds payment service providers (PSPs) taking part in Faster Payments, the near-instant retail system, or CHAPS, the high-value same-day system, and covers payments on those systems only. The mechanism has two layers. The first protects the customer: the sending PSP, which holds the victim’s account, reimburses the customer quickly. The second settles the cost between firms: the receiving PSP, which holds the account that received the money, bears a share. The split is designed to be roughly equal, limited to what is attributable to the receiving firm, and the sending PSP claims it only after reimbursing the customer, so the victim is never left waiting while the banks argue. The reason for sharing is incentive: a scheme that charged only the sending firm would give the receiving firm, which controls account opening and monitoring, no reason to police mule accounts (Chapter 3.3). Three features shape practice. An optional small excess may be applied but never to a vulnerable customer, and the cost split is calculated as if it applied. There is a maximum payable per claim. And a short decision period has a clock that can be paused only for listed reasons, such as information needed from the victim or receiving firm, suspected first-party fraud or a multi-step scam, with an outer limit. The figures are in the current rules.


## Scenario — THE BANK THAT WANTED TO WAIT

Priya Nair reports to Larchmont Bank that she paid a large sum by Faster Payments to a fraudster posing as her building contractor. She is an individual, not vulnerable, and reports within days. The money went to an account at Delmar Credit. A Larchmont analyst proposes holding the claim until Delmar Credit confirms it will pay its share, to avoid carrying the whole loss. The team lead notes that the decision clock keeps running. **Question.** Is the plan to wait for Delmar Credit’s confirmation compatible with the scheme? **Answer.** No. The sending PSP reimburses first and claims from the receiving PSP afterwards, so Larchmont’s duty does not depend on Delmar Credit agreeing to pay.

The clock can be paused only for listed reasons, and waiting for the other bank’s promise is not one of them. A sound practitioner decides within the period, pays, claims the receiving firm’s share, and records any lawful pause with its reason. The tempting error is to treat the receiving bank as a guarantor whose agreement must come first.


**7.1.3** Defences and carve-outs: first-party fraud, gross negligence and vulnerability A firm may refuse reimbursement in only two situations. The first is first-party fraud, where the claimant is in truth the fraudster or is working with one. The second is gross negligence by the customer. There is no general rule that a customer who “should have known better” loses protection. Gross negligence is a high bar: a significant degree of carelessness, not ordinary lack of caution. The scheme asks whether the customer paid regard to clear interventions such as a bank warning, reported promptly, answered reasonable requests and agreed to police reporting if asked. The burden of proof sits with the firm and cannot be shifted to the customer through terms and conditions. Neither exception applies to a vulnerable customer, judged case by case against the regulator’s definition of someone especially susceptible to harm because of personal circumstances.


**7.1.4** Failure to prevent fraud The Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act created an offence of failure to prevent fraud. Chapter 2.6 explains why a programme must account for it. It is not a reimbursement rule and repays no victim. It makes a large organisation criminally liable when someone associated with it commits a fraud intending to benefit the organisation, unless the organisation shows it had reasonable prevention procedures. Four elements matter. First, the organisation must be large, a body corporate or partnership wherever formed: it is caught if it meets at least two of three tests on turnover, balance sheet total and employee numbers, with group figures counted together for a parent. Second, the fraud must be committed by an associated person: an employee, agent, subsidiary or anyone performing services on the organisation’s behalf. Third, it must be a listed offence, such as fraud by false representation, false accounting or fraudulent trading, including aiding and abetting. Fourth, the person must have intended to benefit the organisation, or someone to whom services are provided on its behalf. There is no liability where the organisation was, or was meant to be, the victim: the employee who steals from the employer does not trigger the offence. The defence is that the organisation had prevention procedures it was reasonable to expect, or that none was reasonable. The burden 144 lies on the organisation, to the civil standard of proof, and the penalty is an unlimited fine. Government guidance sets six principles: top-level commitment, risk assessment, proportionate procedures, due diligence, training and communication, and monitoring and review. It is advisory and not a safe harbour.


## Scenario — THE AGENT AND THE ACCOUNTS

Kestrel Lending is a single company that exceeds the limits on turnover and balance sheet total for a large organisation, although its employee count is below the limit. A broker, Daniel Ruiz, is paid for each completed loan. He falsifies income documents so that loans complete, knowing Kestrel Lending earns a fee on each. Separately, a clerk makes false expense claims. The compliance lead says neither event can engage the offence, because Kestrel Lending was not “the fraudster” and, with few employees, is not large anyway. **Question.** Does either event engage the offence, and is Kestrel Lending large? **Answer.** Only the broker’s conduct engages it. Kestrel Lending meets two of the three size tests, so it is large despite its headcount.

Daniel is an agent and so can be an associated person, and he committed a fraud intending to benefit Kestrel Lending through the fees. The clerk does not engage the offence: he intends to benefit himself, and the company is the victim. The compliance lead made two errors. “Not the fraudster” misreads the offence: it is enough that the organisation failed to prevent a benefiting fraud by someone acting for it. And headcount is only one of three tests, of which two must be met. Kestrel Lending’s defence is reasonable prevention procedures, so a sound response tests the broker channel against the six principles.


## What to take from this chapter
- The first triage question is who initiated the payment: authorised payments fall within the APP scheme, and payments made by someone else without consent are unauthorised and follow different law
- The APP scheme protects individuals, microenterprises and charities on covered systems: the sending firm reimburses first, then claims a share from the receiving firm
- A firm may refuse only for first-party fraud or gross negligence, a significant degree of carelessness, the burden of proof is the firm’s, and neither exception applies to a vulnerable customer
- Failure to prevent fraud is a corporate offence, not a reimbursement rule: a large organisation is liable for an associated person’s fraud intended to benefit it unless it shows reasonable prevention procedures
- There is no offence where the organisation was the intended victim, and an agent or service provider can be an associated person.
