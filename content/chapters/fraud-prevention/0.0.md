2 Anti-Fraud Certification — Study Guide Prepared as the course material for the Certified Fraud Prevention Analyst credential. Accredited by the accreditor. This guide is educational material. It is not legal advice, and it does not state what the law requires of any particular organisation, payment or customer. This guide teaches principles and mechanisms, which change slowly. It deliberately does not print figures, dates or thresholds, because they go out of date. For current numbers and rules, use the regulator or source named in the text. Rely on the text of the law, the guidance of the regulators and professional advice rather than on anything here. Where a point could not be confirmed against a primary source when the guide was prepared, the text says “to be verified”, and that point should not be relied on until it has been checked. All organisations, people, accounts, customers and cases used in examples and scenarios are invented, and the amounts in worked examples are invented to teach the reasoning. 3 Who this guide is for This guide is written for the person who has to deal with fraud inside a bank, a fintech or a payments firm: who has to decide whether a payment should be held, whether a pattern is a scheme, whether a control is worth its cost, or what to say to a customer who has just lost their savings. It assumes no prior knowledge of fraud work. It assumes you are comfortable with the ordinary vocabulary of banking, and it explains every fraud term the first time it appears. That is a wider group than the job title “fraud analyst” suggests. Fraud touches operations, customer service, payments, credit, compliance, internal audit, risk, product and technology. In many firms the work of preventing it is shared between people who would never describe themselves as fraud specialists. If you work in fraud operations You will already know the alerts, the queues and the pressure to clear them. What this guide adds is the reasoning behind the work: why a rule exists, what a metric does and does not tell you, and how a decision on one case should feed the controls that produced it. Modules 5 and 6 are closest to your day. The habit most worth building is to ask what a signal could mean besides fraud, and what a sound decision would look like if you turned out to be wrong. If you work in compliance, risk or audit You will recognise governance, risk assessment, three lines, evidence and reporting. The new part is the subject matter. Fraud has its own schemes, its own language of loss and its own liability rules, and these differ sharply from the rules for money laundering, which you may know better. Module 2 sets out the programme and Module 7 sets out who bears the loss. If you work in product, payments or technology Every control you build adds friction for a genuine customer, and every gap you leave is a route for a criminal. This guide is useful to you for the trade-off it describes: what each control prevents, what it costs, and how it fails. Chapters 2.3, 3.1, 3.2 and 5.5 are the best starting points. If you are moving into this field Fraud work has a low barrier to entry and an unusually fast-moving subject. That is an opportunity, and it is also a risk, because it is easy to learn the names of schemes and to mistake that for understanding them. If this is your position, read in order. Modules 1 and 2 give you the vocabulary and the frame, and everything after assumes them. What you need before you start Nothing formal. No prior certification, no degree requirement and no minimum experience. You do need to be willing to read a scenario carefully, because in fraud work the detail that changes the answer is usually a small one: whether the customer made the payment or the criminal did, whether the account was opened last week or last year, whether an alert was ignored or never raised. 4 Two distinctions will help throughout. The first is between authorised and unauthorised payments: a payment the customer made, however badly deceived, and a payment they did not make. The second is between the three origins of fraud: first-party, where the customer is the fraudster; third-party, where an outsider is; and insider, where a member of staff is. Chapter 1.4 explains both, and much of the guide depends on them. 5 The work this prepares you for Fraud work is described in two unhelpful ways: as a technical contest between criminals and software, or as a matter of suspicion and interrogation. It is neither of these. What the work actually is Most of it is judgement under incomplete information. A payment is in front of you, a customer is on the phone or a system has scored an application, and you have a few minutes and some of the facts. The skill is knowing which facts to look for, which to trust and what the next sensible step is. The work divides into four stages, which the guide returns to throughout. Prevent: make the scheme harder or less attractive. Detect: notice it when it happens, in time to matter. Respond: investigate, deal with the customer and report. Recover: get money back where it can be got, and learn from what went wrong. A firm that is strong at one stage and weak at another has a gap that criminals will find. What it is not is catching villains. Most of the customers you deal with are victims or are innocent. Most alerts are false. Much of the real work is deciding quickly and fairly, in a way you could explain to the customer, to a regulator and to yourself a year later. Why it is harder than it sounds Because the criminals adapt. A control works until it becomes known, and then the scheme moves to the next weakest point. A rule that was right last quarter can be a source of false alerts and missed fraud this quarter. Because the cost of being wrong runs both ways. Stop too little and the customer loses money, and in many places the firm must pay it back. Stop too much and genuine customers are insulted, delayed or driven away. There is no setting that removes the trade-off, and the work is to place it deliberately. Because the rules about who bears the loss differ by country and by type of payment, and they have been changing quickly. A scam that costs the customer everything in one country may cost the bank in another. Module 7 sets out the main regimes and, as importantly, how to tell when a source about them has gone out of date. And because fraud does not respect organisation charts. A single scheme can start in an online application, pass through a call centre, move through a mule account and end in a laundering report. The guide treats those connections as part of the subject. Where it leads Fraud work sits beside financial crime compliance, cyber security, credit risk, operational risk and customer protection, and many practitioners move between them. Investigation skills, evidence handling and the habit of writing a decision down carry across all of them. 6 Foreword Most firms already do some of this well. They have rules that stop obvious fraud, staff who spot odd behaviour and a process for dealing with complaints. What many lack is a connected picture: a clear view of which schemes matter, which controls address them, how well those controls work and who is responsible when they do not. This guide tries to supply that picture. It does so with plain language, defined terms and a lot of worked examples, because fraud is learned by applying it. Every chapter ends with scenarios that ask what a sound practitioner would do, and each module contains a composite case study of the kind that real firms have lived through. Four themes run through it and are worth stating at the outset. The first is that fraud is a set of different problems, not one. A scam in which a customer is persuaded to pay, a takeover in which a criminal logs in as the customer and a card used by someone who stole it have different signals, different controls and, in many places, different rules about who pays. Treating them alike is the commonest early mistake. The second is that every control has a cost to genuine customers. A control that is never felt is probably not doing much, and a control that is felt by everyone is probably too blunt. The practitioner’s job is to know which of the two they are looking at, and to have the evidence for it. The third is that numbers must be read, not just quoted. Headline loss figures from different sources measure different things, over different periods, for different populations. A figure without its definition is a rumour. Chapter 1.3 teaches how to read them, and Chapter 1.5 teaches how to read the firm’s own. The fourth is that the record is the work. An alert closed with the note “OK”, a decision that cannot be explained and a lesson that was not written down will all be paid for later. The guide returns often to the discipline of recording what was decided and why. Work through the modules in order. Each assumes the one before it. 7 How to use this guide The structure is consistent throughout, and knowing what each element is for will save you time. Modules, chapters and topics There are seven modules. Each holds five to seven chapters, and each chapter is divided into numbered topics. A topic is a single idea, short enough to read in one sitting. Cross-references use the full number. A reference to Chapter 3.2 means Module 3, chapter 2; a reference to topic 3.2.1 means the first topic within it. Chapters begin on a fresh page, so you can stop at the end of one without losing your place. Scenarios Every chapter contains scenarios: a situation, a question and an answer. Every organisation and person in them is invented. Read the situation and the question, and decide what you would do before reading the answer. The scenarios are written so that the obvious answer is often incomplete or wrong, and the answer explains why. Working them first, and reading them second, is the single most useful habit the guide can give you. Case studies Each module contains one longer case study, with a topic title that begins “Case study”. These are fictional composites told as a short timeline: what happened, what the firm saw and missed, what it did and what it learned. They are the best place to see how the ideas of a module fit together under pressure. What to take from this chapter Each chapter ends with a summary panel. It is a revision aid and not a substitute for the chapter, but it is the quickest way to check that you have the points that matter before moving on. Quick reference The back of the guide holds a glossary, a table of fraud types at a glance, a table of key distinctions and a control and response checklist. They are designed for the moment before an assessment, or the moment a question lands on your desk. “To be verified” Where a point could not be confirmed against a primary source when the guide was prepared, the text says “to be verified”. This module settles those five points. It gives the common legal ideas behind fraud offences, the two classic models of motive, a careful method for reading loss datasets, a map of the fraud function, and the vocabulary of loss that fraud teams use every day. It teaches how to read figures, not which figures to remember, because current numbers are best taken from the publishers.
