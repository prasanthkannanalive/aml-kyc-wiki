# Money mules and mule accounts

Every scam and every takeover ends in the same practical problem for the criminal: the money has landed in an account that can be traced, so it has to be moved quickly and cleanly. Mule accounts solve that problem. They sit between the crime and the criminal, which makes them the best point at which an institution can see a whole network and the point at which fraud meets money laundering. This chapter explains who mules are, how accounts become mule accounts, what the flow of money and the account signals look like, how fraud and anti-money laundering work overlap, and why a possible mule is often also a victim.


**3.3.1** Witting, unwitting and complicit mules A money mule is a person who moves money for someone else, usually money obtained through crime. The US Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) describes a mule as a person, witting or unwitting, who moves illicit funds on behalf of another. Muling is a form of money laundering, because it helps to separate the funds from their source. The term also covers the account itself: a mule account is any account used to receive and move proceeds of crime. The key distinction is what the account holder knew. It matters for how the institution treats the customer, how the case is recorded and how any criminal liability is judged, which is a matter for the authorities, not for the analyst. **3.3.2** How accounts become mule accounts At awareness level, an account becomes a mule account in one of four ways. Each is a different failure or attack for the institution to prevent, and each leaves different evidence. Opened for the purpose. Someone opens an account intending to use it for moving criminal money. They may use their own identity, a borrowed identity or a synthetic one, which is a fabricated person built from a mix of real and invented details, and Chapter 3.5 covers identity and application fraud. The signal is at onboarding and in the first weeks: little activity that matches the stated purpose, then a burst of inbound payments. Genuine account, used by arrangement. The customer opened the account honestly and later knowingly lets it be used: the holder knows the account is being used by someone else, whether because they rent or lend it for a fee or because they are pressured into it. Cifas describes this as misuse of facility: the genuine holder misusing their own account. The signal is a change in the pattern of an established account. Taken over. The account belongs to an innocent customer but has been taken over by a criminal, as in Chapter 3.2, and used to receive and move money. The customer is a victim and may not know. The signals are the takeover signals described in Chapter 3.2, followed by the flow signals below. Victim turned mule. The holder believes the activity is legitimate, or is themselves being defrauded. A scam victim is told to receive money and pass it on, as part of a job, a romance or a “recovery” story. They are not complicit, and they are not in control. Mr Albright in the case study at the end of this chapter, and Camille in the scenario below, fall into this group.


**3.3.3** The flow pattern and account signals Seen from the receiving side, a mule account usually performs one job: it receives money and passes it on, quickly. The pattern has three parts. Money arrives from several unrelated senders, often victims of different scams, in amounts that do not fit the account’s history. Very little of it stays. Within a short time it leaves by a route that makes recovery harder: onward transfers to other accounts, cash withdrawals, conversion to crypto-assets or prepaid products, or payments to a second tier of accounts. This is layering, the stage of money laundering in which funds are moved to separate them from their source. Networks usually use several tiers, so the first account to receive the victim’s money is only the start of the chain. Two features separate a mule account from an ordinary busy one. Pass-through speed is one: a legitimate account’s outflows normally fit the customer’s known pattern and purpose, whereas a mule’s balance returns close to where it started. Counterparty spread is the other: many unconnected payers feeding a single account, and the proceeds leaving to a different small set of recipients. Neither is conclusive: a small trader paid by many customers can look similar.


## Scenario — THE BUSY SHOP ACCOUNT

Larchmont Bank’s monitoring flags a business account held by Dev Kapoor, who sells second-hand bicycles online. Over one week the account received credits from many unrelated senders and sent most of the money onward within hours, mainly to two payees and as cash withdrawals. The analyst, Sofia Marchetti, notes that the account is only a few weeks old and that the stated turnover on opening was small. A colleague argues that many payers and fast onward payments are the mule pattern, so the account should be treated as a mule account now. **Question.** Is the pattern enough to conclude that this is a mule account? **Answer.** No, not yet.

Many unrelated payers and quick onward payments are the mule pattern, but a small online trader can show the same flow: customers pay in, and the trader pays suppliers and withdraws cash. What separates the two is whether the activity fits a lawful purpose. Here the thin history and the gap between stated and actual turnover raise the risk, yet they do not prove it. Sofia should test the explanation against the evidence. She can look at payment references, whether the inbound payers look like customers or like victims, whether the onward payees are suppliers, and whether any inbound payment has been reported as fraud by another bank. A conversation with Mr Kapoor and a request for trading records would settle most of it.

A limited restriction is justified while the question is open, but labelling him a mule before checking is not.


**3.3.4** Where fraud and money laundering meet Mule activity is the point where two disciplines share a case. Fraud is the predicate offence, the crime that produces the proceeds. Moving the proceeds is money laundering. The same account can therefore be a fraud issue, because it received a victim’s money, and an anti-money laundering issue, because it is being used to launder it. The practical consequence is organisational. Where fraud and anti-money laundering are separate teams, a mule account can fall between them: the fraud team sees a reported payment and stops at recovery, while the anti-money laundering team sees an unusual pattern and does not know it is linked to a scam. The remedy is a shared view of the account, a clear owner for the case and a route for the facts to pass in both directions, which Chapter 1.4 describes in the context of how fraud sits in the institution.


**3.3.5** The mule as victim and as risk An institution that treats every possible mule as a criminal makes three mistakes. It misjudges the person, because many mules are deceived or pressured. It loses intelligence, because a frightened person who is told they are a suspect stops talking, and the recruiter goes unidentified. And it can cause real harm to a customer who is already a victim, since losing an account can mean losing wages, benefits and access to payments. An institution that treats every mule only as a victim makes the opposite mistake. The account is a channel for other victims’ money, and leaving it open while the facts are sorted can mean further losses. The balance is: treat the account as a risk and the person as a possible victim. In practice, that means a sequence. Restrict the account as policy and law permit, so that further proceeds do not pass through. Contact the customer and ask open questions about where the money came from, who asked them to receive it and what they were told. Listen for signs of recruitment: a job that involves receiving and forwarding payments, a person they have never met giving instructions, a fee for the use of the account. Tell them plainly what to stop doing and where to seek help. Record the outcome carefully, because closing the account or recording the 60 customer on an industry fraud database must rest on evidence. Cifas, for example, expects reasonable grounds and clear, relevant evidence before a member records a case.


**3.3.6** Case study: The apprentice, the account and the long weekend Composite case, fictional. Larchmont Bank runs a retail fraud team of eight analysts, with a separate financial crime team. A customer, Jonas Albright, is nineteen and has held a basic current account for a year, used mainly for a part-time wage and small card purchases. Nothing at opening or since suggested a risk, and the onboarding team saw an ordinary young customer with a thin history. A thin history is common among recruited mules, so it is a reason to watch, not a reason to refuse an account. On a Thursday evening, a customer of another bank reports that she paid a deposit for a rental property to an account at Larchmont and the property does not exist. Her bank sends an alert to Larchmont’s fraud team through the inter-bank fraud reporting channel. The alert arrives after the team has gone home. It is read on Monday morning. Over the long weekend, Mr Albright’s account receives nine payments from seven different senders, each with a reference suggesting a rental or a purchase. By Sunday evening his account has received more in three days than in the whole of the previous year. Within hours of each credit, most of the money leaves: some by transfer to two new payees, some by cash withdrawal at machines in a city he has never visited, and some by transfer to a crypto-asset exchange. His mobile application shows logins from the same phone throughout, but the session data records that remote access software was running during two of the logins. Larchmont’s transaction monitoring generates an alert on Saturday for unusual inflows, but the queue is not staffed until Monday, and nobody links it to Thursday’s alert. On Monday, the analyst, Kofi Mensah, finds the incoming alert and the queued monitoring alert together. He checks the account history, sees the pass-through pattern, and restricts the account to stop further outbound payments. He then calls Mr Albright that morning. Mr Albright says he took a job online as a “payment coordinator” for a company that pays him a share of each transfer he handles; he was told to keep the money moving, to say it was for family if the bank asked, and to install an application so his “manager” could help with setup. He has been paid a small fee. He sounds frightened and says he did not think it was illegal. Kofi decides that closing the account and recording a fraud marker is premature, since the evidence suggests Mr Albright was recruited and coached, but that leaving it open is not acceptable. He restricts the account, records the full conversation, explains to Mr Albright in plain terms what he must stop doing and why, and passes the case to the financial crime team with the recruiter’s details and the account numbers of the onward payees. Financial crime decides about reporting and about whether to share information with the institutions holding the onward accounts. A senior manager later decides to close the account and records the reasons in the case file. The weekend gap is the costly part, in plain terms: several victims lost money that a faster reaction could have held, Mr Albright lost his account and the trust of a bank he had used since leaving school, and Larchmont spent a week of analyst and financial-crime time on one case. The alert was available on Thursday, and each hour of delay allowed more of the victims’ money to leave. Cash that has been withdrawn is very hard to recover. The first victim’s bank recovered only what remained. Larchmont later made four changes, all of them to staffing and process: inbound fraud 61 alerts from other institutions are reviewed at weekends, inflow alerts on new accounts are staffed on the same schedule, a monitoring alert on an account is linked automatically to any inbound fraud report that names the same account, and the onboarding team receives feedback about the profile of recruited accounts. The lessons are these. First, speed is the control, because the criminals were using the account long before the bank looked: a mule account is useful to the criminal only while it can pass money on. Second, two alerts about one account were more informative together than apart, so related alerts should be linked automatically. Third, the customer was both a risk and a victim.


## Scenario — THE FAVOUR FOR A FRIEND ONLINE

Camille Duarte, a student, tells a fraud analyst at Delmar Credit that for two months she has been messaging a man she met on a dating app. He says his own bank account is frozen and that his customers abroad need to pay him, so he asks her to receive their payments and forward most of the money to another account, and she keeps a small share as thanks. She says he has been kind and consistent, she has never met him, and she assumed the payers were his customers. Her account shows several unrelated senders a week and fast onward transfers. The analyst’s manager says Camille should be recorded as a deliberate mule and the account closed, because she took a share and the pattern is textbook. **Question.** Is the manager’s conclusion sound? **Answer.** It goes too far.

The pattern is consistent with mule activity, and the account is clearly a risk, but the evidence of what Camille knew points toward an unwitting or, at most, partly aware participant who has been drawn in through a romance story and is likely a victim herself. A share of the money does not by itself prove knowledge, because recruiters offer small rewards so that the arrangement feels like a favour. Recording her as a deliberate mule needs evidence of what she knew, and two months of friendly messages and a plausible story are evidence of recruitment, not of guilt.

The sound course is to restrict the account so no further proceeds pass through, take her account of events in full, pass the recruiter’s details and the onward accounts to the financial crime team, and give her clear information about what to stop doing. The decision on closing the account and on any report belongs to the people designated to make it. Camille is a risk to the institution and is likely also a victim, and the process should protect both interests.


## What to take from this chapter
- A money mule is someone who moves money for another person, and a mule account is any account used to receive and move proceeds of crime
- Mules are witting, unwitting or complicit, and the difference is the knowledge they had, which is found by talking to them, not by looking at a screen
- Accounts become mule accounts in four ways: opened for the purpose, genuine accounts used by arrangement, taken-over accounts, and victims turned mules
- The core flow is many unrelated payers in, fast pass-through, and exit by transfer, cash or conversion; pass-through speed and counterparty spread separate a mule from a busy honest account
- Mule activity is where fraud and money laundering meet, so the facts must pass between the two teams quickly, and the reporting detail sits in Chapter 6.5
- Treat the account as a risk and the person as a possible victim: restrict the account, ask open questions, record the evidence and decide on closure and reporting at the right level
- Speed is the main control, because a mule account is valuable to a criminal only while it can pass money on.
