Tips, hotlines and whistleblowing Tips detect more occupational fraud than any other method: someone says something. A colleague notices a pattern, a supplier is asked for a favour, a customer sees a charge that makes no sense. Whether that person speaks, and to whom, depends on the reporting channel the organisation has built and on whether people trust it. This chapter explains the channels, the protections that make people willing to use them, how reports should be received and triaged, and why the quality of a channel matters more than its existence. 4.5.1  Why tips matter and who gives them The ACFE’s research finds that tips are the most common single way in which occupational fraud is detected, ahead of internal audit, management review and other methods. It also reports that the largest source of tips is the organisation’s own employees, followed by customers, anonymous sources and vendors. A reporting system therefore has to be open to all of them, not only to staff. Controls and analytics find patterns, but a person can say “something is not right here” before any pattern exists. 4.5.2  Channels and how to design them A reporting channel is any route by which a person can raise a concern. Good practice offers several, because people differ in what they trust and in what they can use. Channel Strengths Weaknesses Line manager Familiar, immediate, no special system The manager may be involved or may dismiss the concern; the reporter is exposed Compliance, HR or fraud team contact Trained recipient, closer to the right action Staff may fear being identified Telephone hotline Allows a conversation and follow-up questions Needs staffing or a provider; some people avoid voice Web form or app Available at any time, easy to attach documents, supports anonymous two-way messaging Needs clear design; some staff doubt that it is anonymous Independent third-party service Seen as separate from management; can offer anonymity and a trained first contact Cost; the provider needs good briefing Direct line to the audit committee or board Gives a route when management is the problem Used rarely; needs publicity so staff know it exists Table 4.5: Reporting channels and their trade-offs A worked example shows why variety matters. An employee who suspects her manager of splitting orders will not use the line manager route and may not trust HR if the manager is senior. A web form with two-way anonymous messaging lets her report from home and answer questions without revealing herself, and a direct line to the audit committee gives her somewhere to go if the manager is a director. Three design points matter. First, one route must bypass management, because the person suspected may be a manager. Second, the channel must reach people outside the firm: vendors, customers and agents should know they can report, and third-party contracts should say so (Chapter 4.4). Third, the channel must be publicised, in plain language, at hiring, in training and where staff work. A hotline nobody remembers is of no use. 97 4.5.3  Anonymity, confidentiality and non-retaliation Three ideas are often confused. Anonymity means the receiver does not know who the reporter is. Confidentiality means the receiver knows, but promises to limit who else does. Protection against retaliation means the organisation will not punish the reporter for raising a concern in good faith. A sound programme offers all three, and is honest about the limits of each. Anonymity encourages people who fear exposure to speak, and the ACFE counts anonymous reports among the sources of tips. It has costs. The investigator cannot ask follow-up questions unless the system supports two-way anonymous messaging, and the reporter cannot be told the outcome. The honest message to staff is that the organisation will protect identity as far as it can, but cannot guarantee it, for instance if a court orders disclosure. Non-retaliation is the commitment that matters most for trust. People will not report if they believe it will cost them their job, promotion or standing. Retaliation can be obvious, as in dismissal, or quiet, as in a poorer rota or exclusion from meetings. The organisation should state that retaliation is itself misconduct, check on the reporter’s treatment after a report, and deal firmly with any case. Whistleblower protection laws exist in many jurisdictions. They set out who is protected, what is protected and what remedies exist. The specific rules differ widely and are outside this guide, and an organisation should take legal advice on those that apply to it. What matters for the analyst is that the internal policy should meet or exceed the legal minimum and should not depend on the reporter knowing the law. SCENARIO — THE ANONYMOUS MESSAGE Corvane Bank’s web hotline receives an anonymous message: “The head of procurement is splitting orders to stay under the tender limit, and a supplier called Ridgeway pays for his holidays.” The message gives two purchase order numbers and no name. The hotline allows anonymous replies. The compliance officer, Neil Archer, considers three options: ignore it because it is anonymous and unsupported, forward the full message to the head of procurement’s own director for comment, or log it, test the purchase orders independently and reply through the system asking for more detail. Question.  Which option is sound, and why? Answer.  The third. An anonymous report is still information, and the message contains specific, checkable details: two purchase order numbers, a named supplier and a described method. Order splitting and supplier hospitality are recognised bid-rigging and kickback indicators (Chapter 4.2). The bank should check the orders against the tender limit and the supplier’s history, and look for gifts or hospitality records, while keeping the circle of knowledge small. Replying through the system invites the reporter to add facts without revealing identity. Ignoring the message would waste a lead, and the ACFE’s research puts tips at the head of detection methods. Forwarding it to the director would break confidentiality, risk alerting the person concerned and may involve someone who is not independent. The right approach is to treat the report as a lead to test, not as proof, and to protect both the reporter and the person reported until facts are known. 4.5.4  Handling reports: intake, triage and feedback A report is the start of a process. How it is handled decides whether the tip becomes a finding. At intake, the recipient records the report in a case log with the date, channel, summary and evidence, and assigns a reference number. The recipient listens without judging, promises no outcomes, and tells the reporter how confidentiality will be handled and that retaliation is forbidden. Evidence provided is secured at once. 98 Triage decides what happens next. The reviewer asks whether the allegation is specific and checkable, how serious it is, whether it is about fraud or belongs elsewhere (for example to HR), whether there is a risk of lost evidence or retaliation that calls for immediate action, and who must be independent: if the subject is in the compliance function or is a senior executive, the report must go outside that line, for example to the audit committee. Triage should be done by someone trained and independent of the subject. The investigation follows the principles in Module 6. The ACFE’s Code of Professional Standards, written for its own members, sets out principles that are good practice for any investigation: the examination is legal, professional and thorough; the investigator guards against conjecture and bias and weighs evidence pointing away from guilt as well as toward it; evidence is controlled; confidentiality is kept unless disclosure is authorised or required; and reports state facts and give no opinion on legal guilt or innocence. Feedback closes the loop. The reporter is told that the report was received, and later, as far as is allowed, that it was looked into and that action has been taken. But silence teaches staff that reporting is pointless. 4.5.5  Why the quality of the channel matters A hotline is a process and a promise, not a telephone number. Its quality is judged by what happens after a report. A good channel is known, trusted, quick and independent. The signs of a poor one are clear. It is run by a function that is also the subject of many reports. Reports vanish without reply. Names leak. A reporter is moved or left out after speaking. The channel exists on paper only. A low number of reports is not, by itself, good news: it may mean that people do not trust the channel. To test a channel, an audit team can survey staff anonymously on whether they know how to report and would trust the process, trace recent reports from intake to outcome, and compare the number of reports with problems known from other sources such as audit findings and complaints. A channel with few reports while other sources show many problems is not trusted. SCENARIO — A HOTLINE THAT NOBODY USES Delmar Credit reports to its audit committee that its ethics hotline, run by the human resources department, received only two reports last year, both about workplace conduct. The head of HR presents this as evidence of a strong ethical culture and proposes no change. A recent internal audit found that most staff did not know the hotline number, that reports are logged in a spreadsheet kept by HR, and that two employees who had raised concerns informally with their managers said they later felt sidelined. Internally, the fraud team has also discovered a purchasing scheme that no one had reported. Question.  Is the low volume of reports reassuring? Answer.  No. The surrounding facts point to a channel that is not trusted or not known. Staff do not know the number, the reports are held by a department that also handles staff discipline, and the people who spoke up say they were treated badly. A scheme was running unreported. The ACFE’s research finds that tips are the leading way in which fraud is detected, so a near-absence of tips alongside an undetected scheme suggests that the channel is not doing its job. A low count measures use, not the level of honesty. A sound practitioner would recommend a review of the channel: publicise it with a route that bypasses HR and management, such as an independent provider or the audit committee, secure the case log, check how the two informal reporters were treated, state that retaliation is misconduct, and report to the committee on use, speed, outcomes and staff awareness. 99 WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — Tips are the most common single route by which the ACFE finds occupational fraud to have been detected, and employees are the largest source. — A good reporting system offers several channels, including one that bypasses management, and is open to customers, vendors and agents as well as staff. — Anonymity, confidentiality and non-retaliation are different protections, and a programme should offer all three and be honest about their limits. — Whistleblower protection laws exist in many jurisdictions, but the specific rules are outside this guide, and an internal policy should not depend on the reporter knowing them. — Handling a report means recording it, triaging it by an independent and trained person, investigating with an open mind, giving feedback and learning from the result. — ACFE guidance on professional investigations expects evidence to be controlled, confidentiality to be kept, and no opinion to be given on legal guilt. — A hotline is judged by trust, speed and outcomes, and a low report count may signal distrust, not a clean organisation. 100 MODULE 5 Detection and analytics Prevention stops what it can see coming, and detection catches the rest.
