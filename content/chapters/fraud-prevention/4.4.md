# Third-party and outsourcing risk

Banks and payments firms rely on outsiders to do work that touches customers, money and data. A third party is any organisation outside the firm that provides a service, a product or access. When the work is outsourced, the risk stays with the institution. In practice, customers and supervisors look to the firm for what its outsourced call centre, broker or partner bank does in its name. This chapter explains how third parties become a fraud channel, the main types, and the cycle of due diligence, contract, monitoring and exit that keeps the risk in check.


**4.4.1** How third parties become a fraud channel A third party can be a victim, a tool or a perpetrator of fraud, and the institution is exposed in each case. As a victim, the third party is compromised, and the compromise passes to the institution through the connection between them. As a tool, an outsider uses the third party’s access, for example a stolen vendor login, to reach the institution. As a perpetrator, the third party or its staff commits the fraud directly, by inflating bills, misusing data, or acting dishonestly toward the firm’s customers. Three features set outsourcing apart from internal risk. The first is distance: the institution cannot see the third party’s staff, systems or culture, so it relies on assurance. The second is access: a third party often holds customer data, system credentials or the ability to move money. The third is chains: the third party uses subcontractors of its own, and the institution may not know them. The risk of the weakest link is real. A fraud that began in a subcontractor’s call centre is, to the customer, the bank’s fraud. The UK failure-to-prevent-fraud offence, described in Chapter 2.6, can reach frauds committed by agents and others who provide services for the organisation, but only where the fraud was intended to benefit the organisation. It is not a general rule that the firm is liable for everything its suppliers do, and most frauds in this chapter, such as shell vendors or broker inflation, harm the firm or its customers instead. The detail is in Module 7. The planning point is that the fraud risk assessment must include third parties, not only staff.

**4.4.3** Due diligence before onboarding Due diligence is the work done before a contract is signed to find out whether the third party is real, honest and capable. It is proportionate: the more access to money, data or customers, the deeper the inquiry. For fraud purposes, due diligence asks several questions. Who owns and controls the third party? Checks on beneficial owners, directors and any link to the firm’s own staff address conflicts of interest and shell vendors. What is its record? Adverse media, regulatory history and litigation show whether there is a history of dishonesty or weak control. How does it recruit and supervise staff with access? Vetting, training and supervision matter most for those who touch customers or money. What controls does it have over data, access and fraud? Evidence is better than assurances: an independent audit report and a sample of completed checks can be tested, a signed questionnaire cannot. Who are its subcontractors? And is it financially stable, because a failing supplier is under pressure to cut corners? Due diligence is repeated, not done once: ownership changes, subcontractors are added and controls decay.


## Scenario — THE BROKER WHO NEVER FAILS

Delmar Credit receives personal-loan applications through a network of brokers, and many of its loans are written through them. The fraud team notices that Sundial Finance, a broker introduced a year ago, has an unusually low rate of declined applications, and that a high share of its approved loans go into early arrears with the first payment missed. Sundial is paid commission on each funded loan, and its commission rate is the highest in the network. **Question.** How should the fraud team read this, and what should it do? **Answer.** The team should read it as a probable application fraud pattern involving the broker, until the data show otherwise. Low declines with early default is the typical outcome when applications are doctored to pass the firm’s checks.

The commission structure gives Sundial a reason to inflate. Passing due diligence at onboarding is a snapshot taken a year ago; it does not show how Sundial behaves now, and the absence of complaints is not evidence of quality when the victims may be the firm itself or identities whose real owners have not yet been contacted. The team should compare Sundial’s applications with those from other sources, sample the files for verification of income and employer, and share its findings with the relationship manager and the third-party risk function. Depending on the findings it may suspend new business pending review, invoke contract rights to audit and to claw back commission, and consider reporting.


**4.4.4** Contract clauses and monitoring The contract is the firm’s main tool for turning expectations into obligations. For fraud risk, the clauses that matter are practical ones. This is not legal advice: the wording is for the firm’s legal advisers. — Standards: a duty to maintain controls, vetting and training equivalent to the firm’s own for staff with access to money, data or customers. — Right to audit: the firm’s, and its regulator’s, right to inspect the third party’s premises, records and controls, including those of subcontractors. — Reporting: a duty to tell the firm promptly of fraud, breach, suspected staff misconduct or an approach by criminals, with defined timescales, and a route for the third party’s own staff to report concerns to the firm’s channel (Chapter 4.5). 94 — Cooperation: a duty to cooperate with investigations, preserve records and give access to people and data. — Subcontracting: no subcontracting of critical work without consent, and flow-down of the same obligations. — Remedies: rights to withhold payment, claw back commission, suspend work and terminate for cause. — Data: rules on how the third party may hold, use, protect and return the firm’s customer data. Monitoring is the continuing check that the third party is behaving as promised. Performance indicators, such as complaint and error rates, show whether the service works. Fraud-specific indicators, such as loss rates by broker or agent, early default, repeat customers of one agent and the volume of resets by an outsourced team, show whether the third party is a channel for fraud. Periodic reviews, with tests, audits and refreshed due diligence, show whether controls still exist. The business owner is accountable, with the fraud and risk functions providing challenge.


**4.4.5** Exit and containment The relationship will end, by plan or in a crisis, and the firm should be able to leave without losing control of its data or its customers. An exit plan sets out how services would be moved in-house or to another provider, how data would be retrieved, and how the firm would continue to meet its obligations meanwhile. Exit from a third party suspected of fraud has special features. The firm needs to preserve records and evidence before the third party knows that action is coming. It should then restrict access to systems and data, secure customer data and any money the third party holds, and revoke credentials, including those of individual staff. Contract rights to terminate for cause, withhold payment and recover losses should be reviewed with legal advisers before notice is given. Invoking audit and cooperation rights tells the third party that the firm is looking, so it is done after the firm’s own evidence is secure. Customers affected may need to be contacted, and the matter may need reporting to a regulator or law enforcement, which Module 6 explains. If the firm gave notice first, the third party could delete records and warn the individuals involved.


## Scenario — CUTTING OFF THE CALL CENTRE

Larchmont Bank outsources part of its card servicing to Larkfield Contact Services, whose staff work in a separate building and use the bank’s systems through remote logins. The fraud team’s analytics show that card replacements and address changes handled by one Larkfield team have been followed by unauthorised transactions at a much higher rate than for the bank’s own agents. The contract allows audit and termination for cause. A senior manager proposes ending the contract at once by sending notice, and removing Larkfield’s access to all systems the same day. The legal adviser suggests collecting the relevant records first. **Question.** What is the sound sequence? **Answer.** The legal adviser is right: evidence first.

The bank should first secure its own logs, recordings and the data that show the pattern. It should add extra verification to the affected actions without announcing why. It should invoke its audit and cooperation rights only after legal advice, once its own evidence is secure, because invoking them tells Larkfield that the bank is looking, and Larkfield or some of its staff may be involved. If the bank gives notice and cuts access immediately, Larkfield’s records, and the opportunity to identify the individuals involved, may be lost, and the bank may find it has weakened its own position under the contract. At the same time, the bank should not wait for certainty before protecting customers.

It can add extra verification to the high-risk actions the Larkfield team performs, for example a second approval or a call to the customer for card replacements and address changes, presented as a general control change, and can monitor affected accounts closely. Termination, reporting and customer contact follow once the facts are clear, with legal and regulatory advice. Containment and evidence preservation can proceed together.


## What to take from this chapter
- A third party can be a victim, a tool or a perpetrator of fraud, and in practice customers and supervisors look to the institution for what its outsourcers do in its name
- The fraud risk assessment must cover third parties, and the UK failure-to-prevent-fraud offence can reach frauds by agents and service providers only where the fraud was intended to benefit the organisation (Chapter 2.6 and Module 7)
- Brokers and agents paid on volume have a built-in incentive to inflate, so their results must be tested against the firm’s own data
- Due diligence should verify ownership, record, staff vetting, controls and subcontractors, and should rely on evidence, not on self-completed questionnaires
- Contracts should secure standards, audit, prompt reporting, cooperation, control of subcontracting, remedies and data handling, and the rights must actually be used
- Monitoring should track fraud-specific indicators for each third party, not only service quality
- Exit planning should allow evidence to be preserved and access to be restricted before a suspect third party is told, and containment can proceed while termination is prepared.
