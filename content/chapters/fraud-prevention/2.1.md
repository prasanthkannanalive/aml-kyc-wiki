Governance and tone A fraud programme starts with decisions that no control can make for itself: who is accountable, how much fraud risk the organisation will accept, and whether staff believe that integrity is expected of everyone, including the most senior. This chapter introduces the main framework used in this guide, the Fraud Risk Management Guide published by COSO with the ACFE, and shows how its first principle turns governance into something an analyst can test. 2.1.1  What a fraud risk management programme is A fraud risk management programme is the organised set of policies, roles, assessments, controls, reporting routes and reviews that an organisation uses to deal with fraud on purpose rather than by reaction. A fraud system, a fraud team or a fraud policy is one part. The programme is the way those parts are joined, owned and kept up to date. Without a programme, fraud work is driven by incidents. A large loss leads to a new rule, the rule creates friction, the friction is relaxed after complaints, and the same gap reopens a year later. With a programme, the organisation can answer five plain questions. Who is accountable for fraud risk? Which fraud schemes could hurt us? What stands between each scheme and a loss? How do we know those controls still work? What do we do when fraud is found? In a bank, fintech or payments firm these questions cover fraud by customers, by criminals against customers, by staff and by suppliers and partners: the three origins from Chapter 1.4, plus third parties (Chapter 4.4).  2.1.2  The COSO and ACFE guide and its five principles The Fraud Risk Management Guide was published by COSO, the Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission, together with the ACFE. COSO is best known for its Internal Control Framework, which has five components. The fraud guide sets out five principles, one for each component, so fraud risk management can be placed inside the structure many institutions already use instead of being invented beside it. The guide adds a data analytics point of focus to each principle and deals with technology, cyber fraud and digital assets. For each row, ask what evidence would show that the principle is present: a board-approved policy and appetite statement; an assessment that names specific schemes; a list of controls with owners and test dates; a reporting route that staff have really used; reports from control owners and internal audit that track deficiencies to closure. A programme that can show evidence for only two rows has two principles in place, however good its detection system is, and the missing rows are where the next loss is likely to come from. 2.1.3  Control environment, accountability and what the board sees The first principle asks for a programme that shows commitment to integrity and ethics from the board and senior management. Three things make that commitment real instead of decorative. The first is a written fraud policy that says fraud will not be tolerated, defines it, states who is accountable, tells staff how to raise concerns and says what happens to those who commit or cover up fraud.  The second is a statement of risk appetite: the amount and type of fraud risk the organisation will accept in pursuit of its aims. A payments firm that wants instant payments has chosen to accept some loss as the price of speed. That choice is legitimate, but it should be made openly by someone with authority, and written down with limits. A practical test of the real control environment is to ask who can override a fraud control, how often it has happened, and who reviewed it. Otherwise appetite is set silently by whoever tunes a rule or approves a launch. The third is behaviour.  Governance also fails at the joins between functions. Card fraud sits in operations, internal fraud in security, vendor fraud in procurement, application fraud in credit. Nobody owns the whole, and a fake-invoice scam and the false-document account that received the money are closed as two cases. A programme names a senior owner who sees all categories, an owner for each category of fraud risk, and an owner for each control, because a control with no owner decays. The board needs the main fraud risks as assessed, loss against stated appetite, significant control weaknesses, major incidents and lessons, and whether the programme has the resources it needs. It also needs a route to hear bad news: a programme that treats a rise in detected fraud as possibly good news is healthier than one that rewards low numbers. SCENARIO — THE TEAM THAT WAS THE PROGRAMME Larchmont Bank has a fraud operations team of twelve people, a modern transaction monitoring system and a quarterly fraud report that goes to the executive committee. The Chief Operating Officer tells the board that Larchmont has a complete fraud risk management programme and no further work is needed. When asked, nobody can show a board-approved statement of fraud risk appetite, a list of who is accountable for fraud in the lending and procurement functions, or a record of when the fraud risk assessment was last updated.  Question.  Does Larchmont have a fraud risk management programme in the sense used by the COSO and ACFE guide? Answer.  Not yet. Larchmont has strong fraud operations, which is one part of control activity. A programme needs a communicated commitment from the board, a documented assessment of specific schemes, selected preventive and detective controls, a reporting and investigation process and monitoring of whether all of this works. The missing appetite statement, the missing accountability for lending and procurement fraud and the stale assessment show that the first two principles are not in place, and the card-and-payment focus shows that whole categories of fraud are unowned. 32 A sound analyst lists the five principles, record which are evidenced and which are not, and propose the first deliverables: a board-approved policy and appetite statement, named owners for each fraud category and a dated plan for the assessment. The common error is to treat a good detection team as proof that governance exists. SCENARIO — THE EXECUTIVE WHO WAIVED THE CHECK At Delmar Credit, a regional lender, the head of sales, Ravi Kapoor, is under pressure to hit a quarterly volume target. He instructs the underwriting team to skip the independent income check on loans from a favoured broker, saying the broker is trusted and the delay is costing deals. A junior underwriter, Mei Lin Tran, complies because Ravi sits two levels above her. Three months later, several of those loans default within weeks of drawdown. Question.  What does this show about the control environment, and what would a sound programme have had in place? Answer.  It shows a failure of the first principle in practice. A fraud policy and an income-check procedure existed, but a senior person could override the control without challenge, an incentive pushed towards cutting corners, and there was no route by which the junior underwriter’s concern would reach anyone with authority to act. Tone at the top was set by the instruction, not by the policy. A sound programme would require overrides of fraud controls to be approved in writing by someone independent of the sales line, logged and reported, would give staff a protected route to raise concerns, and would review whether the volume target itself rewarded risk. The common error is to blame the junior employee for complying instead of fixing the governance that made compliance the easiest course. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — A fraud risk management programme is a joined-up set of governance, assessment, controls, reporting and review, not a single team or system. — The COSO and ACFE guide has five principles that mirror the five components of the COSO Internal Control Framework: control environment, risk assessment, control activities, information and communication, and monitoring. — The control environment principle requires a communicated programme that shows board and senior management commitment to integrity and ethics. — Risk appetite for fraud should be set openly by someone with authority, not silently by whoever tunes a rule. — A programme needs a senior owner across all fraud categories, an owner for each category and an owner for each control. — Overrides of fraud controls by senior staff are a direct test of tone at the top and should be approved independently, logged and reported. 33
