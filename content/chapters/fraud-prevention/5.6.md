# Model risk, data and governance

A fraud model that is wrong does harm in two directions: it lets fraud through, and it disrupts honest customers. Model risk is the risk of loss or harm from a model that is flawed, misused or allowed to decay. This chapter sets out the principles of US model risk guidance, then covers validation, explainability, bias and fairness, data quality, privacy, change control and the place of human judgement. The legal and regulatory frame, including data protection and artificial intelligence rules, is in Module 7.


**5.6.1** US model risk guidance and its scope US supervisors publish interagency model risk guidance for banking organisations. The box below outlines its principles and limits. It describes supervisory expectations and is not advice on what any firm must do. Principle What the guidance says Nature Supervisory guidance from the federal banking agencies, not a statute; it describes expectations for governing models Core expectations A model inventory, sound development, independent validation, ongoing monitoring and clear ownership, scaled to the model’s materiality and the firm’s size and complexity Scope Most relevant to larger banking organisations; generative and agentic AI are treated as novel and fast-changing and fall outside it Box: US model risk guidance in principle. Whether simple rule-based checks count as a “model” is a question to settle from the guidance text, but a machine-learning score estimates a probability from data, so most firms treat it as a model and govern it as one. An exclusion from the guidance is not an exclusion from risk management: a firm using a generative or agentic AI tool in fraud work must still understand, test, control and monitor it under its own policies and any other rules that apply, and those are for Module 7.


**5.6.2** Validation and ongoing monitoring Validation is the independent testing of whether a model does what it is meant to do. Three components are widely recognised in practice. Conceptual soundness asks whether the design, assumptions, data and method are reasonable for the purpose. Outcomes analysis compares what the model predicted with what happened, including back-testing on data it was not trained on. Ongoing monitoring tracks performance, inputs and stability after the model goes live. Validation should be independent of the people who built the model, because builders tend to defend their own choices.


**5.6.3** Explainability, bias and fairness Explainability is the ability to say why a model produced a particular score. It matters for several reasons. Analysts need to know which signals drove an alert so that they can investigate it, customers whose payments are held may ask why, regulators may need to see that a decision rested on sound grounds, and model owners need to spot when a model is leaning on a signal that has stopped meaning what it used to. Fairness asks whether a model treats groups of customers equitably, because customers flagged more often bear more friction and more declines. Bias can enter through the data, as when labels reflect past investigation patterns, and through features that stand in for a protected characteristic without naming it, such as a postcode that tracks ethnicity. Testing for fairness means comparing alert, decline and error rates across groups, asking whether differences are explained by genuine risk, and acting where they are not. Equality and consumer-protection law may apply, and Module 7 sets out the frame.


## Scenario — THE POSTCODE THAT PREDICTS

Fieldgate Bank’s new card-fraud model includes a feature that groups customers by neighbourhood, based on historic confirmed fraud in each area. The model’s overall performance is strong. A fairness review by analyst Imogen Rhys finds that customers in two particular neighbourhoods are three times as likely to have a card declined, even though a sample review finds most of those declines were genuine purchases. The modelling lead argues that the feature is predictive and the results only reflect real risk. No one has yet compared error rates between neighbourhoods. **Question.** What should the review conclude? **Answer.** The strong overall performance does not answer the fairness question.

The feature carries past investigation patterns: areas where more fraud was confirmed in the past, perhaps because more attention was paid to them, are scored as riskier, and so customers who live there face more declines of genuine purchases. If the neighbourhoods are also linked to a protected characteristic, the feature may act as a proxy for it. The sample review, which found mostly genuine declines, suggests the feature is producing errors that fall on certain customers. The sound response is to test the model with and without the feature, compare error rates by group, and consider replacing the area-based feature with behavioural features that describe the customer’s own activity. The decision and its reasons should be documented, and escalated through the model governance route.

Predictive power alone is not a defence when the cost of errors is unevenly shared.


**5.6.4** Data quality and change control A model is only as good as its data. Data quality has several dimensions: completeness (are fields missing?), accuracy (are they right?), timeliness (do they arrive in time for the decision?), consistency (does a field mean the same in each system?) and lineage (can you trace a value back to its source?). Data checks at the point of input, with alerts for unusual shifts, are part of model monitoring. Change control applies to every part of the detection system: rules, models, features, thresholds and data feeds. Each change has a documented reason, an owner, testing, independent approval, a version number and a rollback plan. Models should be released first in a shadow mode where they score but do not act, then in a limited roll-out. Chapter 5.4 showed what can happen when this discipline is skipped.


**5.6.5** Human-in-the-loop A human in the loop means that a person reviews or can override a machine’s output before it affects a customer. In fraud work this is the normal pattern for higher-impact actions such as closing an account, filing a report or accusing a customer. For lower-impact actions at high volume, such as scoring a card payment in milliseconds, a human cannot review each decision, and the human role moves to designing, monitoring and challenging the system. Human review has its own risks. People can defer too readily to the machine, a habit called automation bias, and sign off on scores they do not understand. Reviewers who see only the 120 recommendation, and not the evidence, cannot form an independent judgement, and overrides need monitoring: if analysts never overturn a model’s alerts, the review may be a formality.


## Scenario — THE FEED THAT WENT QUIET

Marlow Card Services runs a machine-learning fraud model that depends partly on a feed of device data from a third-party provider. After a supplier update, one field in that feed, indicating whether a device is new, starts arriving blank for many events. Nobody changes the model. Over the next three weeks the model’s alert rate falls steadily and the fraud team celebrates fewer alerts. The head of model validation, Kwame Asante, notices that fraud losses reported by customers are rising at the same time. **Question.** What has happened, and what should Kwame recommend? **Answer.** The silent change in the data feed has altered the model’s inputs. Because the new-device field is blank, the model cannot see a key signal, so it scores risky events as lower risk.

The fall in alerts is not good news; it reflects blindness, and rising customer-reported losses point the same way. This is a data-quality failure that model monitoring should have caught through input-stability checks. Kwame should recommend restoring the field with the supplier, or reverting to a state in which the model can run correctly, at once. A review of events since the change should follow, to find frauds that slipped through.


## What to take from this chapter
- US model risk guidance is supervisory, not statute, expects an inventory, validation, monitoring and ownership scaled to materiality, and treats generative and agentic AI as outside its scope
- An exclusion from model guidance is not an exclusion from risk management, and the wider legal frame is in Module
- Validation covers conceptual soundness, outcomes analysis and ongoing monitoring, and it should be independent of the model builders
- Explainability, the ability to say why a model produced a score, matters for analysts, customers and auditors, and a model whose reasons cannot be given plainly is hard to govern
- Fairness testing compares alert, decline and error rates across groups, and predictive power does not excuse errors that fall unevenly
- Data quality failures can change scores without anyone touching the model, so inputs need monitoring
