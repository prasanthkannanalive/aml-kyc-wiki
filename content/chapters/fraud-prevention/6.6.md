# Learning from cases

A closed case is also a free lesson, and institutions that skip the lesson pay for the same loss again. Learning means asking what allowed the fraud, which control should have caught it, and what will change. This chapter covers post-incident review, root cause, feeding findings back into controls, and keeping a lessons-learned record. Review method is practitioner method and differs between firms. It ends with a case study that draws the module together.


**6.6.1** The post-incident review A post-incident review is a structured meeting and write-up held after a significant case, a run of similar cases, or a near miss. Its purpose is to understand how the event happened and what to change. It is not a disciplinary hearing, and people will not speak candidly if it feels like one. Many teams call the approach blameless: it looks first at systems and decisions, not fault. Blameless does not mean unaccountable: misconduct or serious negligence goes to the proper process separately. Hold the review soon enough that memories and logs are fresh, but not while the case is live. Invite the people who worked the case, the control owners, and someone from outside who can ask naive questions, and prepare the timeline beforehand. It should answer five questions: what happened, in sequence; what the institution saw, and when; what it missed or misread; what it did well; and what should change. Set criteria in advance for which cases get a full review, such as a large loss, a new scheme, a failed control or a vulnerable victim; smaller cases can be reviewed in batches by type.


**6.6.2** Root cause and contributing factors A root cause is the underlying reason an event was possible, as opposed to the event itself or its immediate trigger. The immediate trigger of an account takeover may be a phished password. The root cause may be that the institution allowed a payee to be added and a large payment to go out within minutes of a device change with no friction. The institution cannot fix the customer’s inbox, but it can fix the root cause. A simple technique is to ask “why” repeatedly until the answer is something the institution can change: a control, a process, a decision or a resourcing choice. Also look for contributing factors, which made the event likelier or worse without being the main cause: understaffing at the weekend, a rule that was switched off, or a hand-off that lost information. Watch for two traps. The first is human error as an endpoint. “The agent did not follow the procedure” is a finding, not a root cause; ask why it was easy to miss, why the procedure was unclear, or why the agent was under pressure. The second is hindsight bias. Events look obvious afterwards, so judge decisions by what was known at the time. Findings fall into categories that suggest actions: control design (change the rule or add a step-up check), operation (fix resourcing or queues), process (redesign the hand-off), information (share data), people (update training) and external (add the new scheme to the risk assessment).


**6.6.3** Control feedback and the lessons-learned record Findings only matter if they reach the controls. A new scheme goes into the fraud risk assessment (Chapter 2.2). A weak control gets redesigned and its owner named (Chapter 2.3). A missed 138 pattern may need a new rule or model change, tested and tuned before release (Chapters 5.2 to 5.4). A customer-handling failure goes back into scripts and training (Chapter 6.3). Each action needs an owner, a date and a way to confirm it was done, and a later check on whether it worked. The lessons-learned record is the written trail: case reference and type; summary; timeline; what worked; root cause and contributing factors; actions, with owner, date and status; and any wider message. Keep it free of unneeded personal detail, restrict access (ACFE Code, III.F.1), and make it searchable by scheme and control, because patterns across records are the real prize. Review the record periodically for overdue actions and recurring lessons: recurrence is a sign that an action was too weak or never happened. Report the themes upwards (Chapter 2.4).


**6.6.4** Case study: The Quiet Friday Payments Composite case, fictional. Larchmont Bank is a mid-sized retail bank. Its customer Mrs Ngozi Adeyemi, a retired teacher, banks mainly online. Over three weeks she receives calls and texts from someone claiming to be from the bank’s fraud team. The caller says her account is under attack and that she must move her savings to a “safe account” and read out codes to confirm. On a Friday evening she makes three payments to a newly added payee. What the bank saw. The first payment triggered a payee-confirmation warning, which Mrs Adeyemi clicked past on the caller’s instruction. The rule engine scored each payment as medium risk because each was below the usual alert level for her segment, although together they were far out of pattern. The alerts were queued for Monday because the weekend team worked only high-risk items. What was missed. Four things. The queue ranked by single payment size, not by the sequence. The weekend team had no authority to hold medium-risk alerts. The payee warning was generic and did not mention the scam being run. And a note on her profile recorded that she had called the branch twice the week before, confused about a text message; nobody had linked it to the alert. What happened. On Monday the analyst, Kenji Morales, saw the three payments, called Mrs Adeyemi on a verified number and learned of the scam. He opened a case, sent recall requests and an alert to the receiving institution, and reported to the police. Part of the funds had been moved on; part had been frozen by the receiving institution. Kenji told Mrs Adeyemi truthfully that recovery was partial and uncertain, arranged a follow-up call, and with her consent noted her vulnerability on her profile. Her claim was assessed under the reimbursement rules that applied to her payments (Chapter 7.1). What it cost. The bank bore a loss on the unrecovered amount, the investigation and the complaint. Mrs Adeyemi told the review she had felt foolish; Kenji’s plain, unhurried tone was, in her words, the reason she answered the phone at all. For a time she stopped using online banking. The review. Larchmont held a blameless post-incident review within three weeks, inviting the receiving institution’s contact to describe what its side had seen. It asked honestly whether the Friday-evening staff could have held the third payment, and concluded that only someone with authority who could see the sequence could have done so. The root cause was in control design: a series of payments to a new payee, each under the alert level, was not treated as one event. Contributing factors were the weekend authority gap, the generic warning and the unlinked 139 notes. It also recorded what worked: Kenji’s quick call, the same-day alert and the careful conversation. Actions followed. The rule team added a sequence rule, tested and tuned under change control (Chapters 5.4 and 5.6). The weekend team received authority to place short holds on defined alert types, with mandatory review. The payee warning was rewritten to name the safe-account scam, branch and fraud notes were linked in the case view, and training used the case with names removed. Second look. Larchmont also checked its other weekend alerts for the previous quarter and found two earlier customers with the same pattern, both closed on Monday with part of the money gone. Those cases were reopened and the customers contacted. The follow-up showed that the failure was a feature of the design, not an accident of one weekend, and gave the review its strongest argument for funding the changes. Lessons. A control that judges each payment alone will miss a scheme built from many small ones. Authority has to match the hours when the fraud happens. Warnings work better when they describe the scheme the customer is living through. And the best review records what went right as well as what went wrong.


## Scenario — THE ACTION THAT WAS NEVER OWNED

After a similar post-incident review at Delmar Credit, a working group agrees three actions. The minutes read: “Consider improving weekend cover; rewrite the payee warning; look at linking notes.” No owners or dates are recorded, because the chair says the team knows what to do. Four months later a similar case occurs on a Saturday; the analyst escalated as required, but the duty manager also had no authority to hold, and there was no on-call route to anyone who did. The new review finds that none of the three actions has been done and that nobody remembers who was meant to act. The chair says the root cause was the weekend analyst. **Question.** What is wrong with the way the lessons were recorded, and with the chair’s conclusion?

**Answer.** The actions were vague and had no owner, date or check, so nothing happened. The chair’s conclusion ends the analysis at a person, which is the human-error trap. The analyst did escalate. The root cause is that nobody on duty at the weekend had the authority or route to place a hold, a known gap from the first review. Recurrence is a sign that the earlier action was too weak or never took place, and the lessons-learned record should have made that visible. The sound response is to record each action with a named owner, due date, definition of done and follow-up check, and to track overdue items to the risk committee. The new review should treat the earlier failure to deliver as a contributing factor.


## Scenario — HINDSIGHT AT THE REVIEW TABLE

At Delmar Credit, a post-incident review looks at a mule-network case that ran for six weeks before detection. A senior manager says the signs were obvious from the first week and proposes naming the two analysts who closed the early alerts as false positives. An analyst points out that the rules had been tuned shortly before to cut false positives, and that the early alerts carried none of the context that later became clear. Both analysts followed the procedure as written. **Question.** How should the review handle the proposal? **Answer.** It should decline to name individuals at this stage and judge the early decisions by what the analysts knew then. The manager is applying hindsight: the pattern is obvious once the whole story is known.

The tuning decision is a contributing factor in its own right, because tuning done for a sound reason reduced sensitivity to this pattern, and it belongs in the control findings. The review should ask what an analyst would have needed in week one, whether a linked-account view or aggregation rule would have supplied it, and what monitoring existed to detect lost sensitivity after tuning. Bad faith or ignoring a clear instruction goes to the proper process separately.


## What to take from this chapter
- A post-incident review looks at systems and decisions first; blameless does not mean unaccountable, and misconduct goes to the proper process
- A root cause is the underlying reason the event was possible, found by asking why until the answer is something the institution can change
- Human error is a finding to explain, not a root cause, and hindsight makes past decisions look clearer than they were
- Findings must reach controls, the risk assessment, rules and training, each with an owner, a date and a later check
