Preventive and detective controls The third principle asks the organisation to select, develop and deploy preventive and detective fraud control activities. A control is anything that reduces the likelihood or the impact of a scheme. This chapter classifies controls by when they act, explains the structural controls that every financial institution relies on, and deals with the hardest design problem in the field: every control that stops a criminal also asks something of an honest customer. 2.3.1  Preventive, detective and corrective controls Controls are classified by the moment at which they act on a scheme. A preventive control stops a fraudulent act from succeeding, before the loss. Examples are two approvals on a large payment, a check that the payee name matches the account, a limit on a new payee’s first payment and a block on a payment to a known mule account. A detective control finds fraud that has happened or is under way. Examples are transaction monitoring, reconciliation of accounts that shows a difference, a review of staff access logs and a hotline. A corrective control limits the harm once fraud is found and prevents repetition: freezing an account, recalling a payment, restoring a customer’s access safely, closing the weakness the scheme used and recovering funds. But no preventive control is perfect, and criminals test them continually. Prevention is usually the cheapest way to deal with fraud, since a loss that never occurs has no investigation cost, no recovery effort and no customer harm. Detection reduces how long a successful scheme runs and how much it takes. A programme that relies on prevention alone has nothing when a control fails, and a programme that relies on detection alone accepts every loss as a cost of doing business. 2.3.2  Structural controls: segregation, authority and verification Some controls do not look at fraud at all. They are structures that make fraud harder because they spread power and add checks. Segregation of duties means that no one person controls all stages of a transaction that could be abused: authorising it, recording it, handling the asset and reconciling the result. If the person who creates a supplier also approves payments to it and reconciles the account, they can pay a fictitious supplier and nobody will notice. If the roles are split, a fraud now needs collusion, which is harder, riskier and more likely to be discovered. Where staff numbers are too small to split duties fully, compensating controls such as independent review of exception reports are needed. Authorisation limits state who may approve what. Larger or riskier transactions require more senior approval, or two approvers. Limits must be set deliberately, enforced by the system rather than by trust, and reviewed, because limits create a visible line and stop matching how the business works. Chapter 5.2 explains how detection looks for activity clustered near a limit. Verification means confirming a fact independently before relying on it: calling back a supplier on a number already held when asked to change bank details, checking an identity document against a trusted source, or confirming an instruction through a separate channel. A claim made by the person asking for money is not enough. Independence is the key word: a callback to a number given in the suspicious message is not verification. Least privilege gives staff only the system rights their role needs. The usual weaknesses to test are shared logins, rights that built up over time, access left active during leave, approvals given without reading, and pressure to skip a check when a request is urgent. 37 2.3.3  Friction versus customer experience Every fraud control adds friction, which is any step that slows or burdens a legitimate customer: an extra password, a delay on a new payee, a question about the purpose of a payment, a declined card, a locked account. Friction has a price: abandoned applications, lost sales, calls to the contact centre, customers who move their money elsewhere, and customers who learn to click through warnings. So fraud control is a trade-off, not a quest for zero fraud. The aim is to place the right amount of friction at the right point, on the customers and payments where risk is highest, and keep the rest of the journey smooth. This is risk-based or proportionate control, and it needs a way to tell risk apart, using the customer’s history, the device, the payee, the amount and the pattern of the session. Three design ideas help. Apply friction where it will be noticed: a warning shown on every payment is ignored, while one shown only when the payment looks like a known scam, in words that describe what the customer is likely facing, is read. Make friction informative: a payment held briefly while the customer is told why gives them a chance to think, whereas a bare decline gives them nothing to act on. And measure both sides, since fraud loss is easy to count and the cost of friction, such as abandonment, false declines and complaints, is often ignored. SCENARIO — THE ONE-SIZE-FITS-ALL PAUSE After a rise in invoice-redirection scams, in which customers are sent altered bank details for a genuine bill, Larchmont Bank introduces a 24-hour hold on every first payment to a new payee, whatever the amount or the customer. Scam losses on new payees fall. Within two months, complaints rise sharply, the contact centre is overwhelmed with calls about delayed rent and supplier payments, and a group of small business customers moves its accounts to a competitor. Meanwhile the fraud team finds that scam losses where details were changed on an existing payee have not fallen at all, because the hold never applies to them. Question.  What does this show about the design of the control, and what would be a better approach? Answer.  The control is blunt. It applies the same friction to every customer, so the cost falls mostly on honest customers making routine payments, while it covers only one route to the same loss. It reduced one pattern of loss at a high cost in experience and retention, left the neighbouring pattern untouched, and the cost was not measured when the control was designed. A better approach is proportionate friction. Hold or question only payments where signals raise risk, such as a new payee combined with an unusual amount, a change of details on an existing payee, a recent change of device or a customer in a call session. Add informative warnings, offer an easy route to confirm genuine urgent payments and monitor both fraud loss and the effects of friction. 2.3.4  Choosing and combining controls Controls work best in layers, sometimes called defence in depth. No single control is reliable, so the aim is to place several, of different types, between the scheme and the loss. The layers should be genuinely independent. Two controls that rely on the same data feed or the same person are one control with two names. A bank defends against account takeover with a password, a one-time code sent to the registered phone, a device check and a behaviour score. The first two both depend on the customer’s phone and email being secure, so a criminal who controls the phone defeats both. The device check and the behaviour score act on different information. The layers that count are the ones that fail for different reasons. When choosing a control for a scheme, ask a short list of questions. — Does it act on the step of the scheme where the fraudster is most exposed or the institution has the best information? 38 — Is it preventive, detective or both, and what is the corrective step if it fails? — What does it cost, in money, staff time and customer friction? — Can it be tested, who will test it, and who owns it when the owner is absent? A model that is trusted without testing is a risk in itself, a point Chapter 5.6 develops. SCENARIO — TWO CONTROLS, ONE POINT OF FAILURE Delmar Credit protects its supplier payment run with two controls. First, a rule flags any change of supplier bank details for a manual call-back. Second, a monthly report lists all suppliers whose bank details changed during the month. Both controls draw on the same supplier master data table. In one month a supplier’s bank details are changed by someone with write access to the table, and neither control shows it: the rule does not fire and the monthly report lists no change. A large payment is made to the new account. The head of finance says the company had two independent controls. Question.  Were the controls independent, and how should the design be improved? Answer.  They were not. Both rely on the same master data table and its change history, so one successful manipulation defeated both. The institution had one control presented twice. Real independence needs a different source or a different person: for example, a report of bank detail changes taken from system audit logs that the data owner cannot edit, and a call-back to a contact number held separately from the master table. A sound practitioner also adds the missing structure around the table: restricted write access, dual approval for changes to bank details, and alerts on edits to the log itself.  WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — Preventive controls stop a loss before it occurs, detective controls find it while or after it happens, and corrective controls limit harm and prevent repetition. — A scheme defended by only one type of control is fragile; look for prevention, detection and a corrective step. — Segregation of duties, authorisation limits and independent verification are structural controls that spread power and reduce single points of trust. — Verification is only independent if the source of the information is not controlled by the person asking for money. — Every control adds friction, so apply it in proportion to risk and measure the cost to legitimate customers as well as the fraud it stops. — Layered controls must be genuinely independent; two controls fed by the same data or person are one control. 39
