The United States The United States has no single scam-reimbursement rule. It has rules for unauthorised electronic transfers and card use, supervisory guidance on authentication and model risk, a reporting rule that obliges banks to tell the government about suspicious activity, and a voluntary route for banks to share information. This chapter explains Regulation E and Regulation Z, why authorised-scam losses generally fall outside them, and how the reporting and sharing rules work in principle. 7.3.1  Regulation E: unauthorised electronic fund transfers Regulation E implements the Electronic Fund Transfer Act and covers consumer electronic fund transfers (EFTs), such as debit card payments and ACH debits. An unauthorised EFT is one initiated by someone other than the consumer, without actual authority, from which the consumer receives no benefit. The definition excludes a transfer by someone to whom the consumer gave the access device (unless the consumer had notified the institution) and a transfer by or with the consumer acting fraudulently. Consumer liability is tiered by how quickly the consumer reports, provided the required disclosures were given. A prompt report leaves the consumer with a small fixed exposure. A later report raises the exposure to a larger fixed limit at most, built from the small amount plus later transfers that the institution shows timely notice would have prevented. Extenuating circumstances extend the periods, and a lower state-law or contract limit prevails. The burden for the larger tier rests on the institution: if it cannot show, from timestamps, that earlier notice would have prevented the later transfers, it bears them. 7.3.2  Error resolution and credit card liability When a consumer reports an error, including an unauthorised transfer, Regulation E sets the institution’s process. The consumer must give notice within a stated period of the statement showing the error. The institution then has two routes: investigate and decide within a short period, report the result and correct any error promptly; or take longer, but only if it gives the consumer provisional credit, with interest, within the short period. Credit cards sit under Regulation Z. A cardholder’s liability for unauthorised use is capped at a low fixed amount, provided the card was accepted, the issuer gave adequate notice and the issuer provided a way to identify the cardholder. Chapter 6.4 covers the chargeback mechanics that follow a dispute. 7.3.3  Authorised scams, and authentication guidance A customer persuaded by a fraudster to send a payment initiates the transfer. Because an unauthorised transfer must be initiated by someone other than the consumer, a transfer the consumer initiated is on a plain reading outside Regulation E’s protections, even when the consumer was deceived. This is an interpretation, not an official statement, and the official commentary should be consulted. No general federal reimbursement rule for authorised scams has been identified in the sources used here, so outcomes are driven by firm policy, network rules and state law. At the boundary, reports say consumer-regulator guidance treats a transfer made by someone who obtained the credentials through fraud as unauthorised, whatever the consumer’s 149 negligence. This was reported, not read directly, and separates a fraudster who makes the transfer from a consumer who makes it at the fraudster’s request. The Federal Financial Institutions Examination Council (FFIEC) coordinates US bank supervisors. Its authentication guidance expects periodic risk assessments, multi-factor authentication or an equivalent for high-risk activity, and layered security. It is guidance, not a rule, and unlike PSD2 it is risk-based: an institution decides which activities are high risk and records why (Chapter 5.5). 7.3.4  The suspicious activity report rule A suspicious activity report (SAR) is filed with the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN). The bank rule sits within the Bank Secrecy Act regulations. A bank must file where a transaction conducted or attempted through the bank, above a reporting threshold, involves funds the bank knows, suspects or has reason to suspect come from illegal activity, are designed to evade Bank Secrecy Act requirements, or have no business or apparent lawful purpose. Five features matter more than the numbers. The clock runs from initial detection of facts that may constitute a basis for filing, not from the date a committee decides. Where no suspect is identified, a limited extra period is allowed, within an overall limit. In urgent cases, such as an ongoing scheme, the bank also contacts law enforcement directly. The SAR and supporting documents must be kept for a fixed period. And SARs are confidential, with a safe harbour protecting the filer from liability for reporting in good faith. A prudent bank documents its decisions whether or not it files, because the record is what an examiner will ask for. The thresholds, windows and retention period are in the current rule. SCENARIO — THE CLOCK STARTS AT DETECTION An analyst at Larchmont Bank reviews ACH credits into a customer account, well above the reporting threshold in total. The analyst records that the credits show clear signs of a payroll-diversion scheme and appear to be illegally derived. No suspect is identified. A senior investigator reviews the case weeks later, and a colleague formally decides a SAR is warranted later still. The team lead says the clock began on the date of that formal decision. Question.  When did the filing clock start, and what follows from the team lead’s view? Answer.  The clock runs from initial detection of facts that may constitute a basis for filing, not from the decision date, so it started when the analyst recorded the facts. With no suspect identified, the bank may take a limited extra period, but within an overall limit running from initial detection. Measuring from the formal decision would make the filing late. Record the date of initial detection in the case record, and do not let cases wait unobserved. Where activity is ongoing and urgent, also contact law enforcement. 7.3.5  Model-risk guidance Fraud teams rely on scoring models to decide which payments to stop (Chapter 5.3). Model risk management controls the harm a model can do when it is wrong, misused or left unmonitored, and US supervisors express their expectations in guidance, not a rule. It is nonbinding, and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) says non-compliance will not by itself draw supervisory criticism. It is most relevant to larger banks, and generative and agentic AI are excluded as novel. As reported, deterministic rule-based processes are carved 150 out of the model definition, and validation keeps its three components: conceptual soundness, outcomes analysis and ongoing monitoring. Nonbinding does not mean optional in practice: a bank must still show its fraud decisions rest on tools that work. Fixed fraud rules may fall outside the model definition and machine-learning scores likely inside it, to be verified against the text, while generative tools sit outside this guidance but not outside general risk management (Chapter 5.6). 7.3.6  Information sharing between institutions Fraud rarely stays inside one bank: the money moves to another institution. The USA PATRIOT Act sets up a voluntary route for financial institutions to share information with each other, with a safe harbour for sharing within its terms (Chapter 6.5). What follows is as reported by a law-firm summary, so the primary text and the registration mechanics should be checked. As reported, suspicion of fraud can support sharing, because fraud offences are treated as specified unlawful activity. The other institution need not hold the customer’s account. The SAR itself can never be shared. The line to hold is between facts and the report. The facts about a beneficiary account, the payments and the suspected scheme are what the route is for. The existence and content of a SAR are confidential. An investigator who keeps that line can ask for help without breaching confidentiality. SCENARIO — THE FRAUD-ONLY REQUEST Noor Ahmed, an investigator at Cedar Bank, has a customer who sent a large sum to an account at Birchwood Bank after a romance-scam approach. Cedar suspects fraud only. Noor wants to ask Birchwood about the beneficiary account. Cedar’s compliance officer says the sharing route covers only money laundering, that Birchwood’s customer is not Cedar’s customer, so the route is unavailable, and that Noor should instead send Birchwood a copy of Cedar’s SAR. Both banks are assumed to be registered participants. Question.  Is the compliance officer right? Answer.  No on both points. As reported, suspicion of fraud can support sharing within the safe harbour, and no link to the receiving institution’s customer is needed. Noor can ask Birchwood about the beneficiary account and share the facts she holds about the payment and the scheme. The SAR must not be sent. SARs cannot be shared and the rule makes them confidential, so Noor shares the underlying facts and leaves the report out. Because the summary is secondary, she should also check the current fact sheet and both banks’ registration. 151 WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — Regulation E covers transfers initiated by someone other than the consumer without authority, and liability steps up with lateness only for later transfers the institution can show timely notice would have prevented. — Under Regulation E the institution either investigates within a short period or gives provisional credit and takes longer, and credit cards under Regulation Z carry a low fixed cap on cardholder liability for unauthorised use. — Authorised push-payment scam losses are generally outside Regulation E on a plain reading of the definition, and no general federal reimbursement rule was found. — The SAR rule is triggered by knowledge or suspicion of illegal funds, evasion or no lawful purpose, runs its clock from initial detection, and makes the SAR confidential. — Model risk guidance is nonbinding and aimed at larger banks, but a bank must still show its fraud tools work. — The voluntary information-sharing route allows facts about suspected fraud to be shared but never the SAR itself. 152
