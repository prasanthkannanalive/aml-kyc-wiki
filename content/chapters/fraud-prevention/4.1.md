Occupational fraud and how it is classified Occupational fraud is fraud committed by a person against the organisation that employs or engages them. A shared classification matters because it gives fraud teams, auditors, investigators and boards one vocabulary. When a case is described as a billing scheme or a kickback, everyone knows roughly how it ran, what it left behind in the records and which controls should have caught it. This chapter teaches the classification published by the ACFE, an international anti-fraud professional body, its three main branches and their sub-schemes, and the profile of the people who commit these frauds. 4.1.1  Occupational fraud and the fraud tree Occupational fraud means the use of one’s occupation for personal enrichment through the deliberate misuse or misapplication of the employing organisation’s resources or assets. The key words are “against the employer”. The victim is the organisation, not a customer. A teller who steals cash from a drawer commits occupational fraud. A teller who helps a customer defraud a third party is doing something different, and Chapter 4.3 deals with that. The ACFE organises occupational fraud into a classification often called the fraud tree. It is the ACFE’s framework, published and refreshed through its research reports, and this guide uses it as the ACFE’s framework. The tree has three main branches. Asset misappropriation is the theft or misuse of the organisation’s assets. Corruption is the misuse of influence in a business transaction for personal gain, against the employer’s interest. Financial statement fraud is the deliberate misstatement of the organisation’s financial reports. Each branch splits into categories and sub-schemes. The tree is a map, not a law. Real cases often touch more than one branch: a manager who takes bribes from a supplier (corruption) may also approve inflated invoices (a billing scheme, which is asset misappropriation) and then bury the extra cost in the accounts (financial statement fraud). The ACFE counts a case under every branch it involves. 4.1.2  Asset misappropriation and its sub-schemes Asset misappropriation is the branch where an employee steals or misuses what the organisation owns. It is the branch every organisation meets most often, because it needs no accomplice and no complicated paperwork. It splits into cash schemes and non-cash schemes. Cash schemes split again. Theft of cash on hand is taking money from a till, a vault or a petty cash box. Skimming is taking money before it is recorded, so that the books never show it, for example pocketing a payment received from a customer and not posting it. Cash larceny is taking money after it has been recorded, which leaves a gap that reconciliation can find. Fraudulent disbursements are schemes where the organisation itself is made to pay out: billing schemes, expense reimbursement schemes, payroll schemes, cheque and payment tampering, and register disbursements such as false refunds or voids. It includes conflicts of interest, bribery, illegal gratuities and economic extortion. The employee may own part of a supplier, or employ a relative who benefits from a decision. Bribery is the offering, giving, receiving or soliciting of something of value to influence a decision. In the workplace it commonly takes the form of a kickback: a supplier overcharges the employer and returns part of the overcharge to the employee who approved the contract. Bid rigging is related: the employee helps a chosen supplier win a tender. An illegal gratuity is a reward given after a decision, without a prior agreement. Economic extortion is the reverse of a bribe: the employee demands payment for a decision that should have been made on merit. Corruption is harder to detect than asset misappropriation for one reason: nothing goes missing. The books balance. The employer received something, and the invoice was paid, so a reconciliation shows nothing wrong. Kestrel’s prices are well above the market rate. A review also finds that Rohan has approved invoices from Kestrel for jobs that were never delivered. The invoices were paid from the marketing budget. Nothing is missing from any bank account; every payment was authorised in the ordinary way and every reconciliation balances. The internal audit team asks how the case should be classified in its report. Question.  Under the ACFE classification, which branches of occupational fraud does the case involve? Answer.  It involves two branches. The undisclosed ownership link and the inflated prices are corruption: a conflict of interest, and probably a kickback or at least a favoured supplier whose benefit passes to the manager’s family. The invoices for undelivered work are a billing scheme, which sits under fraudulent disbursements and therefore under asset misappropriation. The ACFE counts a case under each branch it touches, so the report should record both. The tempting conclusion is that there is no fraud because “the books balance and every payment was approved”. That confuses an accurate ledger with an honest transaction. Reconciliation proves that money left the account as recorded, not that the goods existed or the price was fair. A sound analyst would look for proof of delivery on each invoice, compare Kestrel’s prices with other suppliers, and ask who disclosed the family tie and who should have been told. 4.1.4  Financial statement fraud Financial statement fraud is the deliberate misstatement or omission of material information in an organisation’s financial reports, to mislead those who rely on them. The ACFE divides it into overstating and understating. Overstatement inflates assets or revenue, or hides liabilities and expenses, to make the organisation look healthier. Understatement does the opposite, perhaps to smooth profits between years or to hide the true size of a fund. This branch is the least frequent in the ACFE’s case data and the most costly per case, and the reason is straightforward. It is usually committed by senior people, who can override controls and direct junior staff, and it can run for years because those who would normally check the numbers report to the people who set them. For a fraud prevention analyst, the point is not to audit the financial statements. External and internal audit do that. The point is to recognise the edges of this branch when a fraud team sees them: unusual manual journal entries, pressure on staff to hit a number, or an instruction to delay writing off a bad account. Those observations should go to the right owner, usually internal audit or the audit committee, without waiting for proof. Management override of controls is especially dangerous here, which is why the independence of the people who receive such reports matters (Chapter 4.5). 4.1.5  Who commits occupational fraud, and the warning signs The ACFE’s research shows a consistent picture, and its main points can be stated without numbers. Perpetrators are overwhelmingly people with no criminal record. They are not usually the colleague the firm would have predicted. The typical fraudster is a trusted employee who knows the process well enough to see the gap. Three patterns repeat. First, losses rise with seniority: owners, executives and managers cause larger losses than ordinary employees, because they hold more authority and can override controls. Second, losses are higher where tenure is longer, which fits the view that trust grows 82 and checking falls, though the data shows the link and not the cause. Third, collusion makes schemes larger, because two or more people can defeat the segregation of duties that stops one. The ACFE also reports that most perpetrators showed at least one behavioural red flag before they were caught. Flags tied to the largest losses include excessive internal pressure, an unusually close relationship with a vendor or customer, a refusal to take leave, and a “wheeler-dealer” attitude. These are warning signs, not proof. Most people under financial pressure, or who like to keep control of their work, are honest. The flag is a reason to look at the records, not to accuse. The data comes from fraud against employers: it is not a measure of fraud by outsiders against banks or consumers. SCENARIO — THE COLLEAGUE WHO NEVER TAKES LEAVE At Westmere Payments, a fictional payments processor, Grace Ellery has been in settlements operations for eleven years. She trained most of the current team, and colleagues go to her when a payout does not reconcile. Her manager has noticed that she insists on handling month-end reconciliations of the merchant payout account herself and has declined offers of help. The manager mentions this to the fraud team as “a bit odd but nothing wrong”. Grace has no disciplinary history and her reconciliations have always balanced. Question.  How should the fraud team treat this information? Answer.  As a risk indicator that justifies a quiet, proportionate check, not as an accusation. Refusing to take leave and insisting on sole control of a process are both behaviours the ACFE reports in cases that were later discovered. Long tenure and sole control of a reconciliation also create the conditions in which a scheme can run unobserved. The fact that the reconciliation always balances is not comfort: a person who controls the reconciliation controls what it shows. The sound response is to treat it as a control question first. Arrange for another person to perform or review the reconciliation, and require continuous leave for the role, as would apply to any role with this level of control. If the process is changed for everyone in the role, no individual is singled out. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — Occupational fraud is committed against the employer, and the ACFE classifies it into asset misappropriation, corruption and financial statement fraud. — Asset misappropriation splits into cash schemes (skimming, larceny, fraudulent disbursements) and non-cash schemes, including the theft of information. — Corruption covers conflicts of interest, bribery, illegal gratuities and economic extortion, and it is hard to detect because the books still balance. — Financial statement fraud is the least common branch and usually involves senior people who can override controls. — A single case often touches more than one branch, and the ACFE counts it under each. — Perpetrators are usually trusted first-time offenders, and warning signs such as refusing leave or a close vendor tie justify a check of the records, not an accusation. — The ACFE data describes fraud against employers; it is not a measure of external fraud against banks. 83
