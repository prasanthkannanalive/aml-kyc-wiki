Almost every regime treats an unauthorised payment more generously than an authorised one. Second, who and what is covered? The UK scheme covers individuals, microenterprises and charities over two named systems, Singapore a defined phishing scam, Regulation E consumer EFTs. Third, who carries the burden of proof? In the UK and under PSD2 the PSP carries it on gross negligence, under the Indian circular the bank, and under Regulation E the institution must show timely notice would have prevented later transfers. Fourth, what are the clocks? Record each clock and its trigger, not only its length. Fifth, who else shares the loss, and what duty triggers it: a receiving bank, a telecoms operator or a platform? SCENARIO — ONE CASE, THREE JURISDICTIONS A group bank operates in the UK, a euro-area member state and the US. Analyst Imogen Park writes a global procedure for “customer reports a scam”. Her draft says: one standard reimbursement period and the optional excess in all cases, with one workflow. UK colleagues object that the excess would hit vulnerable customers. US and euro-area colleagues object that clocks and legal tests differ by jurisdiction, and that some of the cases covered are unauthorised payments, not scams. Question.  Name two things wrong with the draft and the correct structure. Answer.  Branch first on authorisation. The decision clock and optional excess belong to UK authorised scams on covered systems. For euro-area unauthorised cases the rule is an immediate refund, refund first and recover later. US unauthorised EFTs follow Regulation E, while a US authorised scam is generally outside it, so firm policy and counsel decide. The draft’s errors are one clock for all cases and an excess in all cases: the excess is optional and never applies to a vulnerable consumer. A single intake with one common first question and jurisdiction-specific branches is clearer, each rule confirmed by Legal. 7.5.3  Status words, source quality and stale rules In force means the rule applies now. Agreed means the Parliament and the Council have reached a provisional agreement, but the process is not complete and it is not law. Proposed means a draft is on the table. Not adopted means a vote or final step has not happened. Status to be verified means the sources conflicted or could not be read. Do not build a procedure or a customer statement on a proposed rule. A primary source is the official text or the regulator’s own publication. A secondary source is a law-firm note, news report or vendor blog: useful for orientation, but it can simplify and lag, so the text says “as reported”. Then check for supersession. An older page on a regulator’s own site may state a rule that a later policy statement has changed: it is official and still wrong. Find the date on the page, look for a later decision, and log the older page as superseded. Keep a source log of rule, primary reference, date read, checker and next review. 156 SCENARIO — WHICH STATUS WORD? A newsletter reports: “The EU has agreed new rules requiring banks to refund spoofing victims and requiring online platforms to compensate banks.” Alicia Gomez, a policy analyst at Delmar Credit, wants to add a line to the firm’s regulatory horizon report. Her only sources are the newsletter, a law-firm blog and a recital of the Council text; she has found no operative article and no notice of adoption, and the committee reads the report each quarter. Question.  How should Alicia describe the status and the evidence? Answer.  As proposed, not adopted, with its exact stage to be verified. The sources are secondary, the recital creates no duty, and the agreed text could still change. She may say political agreement was reported and the recitals point toward a spoofing refund and platform responsibility, but that operative articles, scope and conditions have not been located. The report should record each source, its type and when it was read, and set a next check against the official legislative tracking pages. Writing “the EU requires” would turn a draft into law. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — The comparison tables show where the loss falls in each regime in principle, and every row carries a source tag of primary, secondary or proposed. — Five questions unlock any regime: was the payment authorised, who and what is covered, who carries the burden of proof, what are the clocks, and who else shares the loss. — In force, agreed, proposed, not adopted and status to be verified are different statuses, and a proposed rule must not be used to build a procedure or a customer statement. — Secondary sources and recital-only reading are weaker than the authentic text, and a source log with a review date turns research into a maintained control. 157
