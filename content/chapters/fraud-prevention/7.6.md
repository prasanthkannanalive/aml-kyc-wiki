AI, deepfakes and emerging fraud Generative AI can produce highly realistic images, audio and video. That changes what a fraud team can trust when it sees or hears something, but it does not change the underlying schemes: impersonation, false identity, payment diversion and social engineering. This chapter sets out what FinCEN, the UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC), FS-ISAC and Europol have said, how NIST digital identity guidance bears on authentication, and what follows for verification. 7.6.1  The FinCEN deepfake alert FinCEN, the US financial crimes authority, issued an alert on fraud schemes involving deepfake media made with generative AI. A deepfake is synthetic or altered media, such as an image, audio or video, that appears real. It gives filers a dedicated key term for the SAR narrative, used with the underlying typology term, so that analysts can find and count the filings. It is an indexing aid and does not change the reporting test. The red flags, paraphrased: a photo or identity document looks altered, matches a gallery of AI-generated faces, or is flagged by detection software. In a live check the customer uses a third-party webcam plugin or switches channels, or refuses multi-factor authentication. The device or location does not match. Activity is coordinated across similar accounts. A new account with little history quickly sends large payments to higher-risk payees, and funds leave fast through hard-to-reverse channels. The alert states that no single flag is conclusive; the value lies in combination. Its mitigations include phishing-resistant authentication, live verification, reverse-image search, metadata review, detection software and enhanced due diligence. 7.6.2  What the NCSC, FS-ISAC and Europol say These are assessments, not reporting instructions. The NCSC has assessed that threat actors already use AI to improve existing tactics, including social engineering, and expects it to make intrusion more effective over time. FS-ISAC, the financial-sector information-sharing body, describes deepfake impersonation of executives, employees and customers, and recommends strengthening existing controls, training, and a taxonomy to prioritise threats. Europol’s threat assessment, known here only through a secondary report, describes fraud as increasingly driven by AI-powered social engineering. The schemes are familiar, and none of these sources says existing controls should be replaced. Easier synthetic media puts more weight on controls that do not rely on a person judging a face or voice. 7.6.3  NIST digital identity guidelines and authentication The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) publishes digital identity guidelines covering identity proofing, authentication and federation. The current edition adds fraud requirements for identity proofing, including controls against injection attacks and forged media such as deepfakes and syncable authenticators such as passkeys. The authentication volume defines three authenticator assurance levels. The lowest allows a single factor with no phishing resistance required. The middle requires two factors and must offer 158 a phishing-resistant option. The highest requires a cryptographic authenticator with a non-exportable key and phishing resistance. Biometrics may be used only as one factor alongside a physical authenticator, and a non-biometric alternative must be offered. Presentation attack detection is required for face, and voice comparison is prohibited. Two lessons follow. A phishing-resistant authenticator defeats the attack in which a customer types codes into a fake page, as in Chapter 7.2, because it will not respond to a fake site. And the voice prohibition matters wherever a call centre treats a recognised voice as proof of identity. The design principle is to build verification around things media cannot forge and keep human judgment of faces and voices out of the critical path: confirm requests on a second channel using contact details from the organisation’s own records (Chapter 3.6), test documents and live checks with tools suited to forged media (Chapter 3.5), treat the vendors that supply them as a risk (Chapter 4.4), and give staff a rule they can follow under pressure, such as “any request to change payment details or send an urgent payment is confirmed by call-back to a known number, whoever appears to be asking”. SCENARIO — THE CHIEF’S VOICE The finance team at Brightwater Payments receives a voice message that sounds like its chief executive, Rafael Ortiz, asking for an urgent transfer to a new supplier account. A follow-up video call on an unfamiliar channel shows a slightly soft face that looks like Rafael. The treasury officer, Hana Sato, is told “I know his voice”. Procedure requires call-back to a known number for any new payee above a set amount, and this is above it. Question.  What should Hana do, and how far do the FinCEN red flags help? Answer.  Hana should follow the call-back procedure and not treat a recognised voice or face as proof. The alert’s red flags, such as a change of channel, are written for customer verification, so here they are only an analogy, and none is conclusive alone. An urgent request, a new payee and an unusual channel combine here, and the procedure exists so that a decision does not rest on whether media looks real. She should call Rafael on a number in the company’s own records, hold the payment until he confirms, and report the attempt. The tempting error is to trust her own judgment of the voice; controls should mean an employee never has to win that contest. SCENARIO — THE SELFIE THAT PASSED Larchmont Bank opens accounts remotely using a selfie video and a photographed identity document. Over one weekend it opens a cluster of accounts. Each has little history, receives a first payment within hours, and sends most of it onward to a small set of higher-risk payees, followed by quick withdrawals. Analyst Joel Reyes notes glitches in two selfie videos and an unusual shared device configuration. His manager says every selfie passed the vendor’s liveness check, so the accounts are legitimate. Question.  How should Joel assess the cluster? Answer.  Treat it as a suspected mule or synthetic-identity cluster, with deepfake use possible, and escalate. No single flag is conclusive, but the combination matches the alert: new low-history accounts, rapid payments to higher-risk payees, quick withdrawals, coordinated activity, device similarity and possible media anomalies. A vendor pass is one input and does not outweigh the pattern, and live checks can themselves be attacked. Joel should restrict the accounts under the bank’s procedures, preserve videos, device and payment records, review the vendor check, and consider a SAR with the deepfake key term where US rules apply.
