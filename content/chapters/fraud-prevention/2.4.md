# Monitoring, metrics and improvement

Controls decay. Products change, criminals adapt, staff turn over and systems are updated. The fourth and fifth principles of the guide deal with this: communicate what is found, and monitor whether the programme is present and functioning. This chapter explains what to measure, how to report it honestly, and how findings become changes that make the next loss less likely. It ends with a case in which one control drifted away from its design for months while every report looked good.


**2.4.1** What to measure Metrics let a programme see itself. A useful set covers four questions: how much fraud is there, how well are controls catching it, what does control cost customers and how quickly does the organisation respond. A firm that tracks only gross monthly loss cannot tell whether a rise means more attempts got through, a failed rule, or one large case; with attempts stopped, loss by scheme and alert quality alongside, it can. Question Example measures What to watch for How much fraud is there? Gross loss, net loss, attempts stopped, cases by scheme, loss against appetite Totals that hide a rise in one scheme How well do controls catch it? Detection rate for known cases, share of loss found by each control, time to detection, alert quality Counting only what was caught; unseen fraud is missing from the data What does control cost customers? False positive ratio, false declines, abandoned applications, complaints Treating low fraud as success when friction has risen sharply How fast is the response? Time to review an alert, to freeze or recall funds, to close a case Averages that hide a long tail of very slow cases A balanced set of internal fraud metrics. Three habits keep measurement honest. Define each metric in writing, including what counts as fraud, as an attempt, as a recovery and as a customer-caused loss, and keep the definition steady. Show trends against the volume of activity behind them, since loss as a share of transaction value is more useful than loss alone in a growing business. And report the cost of friction alongside the loss, so no one celebrates a fall in fraud that was bought with lost customers. Key risk indicators are early warnings that conditions are changing before losses show up, such as a rise in new accounts from one device, more overrides of a control, more customer reports of a scam type or a growing alert backlog. Loss is a lagging measure: by the time it rises, the scheme has been running for some time.


**2.4.2** Reporting, escalation and monitoring the programme The fourth principle asks for a fraud reporting process and a coordinated approach to investigation and corrective action. Upward, a good report to the board is short, compares results with appetite, shows trends, names the largest residual risks, lists significant incidents with lessons and says plainly what decisions are needed. The process should define what is escalated and to whom: a loss above a set level, suspected fraud by a senior person, a pattern across customers, a control failure, a regulatory reporting duty. A junior analyst who finds refunds approved by the same senior manager should know in advance that this goes straight to a named independent route, not to the manager’s line; without that rule the analyst may decide to say nothing. The fifth principle asks the organisation to evaluate whether the principles are present and functioning, and to communicate deficiencies. Evaluations are ongoing, built into daily work through manager checks and control owner testing, and separate, meaning periodic and more independent, such as internal audit and external review. High residual risk deserves more of the latter. Each deficiency is tracked to closure with an owner and a date. Monitoring also has to deal with drift, the slow movement away from the designed state. Rules are switched off during an upgrade and never switched on. A manual check is skipped when the team is short. A threshold is raised to cut alert volume and never reviewed. Drift is rarely deliberate, which makes it hard to see without looking for it.


## Scenario — THE DASHBOARD THAT ONLY SHOWED LOSS

Fernlea Payments reports fraud to its board every quarter using a single chart of net fraud loss. For three quarters the chart has been falling, and the board congratulates the fraud team. The fraud lead, Tomas Reyes, privately knows that the false decline rate on genuine card payments has doubled since the team raised a rule threshold, that complaints about declined payments are rising and that a recently added monitoring feed has been failing intermittently, so some payments are not scored at all. None of this appears in the report. **Question.** What is wrong with the reporting, and what should Tomas do? **Answer.** The report presents one side of the picture.

Falling net loss may reflect good control, but it may equally reflect unscored payments that were never recognised as fraud, or a tighter rule whose cost has been shifted onto genuine customers. A balanced set of metrics covers loss, control effectiveness, customer cost and response speed, and reports data-quality problems directly. Tomas should add the missing measures to the next report, describe the feed failure and its estimated exposure, propose a fix with an owner and a date, and explain that the fall in loss cannot be taken at face value until the feed is repaired.


**2.4.3** Case study: The refund rule that nobody reviewed Composite case, fictional. Harrowgate & Pike Credit is a mid-sized bank with many small-business customers and a fraud programme with a quarterly assessment, a monitoring team and a monthly metrics pack. The case follows one control through its life. Month one. A criminal group begins a campaign against the bank’s small-business customers, sending them altered bank details on what look like genuine supplier invoices. Customers approve first payments to new payees, and losses rise. The fraud team designs a rule that holds first payments to new payees above a set amount for review and a customer call-back. It is recorded in the risk register as the main control for the scheme, with the head of payment fraud, Ingrid Salo, as owner. Month two. Losses fall sharply and the metrics pack credits the rule. The hold queue, however, grows beyond what the review team can clear in a day. Month three. To cut the queue, the operations manager raises the amount above which a payment is held, through an ordinary system ticket. It is not recorded in the register, not reviewed by Ingrid Salo and not reported. Month four. Two of the five reviewers are moved to a dispute-handling team by agreement between two managers. The move does not reach the control owner. Month five. A system upgrade resets the review queue’s priority settings. The rule still runs, but held payments are released automatically when they have waited a set time without review. Month seven. Losses on the scheme begin to rise and within weeks pass their level in month one. The loss shows up in the metrics pack as a spike, months after the first signs. What the bank saw: a fall in losses, read as success, and a queue, read as an operational nuisance. What it missed: three changes to the control that never reached the owner or the register, a measure that counted holds and not reviews, and a deterioration in indicators such as the share of held payments released without review. The bank reconstructed the control’s history from tickets, recalled what funds it could, restored a review requirement so that unreviewed payments are not released, put all threshold changes under the control owner’s approval, and added measures of holds reviewed before release, queue age and payments near each threshold. Why nobody noticed. Each person acted sensibly from where they sat. The operations manager was solving a real queue problem. The control owner never saw a change that altered what her control did. Nothing required her sign-off, and nothing measured whether the control operated. Lessons. A control is the whole chain of rule, queue, staff, settings and measures. Changes to it must pass through its owner. Metrics must show that the control operated, not only that it triggered. Falling loss is good news only after checking that the control still works.


## Scenario — WHICH MEASURE SHOULD HAVE BEEN WATCHED?

A reviewer in internal audit at Harrowgate & Pike Credit is given the case timeline above and asked to propose the single most useful monitoring measure to show that the control had stopped operating as designed. The candidates are a monthly report of net fraud loss, a count of payments held, a report of the share of held payments released without review, and a list of customer complaints about delays. **Question.** Which measure would have shown that the control had stopped operating as designed, and why do the others fall short? **Answer.** The share of held payments released without review. It measures whether the control operated, and it would have risen as soon as the system reset began to release payments automatically, months before losses rose. Net loss lags the problem.

A count of holds fell after the threshold rise, but a fall in holds looks like success and cannot tell a working control from a loosened one. Complaints about delays fall when payments are released without review, so they point the wrong way. A sound practitioner also asks for change control on the rule’s thresholds and staffing, so that the earlier changes would have reached the owner. The wider lesson is that monitoring should measure control operation, not only outcomes.


## What to take from this chapter
- Internal metrics should cover four questions: how much fraud there is, how well controls catch it, what control costs customers and how fast the response is
- Fraud loss is a lagging measure; key risk indicators give earlier warning that conditions or controls are changing
- Reports to the board should be short, compared with appetite, include bad news and say what decisions are needed
- The monitoring principle asks the organisation to evaluate whether the principles are present and functioning, and to communicate deficiencies
- Drift, the slow movement away from the designed state of a control, is rarely deliberate and must be looked for
- A control includes its rule, its queue, its staff, its settings and its measures; changes to any of them should reach the control owner.
