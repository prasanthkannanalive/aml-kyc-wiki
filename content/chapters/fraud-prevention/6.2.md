# Investigating

Investigation is the disciplined work of finding out what happened, to whom, by whom and how, and writing it down so that others can rely on it. It is done under a duty to be fair, to protect evidence and to stay within the law. This chapter covers evidence and chain of custody, building a timeline, interviewing, and reporting findings. The ACFE Code of Professional Standards is the authoritative source used here. Interviewing has no single published standard that this guide relies on, so the interviewing material is conceptual and ethical practitioner method.


**6.2.1** Evidence and chain of custody The ACFE Code of Professional Standards sets a clear test for examinations: they should be legal, professional and thorough, seeking evidence that is complete, reliable and relevant (IV.A.1). It also asks the examiner to keep procedures for evidence, to track chain of custody and to preserve the integrity of relevant evidence (IV.B.1). Chain of custody is a record of who held a piece of evidence, from collection to use, and what was done to it, so that someone can later show it is what it claims to be and has not been altered. Most evidence in an institution is digital: logs, device and session records, call recordings, messages, transaction data and images of documents. Digital evidence is easy to change by accident, so preserve before analysing: take a copy under controlled conditions, record the time, source and person, and work from the copy. The second rule is to preserve early. Logs rotate and recordings are deleted on schedule, so a request to preserve relevant records, a legal hold, should go out as soon as a case looks likely to lead to a report, dispute or prosecution. Export transaction data from the system of record, not a screenshot, and record statements in the customer’s own words.


**6.2.2** Building the timeline and finding the story Most investigations come down to a timeline. Start with the earliest relevant event and list every action in order with its source: message received, credentials changed, new device registered, payee added, payment authorised, funds left, with the time zone beside each. A good timeline separates what is known from what is inferred. A log line is a fact. The statement “the customer shared a one-time passcode” is an inference unless the customer has said so. Mark inferences as such. The timeline then shows two things: the sequence that explains what happened, and the gaps where nothing is yet explained. Link analysis looks at what connects one account, device, address or beneficiary to another: several customers paying one new beneficiary, or several applications sharing one phone number, are connections a single alert would never reveal. A link is a lead, not a conclusion: a shared address can mean a household as readily as a ring. Test the leading explanation against the timeline. For an account takeover claim, ask whether the sequence is one a criminal would need: changed contact details before the payment, new device before the payee. If the customer’s account and the data disagree, do not decide who is wrong. Note the disagreement and go back for the missing piece.


**6.2.3** Interviewing: purpose, planning and fairness An interview is a structured conversation to obtain information, whether from a customer, a witness, a colleague or the person under suspicion. No single published standard is relied on here, so what follows is conceptual and ethical practitioner method. It excludes pressure, threats, trickery and any technique designed to produce a confession rather than information. Plan first: decide what facts are needed that the records cannot supply, review the file so questions are specific, and know what you may say about the matter. Begin by explaining who you are, why you are speaking and how the conversation will be recorded. Build rapport by being courteous and unhurried. Ask open questions, those that cannot be answered with yes or no, such as “tell me what happened when you got the call”. Let the person finish, follow up with narrower questions, use silence rather than pressure, and summarise back what you heard so that errors are corrected on the spot. Document it at the time: who was present, when it began and ended, the questions and answers, in the person’s own words where possible. Stop when the person asks for a lawyer or a representative, when they are distressed to the point that a fair account is not possible, or when you learn that the matter belongs with the police or legal counsel. Stopping is not a failure of technique. Fairness runs through all of this: the person is told the truth about why they are being spoken to, is not pressured or tricked, and is treated as someone whose account may turn out to be right. An unfair interview undermines the Code’s requirements that reports not be misleading (V.A.1) and that evidence be weighed on both sides (IV.A.3). Employee interviews raise employment-law questions that differ from place to place: rights to be accompanied, notice, recording, and what may be put to the person. Take advice from HR and legal counsel first, and where there is any prospect of a criminal investigation, ask counsel whether the institution should interview at all, because an internal interview can affect a later police process.


**6.2.4** When to stop, and how to report Stop the wider investigation, too, when it has answered the question it was opened to answer, or when further work is out of proportion to what it could add, and record the reason. Reporting then turns the work into something others can use. The Code is direct about reports. It must not be misleading (V.A.1). It must rest on sufficient, reliable and relevant evidence within the examiner’s expertise (V.B.1). It must not express an opinion on the legal guilt or innocence of any person (V.B.2). A report can say that a payment was made from a device registered the same hour as a password reset, and that the customer says they did neither. It does not say “the customer is lying” or “the employee committed theft”: guilt is for a court. A usable report states the reason for the work and its scope, the facts and sources, what is unresolved, the limits of the work and what follows.


## Scenario — THE EMPLOYEE INTERVIEW

Larchmont Bank’s investigations team is looking at a branch employee, Dmitri Volkov, who accessed several dormant accounts that were then used for outgoing payments. Lead investigator Ines Carvalho wants to interview him that afternoon. A colleague suggests she call it a routine records check, hoping he will speak more freely, and then press him on the access log if he is vague. HR has not been told, the bank has not decided whether to refer the matter to the police, and Dmitri does not know he is under review. **Question.** Is the colleague’s approach sound, and what should Ines do first? **Answer.** It is not sound. Describing a suspected-misconduct interview as a routine check is deceptive, and pressing a person on a log to extract an admission aims at a confession, not information.

Both break the fairness principle: an interview seeks information and tells the truth about its purpose. A statement obtained that way may be unreliable and may harm later disciplinary or criminal steps. Ines should pause and speak to HR and legal counsel about local rights, whether he may be accompanied, and whether the police should be involved first. If an interview goes ahead, she plans it, explains its purpose truthfully, asks open questions, records it at the time and stops if he asks for representation, while preserving the access logs.


## Scenario — THE REPORT THAT WENT TOO FAR

Rafael Montes, an analyst at Delmar Credit, finishes a report on a suspected application fraud. The evidence shows that the identity documents were submitted from a device linked to three other applications, that the phone number was registered the same week, and that the applicant has not responded to two requests for a call. His draft says: “The applicant is a fraudster who fabricated his identity and should be prosecuted.” His manager asks him to add a paragraph on the documents that were consistent with a genuine applicant, which Rafael had left out as not helpful. **Question.** What is wrong with the draft, and how should it be corrected?

**Answer.** The draft expresses an opinion on legal guilt, which the Code says a report must not do (V.B.2), and it leaves out evidence that points the other way, against the duty to weigh evidence on both sides (IV.A.3). A report that omits it risks being misleading (V.A.1). The facts stated are enough to support a referral without characterisation, which adds nothing and exposes the institution if the explanation proves innocent. A corrected report states the shared device, the phone number timing and the non-response as findings, with sources, and includes the consistent documents with a note of what they do and do not establish. It lists what is unresolved, such as whether the applicant’s identity can be verified by another route, and it recommends the next steps.

The decision to refer or prosecute belongs to the people with authority to make it, not to the analyst’s wording.


## What to take from this chapter
- An examination should be legal, professional and thorough, and seek evidence that is complete, reliable and relevant
- Chain of custody records who held evidence and what was done to it, and digital evidence should be preserved before it is analysed
- A timeline separates facts from inferences, notes time zones and shows gaps; a link between accounts is a lead and not a conclusion
- Interviewing is planned, open, fair and documented at the time; it never uses deception, threats or pressure, and employee interviews need HR and legal advice first
- A report rests on sufficient, reliable and relevant evidence, is not misleading, and gives no opinion on legal guilt or innocence.
