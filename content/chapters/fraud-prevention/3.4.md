Card fraud Card fraud is a long-established payment fraud, and it is still the clearest example of how a payment system spreads risk across several parties. A card payment involves five roles, and each role carries a different part of the loss and holds a different control. This chapter explains the flow, the main ways cards are misused, the controls that reduce fraud, and the rules of dispute and liability. It ends with the hardest judgement in card work: telling a true fraud, where a criminal used the card, from a friendly fraud, where the genuine cardholder disputes a payment they made. 3.4.1  The card payment flow and the five roles A card payment looks simple to the customer, but five parties sit behind it. The cardholder is the person who holds the card and the account. The merchant is the business that sells the goods or service. The acquirer is the bank or payment firm that signs up the merchant, receives its card transactions and pays it. The scheme (also called the card network) is the organisation that sets the rules and runs the infrastructure linking acquirers and issuers. The issuer is the bank that issued the card, holds the cardholder’s account or credit line and decides whether to approve each payment. The flow runs in two stages. In authorisation, the merchant sends the payment details to the acquirer, the acquirer passes them through the scheme to the issuer, and the issuer replies approve or decline within seconds. In clearing and settlement, which follows later, the transaction is confirmed, the issuer pays the acquirer through the scheme, and the acquirer pays the merchant, usually net of fees. Fraud is decided at authorisation, but its cost is settled afterwards, and that gap is why disputes exist. The first division is between card-present (CP) transactions, where the physical card or a device holding it is used at a terminal or machine, and card-not-present (CNP) transactions, where only the card details are used, online, by phone or by mail order. UK Finance calls this second category remote purchase fraud and defines it as a criminal using stolen card details to buy goods online, by phone or by mail order. How to read the published trend data for each type is in Chapter 1.3. Fraud involving the physical card, or a copy of it, has three main types. Lost and stolen fraud is the use of a genuine card that has left the cardholder’s possession, by accident or by theft. 63 Counterfeit fraud is the use of a copy of a card made from stolen data. Fraud on a card never received happens when a card is intercepted in the post or at delivery before the customer has it. Cards stolen or intercepted are exploited fast, so speed of reporting and speed of blocking decide most of the loss. 3.4.3  Card testing and the signs of a compromised merchant Card testing is a pattern where a criminal holding a list of card details checks which ones are live before using them for larger purchases. It tends to appear at merchants whose checkout offers little protection against repeated automated attempts, which is why acquirers monitor for it. From inside an acquirer or a merchant, card testing has a recognisable shape. There is a sudden burst of attempts in a short time. Many different card numbers are used from the same device, network address or session. Most attempts fail, and the failures often carry the same decline reason, such as an invalid expiry date or a mismatched security code. 3.4.4  Tokenisation and cardholder authentication Tokenisation replaces the card number with a substitute value, called a token, that is useless outside the context it was issued for. A merchant that stores tokens instead of card numbers holds nothing a thief can reuse elsewhere. If the token is stolen, the damage is limited and the issuer can cancel the token without replacing the underlying card. Tokenisation therefore attacks the value of stolen data rather than the theft itself. Cardholder authentication adds a step to prove the payer is the cardholder. In card-present payments, the chip on a card generates a one-time cryptogram for each transaction, and a PIN or a device unlock confirms the holder; this is widely seen as having pushed fraud towards card-not-present channels. In card-not-present payments, the main tool is 3-D Secure, a protocol through which the issuer can challenge the payer, for example with a code or an app approval, or approve silently when the risk is low. In the European Economic Area, strong customer authentication (SCA) is required for most electronic payments and needs two independent elements out of knowledge, possession and inherence. It remains effective against the fraud it was designed for, but fraud shifts to exempted transactions. The legal detail is in Chapter 7.2. Authentication has limits that an analyst must remember. It proves that the device and the credentials were used, not that the payer was willing. A customer who is talked into reading out a code, or into approving a prompt, has authenticated, and the fraud has still happened. That is why authentication data helps stop unauthorised fraud but does little against scams where the customer is deceived into acting (see Chapter 3.1). 3.4.5  Chargebacks and how liability moves A chargeback is a reversal of a card payment that the issuer initiates on the cardholder’s behalf under the scheme’s rules. It is not a refund granted by the merchant. It is the mechanism by which the scheme moves the loss from the issuer back towards the acquirer and the merchant when the transaction should not have stood. The cycle is the same in outline across schemes. The cardholder disputes a transaction, and the issuer decides whether the dispute fits a reason code, which is the scheme’s category for the grounds of dispute, such as fraud, goods not received or a duplicate charge. If it fits, the issuer 64 sends a chargeback through the scheme to the acquirer, who debits the merchant. The merchant may respond with evidence, a step called representment, and the acquirer passes it back. Liability follows a few general principles. Under the major schemes’ rules, which differ by scheme, region and transaction type, the examples below describe the usual pattern and are not a rulebook. When a transaction is fraudulent and was authenticated according to the scheme’s rules, the issuer usually keeps the loss, because it approved the payment and it had the chance to authenticate the payer. When a merchant failed to use an available protection, the liability can move to the merchant’s side. The liability shift for chip cards is the clearest example: where a counterfeit card was accepted by a terminal that could not read the chip, the party with the weaker technology bears the loss. In card-not-present payments, authenticating with 3-D Secure generally moves the fraud liability towards the issuer, while an unauthenticated payment leaves it with the merchant. These are rules of the schemes, not of the law, and they change over time, so an analyst should always check the current rules for the scheme and transaction type involved. SCENARIO — WHO PAYS FOR THE ONLINE ORDER Calder Bank issued a credit card to a customer, Mr Haldane. A criminal used the card details to order electronics from an online shop, Brightside Gadgets, which sells through an acquirer, Tarn Payments. Brightside’s checkout offers cardholder authentication through 3-D Secure, but it was switched off for this order because the shop considered it a source of abandoned baskets. Calder approved the payment. Mr Haldane saw it a week later, denied making it, and Calder raised a chargeback with a fraud reason code. Brightside says the order looked normal and asks Tarn to contest the chargeback because the goods were delivered. Question.  On the general scheme logic, who is most likely to bear the loss, and why does delivery not settle it? Answer.  The loss most likely falls on Brightside through Tarn. The order was unauthenticated even though authentication was available, so the merchant side most likely did not use the protection that would have moved fraud liability towards the issuer. Delivery to someone does not show that the genuine cardholder made the purchase. In a fraud dispute the question is whether the cardholder authorised the payment, and a delivery to a criminal’s address proves nothing against that. A sound view for Tarn is that Brightside’s choice to switch authentication off was a business decision with a price. Tarn can still help the merchant respond if it has real evidence tying the order to the cardholder, such as a long account history, but it should also review Brightside’s controls, since a merchant that disables protection tends to attract fraud and disputes. 3.4.6  True fraud and friendly fraud Not every disputed card payment is a criminal’s work. A first-party dispute, often called friendly fraud, arises when the genuine cardholder disputes a payment that they made or benefited from. There is no single agreed regulatory definition of the term, so it is used here as a working description and not as a legal definition. The nearest published categories are the ones Cifas uses: misuse of facility, meaning misuse of an account or product by the genuine holder, and false application. The reasons vary and the difference between them matters. Some cardholders genuinely do not recognise a payment because the merchant’s name on the statement differs from the shop’s trading name. Some forget a purchase, or a family member made it, or a subscription renewed. Some knowingly claim the goods never arrived when they did, or claim fraud to avoid paying for something they have used. The first group are honest mistakes and are best handled by explaining 65 the transaction. The last group is deliberate first-party misrepresentation, which is a fraud by the customer. Distinguishing true from friendly fraud is a judgement built from evidence, not a feeling. Useful signs of a genuine third-party fraud include a new device or location, a change of contact details just before the spending, shipping to an unfamiliar address, and a pattern unlike the customer’s own. Signs of a first-party dispute include the same device and address used in earlier undisputed purchases, delivery to the cardholder’s usual address, a history of similar disputes, and a login or authentication record that ties the cardholder to the purchase. No single sign settles the matter. SCENARIO — THE DISPUTED PARCEL Priya Nair at Delmar Credit reviews a fraud dispute from a cardholder, Mr Osei. He says he did not make an online purchase of sports equipment last month. Delmar’s records show that the order was placed from the phone Mr Osei has used for two years, authenticated by his banking app, and delivered to his home address, where he has received six earlier orders from the same retailer. This is his third fraud dispute in five months, each on a delivered order. Mr Osei says that he has been a loyal customer for years and that Delmar should simply refund him. Question.  How should Priya treat the dispute? Answer.  The evidence points towards a first-party dispute rather than third-party fraud. The device, the authentication, the delivery address and the purchase history all tie Mr Osei to the order, and the repeated pattern of disputes on delivered goods is a recognised warning sign. None of those signs is conclusive alone, but together they support refusing to treat the claim as third-party fraud: Priya should refer it for first-party review and not pay it as third-party fraud unless that review finds a credible explanation. Priya should not accuse Mr Osei of anything on the call. She should record the evidence, give him a fair chance to explain, for example whether a household member used the phone, and apply the bank’s process. If the pattern is established, the case should be referred to the first-party fraud team, and any record shared with other lenders must meet the evidential standard described in Topic 3.5.6 and Chapter 6.5. The error to avoid is a quick refund that rewards the behaviour, or a quick accusation that ignores the chance of an innocent explanation. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — A card payment involves five roles: cardholder, merchant, acquirer, scheme and issuer, and each holds different evidence and different controls. — Physical card fraud covers lost and stolen, counterfeit and cards not received, while card-not-present fraud uses only the card details and is judged by context. — Card testing shows as bursts of small attempts, many card numbers from few sources and a high decline rate with a repeated reason. — Tokenisation reduces the value of stolen data, and authentication such as chip and PIN, 3-D Secure and strong customer authentication checks the payer, but neither stops a customer who is deceived into approving a payment. — A chargeback moves a loss back through the scheme, and liability depends on scheme rules about authentication and technology used, not on whether goods were delivered. — Friendly fraud is a working term for a dispute by the genuine cardholder, and honest mistakes must be separated from deliberate misrepresentation by evidence. 66
