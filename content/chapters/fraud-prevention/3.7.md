# Cheque, deposit and other payment fraud

Paper cheques and cash are fading from everyday payments, but fraud against them has not disappeared, and the habits of mind they teach apply to every newer channel. A cheque is a promise that clears on a delay, and a deposit credited today can be reversed tomorrow. The gap between “funds shown” and “funds final” is where much of this fraud lives. This chapter covers cheque fraud, deposit fraud and kiting, and a short section on emerging channels, with the emphasis on how each works and the signs an institution can use to spot it.


**3.7.1** Cheque fraud: counterfeit, forged and altered A cheque is a written instruction from an account holder to their bank to pay a sum to a named payee. It is processed through a clearing system, and a bank that receives a cheque for deposit usually credits the customer’s account before the paying bank has confirmed that the cheque is good. The paying bank can return the cheque unpaid under the clearing rules that apply, and the deposit is then reversed. The delay between credit and certainty is the weakness. UK Finance describes three types of cheque fraud. A counterfeit cheque is a fake cheque on non-bank paper, drawn on a genuine account. A forged cheque is a genuine cheque, usually stolen, with a forged signature. A fraudulently altered cheque is a genuine cheque changed after it was written, for example by changing the payee or the amount. These three types are useful because they point to different controls. Forgeries are caught by signature comparison, by alerts from the account holder and by positive pay arrangements, in which a business sends the bank a list of cheques it has issued so that the bank can pay only matching items. Alterations are caught by image examination and by the same positive pay match on payee and amount.


**3.7.2** Deposit fraud and kiting Deposit fraud is the use of the deposit process itself, whether at a branch, at a machine or through a phone app, to get value out of the bank before the deposited item is found to be bad. The criminal’s aim is to withdraw or send the money while the credit is still provisional. The usual pattern is a deposit followed by quick movement. A cheque or other item is deposited. Within hours or days the customer withdraws cash, makes a transfer or buys goods. The deposited item is later returned as counterfeit, altered or stolen, and the credit is reversed, leaving the account overdrawn and the bank with the loss. Remote deposit, where the customer photographs the cheque with an app, adds two control issues: duplicate presentment, where the same item is deposited twice, once by image and once at a branch, and the quality of the image that the bank must rely on. A related scam uses the victim as the depositor. A person selling goods or taking a job receives a cheque for more than the agreed amount and is asked to send the difference elsewhere. The cheque is later returned as counterfeit, and the victim has paid out real money against a false credit. The bank sees an ordinary customer depositing a cheque and sending a payment, and the signs are in the story: an overpayment, an urgent request to return the balance, and a payer the customer has never met. Kiting, at awareness level, is the abuse of the delay in clearing to show a balance that no real money supports. The signs are frequent, regular transfers or deposits between the same accounts, high balances that never settle, withdrawals taken against uncleared funds, and accounts that are suddenly overdrawn when the cycle breaks. It can be a scheme or an act of desperation, and the 76 bank’s remedy is to monitor float, apply holds on suspicious deposits and review accounts with a circular pattern.


## Scenario — THE OVERPAID SELLER

Ms Rao, a customer of Larchmont Bank, is selling a second-hand piano online. A buyer from another city sends her a cheque for much more than the asking price, saying that the extra covers a courier whom Ms Rao should pay from her account once the cheque clears. She deposits the cheque at a branch machine, sees the full credit on her account within the hour, and asks the branch to send the extra by transfer to the courier. The teller notes that the credit is showing and that the customer is calm and polite. **Question.** What should the teller do, and why does the visible credit not settle the question? **Answer.** The teller should pause and ask about the transaction.

A cheque for more than the price, with the balance to be sent to a third party, is the textbook shape of an overpayment scam. The credit is provisional: the paying bank has not yet confirmed the cheque, and if it is returned as counterfeit the credit will be reversed. The money Ms Rao sends would then be her own. A sound teller explains in plain words that the credit does not mean the cheque is good, advises her not to send funds until the item has finally cleared, and escalates the case so that the deposit is examined and a hold or a return can be applied.


**3.7.3** Emerging channels in general terms New payment channels do not create new kinds of fraud so much as new routes for old ones. The same deceptions, the same exploitation of a gap between instruction and verification, and the same movement of funds through accounts are present. What changes is speed, reach and the point at which a transaction becomes final. Two examples are instant payments, which settle in seconds and cannot easily be recalled, and digital wallets, which put a card or account on a phone and add a new enrolment step that a criminal can try to abuse. For an analyst, a good habit is to ask four questions of any new channel. How is the customer identified? How is a payment authorised and by whom? When does the payment become final? Who can see the whole picture if something goes wrong? Answering these in advance shows where fraud will go and which controls are needed. The fraud function’s job is to ask these questions before launch and to review the answers once real data arrives.


## Scenario — THE NEW CHANNEL QUESTIONS

Larchmont Bank plans to launch an instant person-to-person payment feature in its mobile app. The product team argues that fraud risk is low because the feature is only for customers who already have accounts and who log in with a fingerprint. Payments up to a generous limit will be instant and final, and the app will not show any warning before sending. New payees can be added in the app within seconds. The product team wants to launch within a month and sees fraud controls as something to add later if problems appear. The fraud team is asked for its view in a short meeting. **Question.** Using the four questions about new channels, what should the fraud team say? **Answer.** The fraud team should say that the risk is not low, and should walk through the four questions.

Customers are identified by a fingerprint on a registered device, which protects against a stranger but not against a customer who is tricked into sending money. A payment is authorised by the customer alone, so a scam victim authorises it willingly. It becomes final at once, so there is no later chance to recall it. And nobody sees the full picture unless the bank builds real-time monitoring and payee checks. A sound recommendation is to launch with a lower initial limit, a payee check or warning for new payees, real-time monitoring of unusual patterns and a clear route for customers to report quickly. The limits can rise as the data shows how the channel behaves.
