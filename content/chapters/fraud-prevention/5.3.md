# Analytics and models

Models do what rules cannot: they weigh dozens or hundreds of weak signals at once and produce a score. They can also find patterns that nobody wrote down. They bring their own problems, including a dependence on past data, a vulnerability to changing behaviour, and a need for careful governance. This chapter explains the main approaches in plain terms, how scores become decisions, how network analysis exposes connected accounts, and what Benford-type tests can and cannot show. Firms differ in how they apply these methods, and the examples are invented to teach the reasoning.


**5.3.1** Rules and models: different jobs A rule asks a yes-or-no question defined in advance. A model estimates how likely an event is, using patterns in past data. The output of a model is usually a score, a number that ranks cases from least to most suspicious. A model does not make a decision. A threshold applied to the score, or a person reading it, does. Rules and models are complements. Rules suit known, sharply defined patterns, hard policy limits and quick responses to a new scheme. Models suit situations with many weak signals, where no single condition is decisive and there is enough history to learn from. Most institutions run a hybrid: rules produce hard stops, feed signals into the model as inputs, or override the score in defined cases, and the model ranks the rest so that the highest scores reach analysts first.


**5.3.2** Supervised models: learning from labelled cases A supervised model is trained on past examples that carry a label, in fraud work usually “confirmed fraud” or “genuine”. The model learns which combinations of inputs went with each outcome, and then scores new cases. The inputs are called features. A feature is any measurable characteristic that the model can use: the amount, the time since the payee was added, the number of payments in the past hour, the age of the account, or whether the device is new. A payment of a certain size means little on its own. The same payment expressed as a multiple of the customer’s usual payment, or compared with similar customers, says much more. Training works by showing the model a large set of labelled cases, then testing it on cases it has not seen. The result is a score. The institution chooses a threshold: scores above it generate an alert or an action, and scores below it pass.


**5.3.3** Network and graph analysis A graph is a set of nodes and the links between them. In fraud work the nodes are accounts, customers, devices, addresses, phone numbers, merchants and payees, and the links are the things they share or the money that flows between them. Network analysis asks questions that single-account analysis cannot: which accounts share a device, how many accounts send money to the same recipient, and how far does a known mule account sit from others in the network? A graph is especially valuable for finding rings and for spreading suspicion. A graph shows a cluster of otherwise unremarkable accounts joined by a shared device or a shared payee. If an account is confirmed as a mule, the accounts that sent it money or received money from it deserve review, and the graph ranks them by closeness. A link is a lead and not proof: two accounts may share a device because they belong to one household, so a graph result is checked against the facts before anyone is accused.


**5.3.4** Benford-type tests Benford’s law is an observation about the leading digits of many naturally occurring sets of numbers. In such data, smaller leading digits appear more often than larger ones: numbers beginning with 1 are common, and numbers beginning with 9 are much rarer. A Benford-type test compares the leading-digit pattern of a set of figures, such as invoice amounts or expense claims, with that expected pattern. A large departure is a reason to look more closely, because invented numbers can fail to follow the natural pattern. This is practitioner method, used mainly by auditors and forensic accountants, and it is best suited to occupational and accounting fraud such as that covered in Module 4. It works best on large volumes. It is unreliable on small data sets, on numbers assigned in sequence such as invoice numbers, and on figures shaped by artificial caps, floors or approval thresholds, where many amounts cluster just below a limit. The test points to where to look; the evidence still has to come from the records.


**5.3.5** The limits of models Models have three well-known limits. The first is drift. A model learns the world as it was in its training data. Customer behaviour changes, new products launch, seasons and economic conditions shift, and fraud methods evolve, so the model’s accuracy declines over time. The second is the adversary. Unlike most prediction problems, fraud has opponents who respond to the controls they meet. Once a scheme is blocked, those behind it change what they do, so the cases a model sees tomorrow are not drawn from the same population as those it learned from. The third is label bias. A model learns from the labels it is given, and labels reflect what the institution found and confirmed, not what actually happened. Fraud that was never detected is labelled genuine. Fraud found only in certain channels or customer groups makes those groups look riskier. If analysts review only high-scoring cases, the labels come from the model’s own earlier judgements, and the model learns to confirm itself.


## Scenario — THE FLAT HISTOGRAM

An internal auditor at Brannock Group runs a Benford-type test on expense claims across five hundred employees and finds that one department’s claims depart noticeably from the expected leading-digit pattern. A colleague suggests escalating the department head to the investigations team as a suspected fraudster. The auditor notes that the expense policy requires manager approval above a fixed amount, and that many claims in the flagged department fall just under it. Other departments with the same policy show a smaller but similar clustering. **Question.** What does the test result support? **Answer.** It supports a closer look at the department’s claims, not an accusation.

A policy cap or approval threshold shapes amounts and creates a departure from the natural pattern with no dishonesty involved, since many people simply claim up to the limit. The Benford-type test is known to be unreliable where thresholds distort the figures, so the departure here has an innocent explanation that must be ruled out first. That said, a cluster of claims just under an approval limit is worth reviewing in its own right, since it can also indicate someone avoiding review. The auditor should examine the receipts and claim patterns, compare them with similar departments, and keep the review factual.


## Scenario — THE LABELS THAT LOOK GOOD

A lender’s application-fraud model is retrained every six months using the cases that analysts confirmed as fraud. Analysts review only applications that the model scores above a high cut-off, because the review team is small. Each retraining improves the model’s measured performance on the confirmed cases. A new data scientist, Tomas Reyes, points out that applications scored below the cut-off are never reviewed and are all labelled genuine, and he asks whether the measured improvement can be trusted. **Question.** What is the weakness Tomas has found? **Answer.** The labels come from the model’s own judgements. Any fraud among the low-scoring applications is never reviewed, so it is labelled genuine and teaches the model that such applications are safe.

Each retraining then agrees more closely with the previous version, which looks like improvement but may only reflect a model learning to confirm itself. This is label bias. A sound remedy is to review a small random sample of applications across all score bands, including low ones, so that the labels include fraud the model missed. Outcome data that arrives later, such as defaults and chargebacks on apparently genuine accounts, can also be fed back as labels, and performance measured on this less biased sample.


## What to take from this chapter
- A rule asks a predefined yes-or-no question, while a model produces a score, and a threshold or a person turns that score into a decision
- Most institutions run a hybrid, with rules for known sharp patterns and hard limits, and models for many weak signals
- Supervised models learn from labelled cases and use features, and unsupervised methods look for the unusual without labels, so their output is a lead and not a decision
- Network analysis finds rings and spreads suspicion from a confirmed mule account, but its links are leads and not proof
- Benford-type tests are practitioner method that directs attention and never proves fraud, and thresholds and small samples distort them
- Models decay through drift, adversaries and label bias, so they need monitoring, retraining and unbiased samples.
