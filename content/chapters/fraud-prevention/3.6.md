# Business email compromise and corporate payment fraud

Business email compromise (BEC) turns the ordinary routine of paying invoices into the route of the loss. The payment is real, the instruction looks real, and the person who sends the money is a trained employee doing their job. What is false is one fact: who is asking for the money, or where it should go. This chapter explains how BEC and its personal-email cousin work, how vendor and executive impersonation differ, why out-of-band verification is the central control, and what a bank’s payments team and a corporate customer can each do. Who bears a loss is in Module 7.


**3.6.1** How BEC and email account compromise work FinCEN’s advisory on the subject describes business email compromise as criminals either breaking into email accounts or impersonating them with spoofed or lookalike addresses, and then sending fraudulent payment instructions or data. The advisory separates two targets. BEC targets operational organisations, and email account compromise (EAC) targets individuals through their personal email. In practice, operational organisations are businesses, charities and public bodies that pay suppliers; an example of EAC is a person buying a house who receives altered payment details. The FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center describes BEC as a scam aimed at businesses or individuals who work with suppliers or regularly make wire payments, using compromised email or other channels, through social engineering or intrusion, to cause unauthorised transfers. There are two ways in which the email channel is abused, and a practitioner should keep them apart. In the first, the criminal controls a genuine mailbox, by stealing the credentials or by other intrusion, and writes from the real account. In the second, the criminal does not control the mailbox but impersonates it, using a forged sender name or a near-identical domain. The scheme usually has four stages. First, research or access: the criminal learns who pays whom, when, and in what style. Second, the approach: a message arrives that fits the pattern of real business, for example a change of bank details or an urgent payment request. Third, the payment: the victim sends funds to an account the criminal controls. Fourth, movement: the funds are moved on quickly, often through several accounts, to make recovery hard. This last stage is why mule accounts (Chapter 3.3) are central to BEC.


**3.6.2** Vendor impersonation, executive impersonation and payment diversion In vendor impersonation, or invoice fraud, the criminal pretends to be a genuine supplier and tells the customer that the supplier’s bank details have changed. The next genuine invoice is then paid into the new account. UK Finance calls this invoice and mandate fraud, and groups it with CEO fraud under malicious redirection, meaning a payment that is diverted to an account that was not the one the payer intended. Payment diversion is the general term for this redirection of an otherwise real payment. In executive impersonation, often called CEO fraud, the criminal pretends to be a senior person, such as a chief executive or a finance director, and instructs a junior employee to make an urgent and confidential payment. The pressure comes from authority and speed: the message may say the matter is secret, that the executive is travelling and cannot be called, or that a deal will fail unless the payment is made today. **3.6.3** Out-of-band verification Out-of-band verification means confirming an instruction through a different channel from the one that carried it. If the instruction arrived by email, the check is not a reply to that email and not a call to a number in that email. It is a call to a number already held in the customer’s own records, or a conversation in person, or a confirmation through a system the criminal does not control. The principle is that a criminal who controls one channel will not usually control the second. The weakness of many verification routines is that they use the compromised channel. A reply asking “is this right?” goes to the criminal. A phone number printed on a forged invoice leads to the criminal. A confirmation from someone who is only relaying the executive’s request is not independent. A good routine is defined by where the contact details come from: they must exist before the request, and they must not come from the request.


## Scenario — THE SUPPLIER WHO CHANGED BANKS

Weaver and Pike, a small construction firm, receives an email from the accounts office of its steel supplier, Ardent Metals, saying that its bank has changed and giving new account details for the next invoice. The invoice is for a larger amount than usual because of a bulk order, and the project manager is waiting for the delivery. The email address is almost identical to the usual one. The accounts clerk, Sunil, phones the number at the bottom of the email, speaks to a polite person who confirms the change, and releases a large payment. A week later the real Ardent Metals chases the unpaid invoice. **Question.** What went wrong in the verification, and what should the process have required? **Answer.** The verification failed because it used the channel the criminal controlled.

The phone number came from the email, so the polite person who confirmed the change was part of the scheme. The near-identical sender address was a warning sign that went unnoticed, and a change of bank details just before a large payment was exactly the trigger that should have forced independent checking. The process should have required Sunil to call a number held on file before the request, such as the number in the original supply contract or a previous genuine invoice, and to speak to a known contact. A second person should have approved any change to payee details, and the first payment to new details should have been held until verified.


**3.6.4** What a bank’s payments team can do Detection looks at the payment against what the bank knows. A new beneficiary on a large payment. A beneficiary account that has just been opened or that has little history. A beneficiary 72 bank or country unusual for the customer. A payee name that does not match the account. A change to payee details followed within hours or days by a large payment. A sequence in which funds arrive in the beneficiary account and move out almost at once. A bank also sees these patterns on the receiving side, where an account that receives a payment from a business and quickly forwards it onward is a candidate mule account. Response is what the bank does when it suspects a diversion. If a payment is still pending, the bank can hold it and contact the customer through verified details. If the payment has gone, speed matters most. The bank should contact the receiving institution at once so that it can freeze or restrict the funds, because funds are moved quickly after receipt. The bank should also record the case in its systems with the typology clearly identified, because FinCEN asks institutions to use the key terms BEC FRAUD or EAC FRAUD in suspicious activity reports so that the cases can be found. The reporting rules are in Chapter 6.5. Recovery methods are covered in Chapter 6.4.


**3.6.5** What a corporate customer can do The core procedure is a rule that bank details are never changed on the strength of an email alone. A change request is checked through a callback to a known number, approved by a second person, and recorded. Payment authority is separated from the person who maintains the supplier list, so no one employee can change a payee and release a payment. Higher-value payments require dual approval. Staff are told that urgency and secrecy are warning signs, not reasons to skip the checks, and that senior colleagues expect to be asked.


**3.6.6** Case study: The completion payment at Hartwell Row Composite case, fictional. This case brings together the elements of the chapter in a timeline. The firms and people are invented. Halden Savings, a regional bank, serves a small legal practice, Hartwell Row Solicitors, which handles property purchases and holds client money in a client account. Day one. A buyer, Ms Varga, is completing the purchase of a flat. Months earlier, a Hartwell Row staff member’s email account was compromised after she entered her password on a convincing fake sign-in page. The criminal has since read the mailbox quietly. He knows the completion date, the amount, the names of the parties and the style of the firm’s emails. He keeps covert access to the mailbox for months. Day four. Two days before completion, a message arrives in the thread between Hartwell Row and the seller’s solicitors, Pellam and Co. It appears to come from Pellam and Co. It says that because of an audit the firm’s client account has changed and asks that the completion funds go to new details. The sender address differs from the real one by one letter. The message follows the tone of earlier messages in the thread, so nothing looks strange. Day five. The Hartwell Row cashier, Daniel, receives a payment instruction from the partner handling the file, based on the new details. Daniel is uneasy because the beneficiary bank is not the one used in earlier deals, but the partner confirms by email that the details are correct. The partner is under pressure because completion is the next day. Daniel releases the transfer through Halden’s online banking. The payee name check returns a close match, and Daniel proceeds, because the warning screen allows it. What Halden saw. The payment was large for a client-account transfer. It went to a beneficiary account at another bank that was new to the customer, opened recently, and with a name that 73 was close to, but not the same as, the seller’s solicitors. Halden’s payment screening produced an alert, but the alert queue was long and the payment was released automatically because the customer had a good history and the payment came from an authorised user in the right session. Day six. The seller’s solicitors report that the funds have not arrived. Hartwell Row phones Halden’s business line. Because no one at Hartwell Row had Halden’s fraud number to hand, the call takes some time to reach the fraud team. Halden contacts the receiving bank. By then most of the funds had been moved on to other accounts within hours of arriving, and only a part remains frozen. What was missed. First, the change of payment details was accepted on the strength of an email in a thread that the criminal could read. No callback was made to a known number for Pellam and Co. Second, the payee name check gave a close match and the warning was treated as routine. Third, Halden’s alert was not reviewed before release, although it had several recognised signs: new beneficiary, recent account, close but inexact name, unusual size. Fourth, the compromised mailbox had no multi-factor authentication, and the covert access went unnoticed for months. What was done. Halden froze what it could, recorded the case under its internal BEC category and made the reports its rules require (Chapter 6.5). Hartwell Row informed its professional regulator, its insurer and its clients, and used its own funds to complete the purchase so that Ms Varga’s completion could go ahead. The firm then required callback verification for every change of payee, switched on multi-factor authentication, reviewed mailbox settings, and agreed with Halden a named contact for emergencies. Halden revised its policy so that payments with a close or no match to a new beneficiary above a set value are held for review. Cost. The costs were real but are not stated in figures here: a partial loss of the transferred funds, the firm’s replacement of client money, higher insurance costs, time lost, strained client relations and regulatory scrutiny. The deeper cost was trust. Lessons. A single weak link, an email accepted without independent checking, was enough. Each party had a chance to stop the loss, and each missed it for an ordinary reason: pressure, routine and an overloaded queue. Controls on both sides of the payment, and a ready line of communication, were the practical remedy.


## Scenario — THE URGENT TRANSFER FROM THE MANAGING DIRECTOR

At Larchmont Bank, a payments analyst, Tomás Herrera, sees a transfer from Orlin Foods, a food manufacturer, to a beneficiary at a bank in another country that Orlin has never paid before. The amount is several times the company’s usual supplier payment. The beneficiary account was opened a few days ago, and the payee name check returned an exact match to the name typed in. The finance clerk, Ahmed, released the payment with his own credentials, and a colleague approved it within minutes without a call. A note on the payment reads “per MD, confidential, pay today”. The managing director is known to be abroad. A colleague tells Tomás that an exact match and two approvals mean the payment is safe, and that holding it would annoy a good customer. **Question.** What should Tomás do?

**Answer.** Tomás should hold the payment and contact the customer through details already on file, and not through the instruction. An exact match shows only that the typed name agrees with the account name, not that the account holder is the intended payee. The two approvals do not help, because the second was given within minutes without an independent check. The facts that matter are a new beneficiary abroad, an account opened days ago, an unusual amount, secrecy and urgency, and an executive who cannot be reached. Together they match executive impersonation. The cost of a short delay and a call is small beside the cost of an unrecoverable payment. If Orlin’s finance director confirms through the verified route that the payment is genuine, Tomás releases it and notes why.

If Orlin did not expect it, the payment is stopped and the case becomes a BEC investigation. The error to avoid is treating a good history, a clean name check and two approvals as proof that the instruction is real.


## What to take from this chapter
- BEC is the use of compromised or impersonated email to cause payments, and email account compromise is the same method aimed at individuals’ personal email
- The two technical routes are control of a genuine mailbox and impersonation through a forged name or lookalike domain
- Vendor impersonation changes where a real invoice is paid, and executive impersonation demands an urgent and secret payment, but both exploit a process that accepts an unverified instruction
- Out-of-band verification confirms an instruction through a channel that the instruction itself did not supply, using contact details held before the request
- A bank’s payments team can warn, offer dual authorisation and payee verification, detect new or young beneficiaries and close name matches, and act fast with the receiving bank
- A corporate customer controls the most important defences: callbacks on any bank-detail change, separation of duties, multi-factor authentication on email and a culture in which pausing a payment is rewarded.
