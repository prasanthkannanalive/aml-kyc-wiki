# The three lines and who owns fraud

Everyone in a financial institution touches fraud, and for that reason it is easy for nobody to own it. The Three Lines Model, published by the Institute of Internal Auditors, is a widely cited way of dividing responsibility for risk. This chapter explains what the model says, then shows how fraud teams apply it, taking care to separate the IIA’s text from the practical application built on it. The test throughout is simple: when a loss occurs, which part of the organisation was supposed to prevent it, and who checked?


**2.5.1** What the Three Lines Model says The IIA’s Three Lines Model replaced the older Three Lines of Defense. It describes the roles that make an organisation’s governance and risk management effective. The governing body, usually the board, is accountable to stakeholders for oversight: it sets direction and risk appetite, oversees risk and control, and establishes internal audit. The first line is management, which runs the operations that deliver products and services and manages the risks that arise in them. It owns risk because the people who run a process are best placed to see it and the only ones who can change it day to day. The second line is made up of management specialists who give expertise, monitoring and challenge on risk, compliance and control. They remain part of management, so they are not independent in the way the third line is. The third line is internal audit, which gives independent and objective assurance and advice, and is accountable to the governing body. Independence from the responsibilities of management is what gives its view weight. The IIA document does not mention fraud. It is a general model of governance, which applies to every risk. Anything said about fraud in the next topic is an application of the model, not IIA text.


**2.5.2** A practical mapping of fraud to the three lines This mapping is the course’s own application of the model to fraud. Neither the IIA document nor the COSO and ACFE guide, which describes roles by function, uses it. In the first line, the business owns fraud risk and fraud controls. The head of a lending product owns application fraud in that product, and the head of payments owns payment fraud. Operations teams that review alerts, authenticate customers and handle disputes are first line, and a fraud operations team usually sits here because it runs controls day to day. The first line identifies schemes, runs the assessment for its area, operates controls and reports losses. In the second line, a risk or compliance function, and sometimes a fraud risk function, sets the programme: policy, appetite, assessment method and reporting standards. It challenges the first line’s assessments, monitors indicators across the organisation, and advises on new products. Its challenge is credible only if it has the skill and the standing to say no. In the third line, internal audit independently tests whether the programme and its controls work. It does not design or run controls for the first line, because that would compromise its independence. It makes sure the owners see what the others see.


**2.5.3** Where the model breaks down for fraud The model is clear on paper and often blurred in practice. Four patterns recur. The first line disowns fraud: product managers launch features without asking how they could be abused, and the cure is to make fraud risk part of the business owner’s own targets and reporting. The second 43 line becomes the operator: a central fraud function ends up running alert queues because it is the only place with the skill, and then marks its own homework, designing the control, running it and judging whether it works. The second line is too weak to challenge: it lacks seniority, data or expertise, and a challenge function that has never overruled anyone has to ask why. The third line is pulled into the work: if internal audit helps design a fraud control, it cannot then give independent assurance on that control. Drift happens by small helpful steps. A central fraud team advises on a new lending product, then writes its rules, then staffs its alert queue because lending has nobody with the skill, then reports the results. After two years the head of the product cannot describe the scheme risks in it, the second line has no independent view, and audit finds that one team designed, ran and judged the control. A yearly check of who does each piece of fraud work, and who checks it, catches the drift before it hardens.


## Scenario — WHO OWNS THE LOSS?

Larchmont Bank’s central fraud team runs all alert queues, writes all detection rules, decides which controls to deploy and reports the results to the risk committee. The head of retail lending, Daniela Voss, says that fraud on her loans is a matter for the fraud team, not for her. The central team’s head says the business never gives him time or resources and so he has taken everything on. Internal audit reports that the fraud team’s rules have never been independently reviewed since the system went live. **Question.** Using the Three Lines Model, what is wrong with this arrangement, and how should responsibility be arranged? **Answer.** Responsibility has moved to the wrong place. In the model, the first line owns risk.

By running the controls and deciding what to deploy, the central team has taken on first line work and also the programme-setting work of the second line, which means it assesses and challenges its own controls. Daniela Voss, who runs the lending process and knows where its weaknesses are, has no ownership. A sound arrangement makes the head of lending the owner of lending fraud risk, with named control owners in the business, and has operational fraud staff report in to the first line. The central team would then focus on policy, method, challenge and cross-organisation monitoring, while internal audit tests independently. Ownership should not stay with whoever happens to have the skill.


## Scenario — AUDIT AS DESIGNER

Fernlea Payments’ head of internal audit, Kwame Asante, is asked by the chief executive to help the fraud team design a new set of controls over supplier payments, because audit knows the process well. Kwame agrees, and his team writes the control descriptions and the testing approach. Six months later the board asks internal audit for assurance that the supplier payment controls are effective. Kwame proposes to assign the same auditor who helped design them. **Question.** Is this acceptable? **Answer.** No. The third line’s value rests on independence and objectivity. Having helped to design the controls, the auditor would be reviewing their own work, and the board could not treat the result as independent assurance.

The model allows internal audit to give advice, but assurance on the same subject needs separation from the advice that shaped it. A sound approach is to have a different auditor, or an external reviewer, carry out the assurance work, and to tell the board about the earlier involvement. In future, audit’s advice should be given in a form that leaves management owning the design. It is the very reason independence is at risk.


## What to take from this chapter
- The IIA’s Three Lines Model describes roles: the governing body, a first line of management, a second line of specialist support and challenge, and a third line of independent internal audit
- The IIA document does not mention fraud, so the mapping of fraud to the lines is this guide’s own practical application, not IIA text
- In the practical mapping, the first line owns fraud risk and controls, the second line sets the programme and challenges, and the third line gives independent assurance
- The second line remains part of management, so it is not independent in the way the third line is
- Common failures are a first line that disowns fraud, a second line that becomes the operator, a second line too weak to challenge and a third line drawn into design.
