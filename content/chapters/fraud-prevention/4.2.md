# Expense, payroll and vendor fraud

Most occupational fraud passes through the finance function, because that is where money leaves the organisation. Expenses, payroll and supplier payments run in large volumes with routine approvals, and so offer cover to anyone who understands them. This chapter explains how the main schemes run, what each leaves behind in the records, how corruption in purchasing works, and which controls stop these schemes.


**4.2.1** Expense reimbursement and payroll schemes In an expense reimbursement scheme, an employee claims money back for costs that were not business costs, were never incurred or were inflated. The variants are well known. A mischaracterised expense passes off a personal cost as business. A fictitious expense invents a cost, often backed by false supporting documents. An overstated expense inflates a real cost. A multiple reimbursement claims the same cost twice, perhaps once on a card and once in cash. The amounts are usually small, which is why these schemes tend to run for long periods before anyone looks. Payroll schemes make the organisation pay money it does not owe. The best known is the ghost employee: a person on the payroll who does not work for the firm, or does not exist, with the pay going to an account the fraudster controls. Others include falsified hours or commissions, inflated pay rates, and changes to an employee’s bank details so that a salary is paid elsewhere. In a bank, commission systems are a particular target, because pay depends on reported sales that can be manipulated. The records show ghost employees clearly if someone looks: no leave taken, no tax or benefit record, a bank account shared with another employee, or an address that matches a payroll clerk’s. Payroll fraud is blocked by separating the people who add or change employees from those who run the pay, and by reconciling headcount between HR, payroll and building access. A mismatch between the number of people paid and the number who can be shown to exist is the plainest red flag, and the analyst checks whether the unmatched payees’ bank accounts match each other, an employee or a payroll clerk.


**4.2.2** Billing schemes and shell vendors A billing scheme is a fraud in which the employee causes the organisation to pay for something that is false, inflated or personal. It is the most common disbursement scheme in the ACFE data on cases. The best known form is the shell company scheme. The employee sets up, or arranges for someone to set up, a company that exists only on paper, then puts it on the vendor master file and approves invoices from it for goods or services that were never supplied. Any later payment goes to an account the fraudster controls. Variants include the pass-through scheme, in which a real but unnecessary middleman buys goods and resells them to the employer at a mark-up, and the personal purchases scheme, in which the employee buys items for their own use and has the employer pay the bill. The signs of a shell vendor can be seen in the vendor master file. The vendor has a post-office box or a residential address, no telephone number or website, an address or bank account that matches an employee’s, a name that resembles a well-known supplier, or no history beyond a few 84 invoices. The invoices have consecutive numbers (the vendor has no other customers), vague descriptions, round amounts and values placed just under an approval threshold. Often the vendor was created and used by the same person. The payments are often for services, which cannot easily be counted or inspected.


## Scenario — THE NEW SUPPLIER

Larchmont Bank, a fictional regional bank, runs a quarterly analytics review of its vendor master file. The review flags Northgate Facilities Ltd, set up eight months ago. Northgate has a single address that is a residential flat, no website, and a bank account in the name of a different person. It has issued invoices every month for “facilities consultancy”, each between the department head’s approval limit and the amount that would require a second approver. All invoices were approved by Tom Whitfield, a department head, who also requested the vendor’s creation. **Question.** What does the pattern suggest, and what should the analyst do first?

**Answer.** The pattern matches a shell vendor scheme: a vendor with no business footprint, a bank account that does not match the supplier name, invoices for an intangible service, values placed below the second-approval level, and the same person requesting the vendor and approving its invoices. That is a failure of segregation of duties, the very separation that is meant to prevent one person from creating and paying a supplier. Together they are strong grounds to investigate. The first step is not to stop payment in a way that tips Tom off. The analyst should preserve the records, confirm independently whether Northgate exists and whether any service was delivered, and refer the matter to the investigation function under the confidentiality rules.

**4.2.3** Conflicts of interest, kickbacks and bid rigging Corruption in purchasing is the quiet counterpart of the billing scheme. The vendor is real, the goods may even be delivered, and the invoice is genuine. What is corrupt is the decision to choose that vendor or to pay that price. A conflict of interest exists when an employee’s personal interest could influence a business decision. It becomes a fraud issue when the interest is hidden. The remedy is disclosure and recusal: staff declare interests at hiring and each year, and a person with an interest takes no part in the decision. A conflict that has been disclosed and managed is an ordinary business matter. A conflict that was hidden is a warning sign in itself. A kickback is a payment from a supplier to an employee in return for a favourable decision. The mechanism is usually an inflated invoice: the supplier raises the price, delivers the goods and returns part of the premium. The signs are prices above those of competitors, a supplier that wins repeatedly without much competition, an employee unusually protective of one relationship, a lifestyle that outstrips pay, and unrecorded gifts or hospitality. Bid rigging corrupts the tender process before the contract is awarded. It leaves signs: specifications that fit a single product, unexplained changes to the timetable, one bidder winning most contracts in a category, losing bidders who never complain, and contracts split into smaller pieces to fall under the competitive-tender threshold.

## Scenario — THE THREE-WAY MATCH THAT PASSED

Delmar Credit, a fictional lender, pays its facilities contractors only after a three-way match. An internal review finds that Marta Kowal, a facilities manager, has approved a series of invoices from Ashgrove Maintenance for “ongoing site inspections”. Each purchase order, service confirmation and invoice agreed, so every payment passed the match. But Marta created the purchase orders, signed the service confirmations herself because no one else was assigned to the sites, and chose Ashgrove without a tender. Ashgrove is owned by a former colleague of hers. No one has ever checked that inspections took place. **Question.** Why did the three-way match fail to catch this, and what should change? **Answer.** The match confirms that three documents agree, not that the documents are true.

Here all three came from the same person, so they agreed by construction. The control was undermined by a lack of segregation of duties: the person who requested the service also confirmed that it had been delivered. In addition, the award of the contract without competition, and the personal link to the owner, are conflict-of-interest and bid-rigging indicators that the match was never designed to see. The fix is to separate the roles. Someone independent of the requester should confirm delivery, for services as well as goods, by an inspection report, access log or site visit. Awards without tender should be approved by a second person, and staff should declare relationships with suppliers.

The error to avoid is to conclude that the control is useless: a three-way match is valuable when the people who feed it are independent.


## What to take from this chapter
- Expense schemes use mischaracterised, fictitious, overstated or duplicate claims, and rely on claims being approved without close checking
- Payroll fraud centres on ghost employees, falsified pay and diverted bank details, and the plainest red flag is a mismatch between people paid and people who can be shown to exist
- Shell vendor schemes show up in the vendor master file: unusual addresses, bank accounts that match employees, consecutive invoice numbers and amounts just under approval limits
- Kickbacks and bid rigging leave the books balanced and are found by comparison: price benchmarking, spend by vendor and repeat winners
- A conflict of interest that is declared and managed is a business matter, and a hidden one is a warning sign
- Three-way match, approval limits and bank-detail change controls work only when the people feeding them are independent of each other, and mandatory leave helps by exposing schemes that need daily attention
- Controls must apply to senior people too, because override of controls is a recurring weakness.
