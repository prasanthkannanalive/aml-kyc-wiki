Organisational liability for fraud Most of this module is about protecting the organisation and its customers from fraud committed by others. This chapter looks at a different question: when is the organisation itself legally responsible because someone connected to it committed fraud? The UK offence of failure to prevent fraud is the clearest example. It rewards the habits taught in this module, and its guidance reads like a short summary of them. The chapter describes how the offence works in principle and how to reason about the defence; the statutory detail is in Chapter 7.1. 2.6.1  What the offence is and who is covered Under the UK Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act, a large organisation can commit an offence if a person associated with it commits a specified fraud offence intending to benefit the organisation, or a person to whom the associated person provides services on the organisation’s behalf. The offence is framed around the associated person’s fraud and the organisation’s failure to prevent it, not around what senior managers knew. It is aimed at fraud that benefits the organisation. It is not a rule about reimbursing customers who were defrauded, and where the organisation was the victim of the fraud, the offence does not apply. Several terms need defining. A large organisation is one that meets size tests set in the Act, which Chapter 7.1 explains; the offence applies to bodies corporate and partnerships wherever they are formed, so a firm outside the United Kingdom may be within its reach. An associated person includes an employee, an agent, a subsidiary and any other person who provides services for or on behalf of the organisation. Whether someone is associated is judged on all the circumstances, and the fraud must be committed in that capacity. The base offences are the fraud and related offences listed in the Act, including fraud by false representation, by failing to disclose information, by abuse of position, false accounting and fraudulent trading. 2.6.2  The reasonable procedures defence The central feature of the offence is its defence. An organisation is not guilty if it proves that it had reasonable prevention procedures in place, or that it was not reasonable in the circumstances to expect it to have any. The burden of proof sits with the organisation, which must show it, not simply deny it. The law does not demand perfect prevention. It asks whether the organisation took sensible, proportionate steps for its size, its business and the risks it actually faces, with more expected where the business is larger or the risks are higher. A firm should ask three questions. What is the exposure: how many people act for us, in what roles, and who could gain us business by deceiving a customer? What do we do now about each exposure? And what could we show, from before the incident, that proves we did it? Government guidance on the offence is advisory, not a safe harbour: following it does not guarantee that the defence succeeds, and a court decides in each case. But a firm that can show it followed the guidance, and tailored it to its risks, is in a strong position. In practice, the organisation will need to show what it did before the fraud happened. A programme with documented assessments, named owners, tested controls, training records and monitoring reports answers that question. A programme that existed only after the incident does not. 46 2.6.3  The six principles of the guidance The Home Office guidance sets out six principles. SCENARIO — THE BROKER AND THE FALSE CLAIM Delmar Credit, a large lender, pays commission to independent brokers who introduce borrowers. A broker, Stefan Brandt, tells borrowers that Delmar’s loans carry no early repayment charge, which is untrue, so that more of them sign. No one at Delmar’s senior level knew. Delmar’s fraud risk assessment covers customer application fraud and staff theft, but has no entry for fraud by brokers against borrowers for Delmar’s benefit. When complaints reveal the pattern, Delmar’s lawyers ask whether the lack of senior knowledge protects the company. Question.  Does the absence of senior management knowledge protect Delmar, and what does the assessment gap suggest? Answer.  No, not on that ground alone. The offence is framed around the associated person’s fraud and the organisation’s failure to prevent it, not around what senior managers knew. If Stefan provides services for Delmar, and the fraud was intended to benefit Delmar as well as himself, the question becomes whether Delmar can prove reasonable prevention procedures. Whether the offence applies is a legal judgement, and Chapter 7.1 gives the detail. 47 The assessment gap is the key weakness. SCENARIO — THE OVERSEAS AGENT AND THE REASONABLE STEPS Fernlea Payments, a large payments firm, uses an overseas agent network to sign up merchants. A sales director, Hana Okafor, learns that one agent has been telling merchants that Fernlea guarantees settlement within an hour, which is untrue, and that merchants signed up on that basis. Fernlea has a written fraud policy, annual staff training for employees only, and no risk assessment of agents. A colleague says that because the agent is a separate company, Fernlea cannot be responsible. Question.  Is the colleague right, and what does the case say about reasonable procedures? Answer.  The colleague is wrong to treat separateness as protection. An associated person includes an agent and anyone providing services for or on behalf of the organisation, and the fraud appears to have been committed to benefit Fernlea as well as the agent. Whether the offence is made out is a legal judgement, but the structure of the offence makes Fernlea’s own procedures the central question. On the facts, Fernlea would struggle to show reasonable procedures: no assessment of agent risk, training limited to employees and no due diligence or monitoring of the network. A sound response is to take legal advice now, stop the misstatement, assess the agent risk, add due diligence, contractual terms and training for agents, and monitor merchant complaints. Relying on corporate separateness, instead of asking what prevention procedures existed, is the trap.
