Reporting and information sharing A fraud case rarely ends inside the investigation team. It goes up to senior management, out to regulators or law enforcement, and sometimes across to other institutions. Each route has its own purpose, its own legal basis and its own limits on what may be said. This chapter covers internal escalation, the main kinds of external report, the principles of suspicious activity reporting where fraud and money laundering meet, and the lawful bases for sharing fraud information. The principles of the legal regimes are in Module 7, and nothing here says what a particular organisation must do. 6.5.1  Internal escalation and external reporting routes Escalation means moving a matter to someone with more authority or a different responsibility. Set triggers in advance, in writing, so no analyst decides alone whether a matter is serious enough; each should name the recipient, the form and how quickly. Common triggers are suspected staff involvement, a large loss, a pattern, a vulnerable victim and any need for counsel. The destination depends on the matter: staff concerns go to HR and legal counsel, suspected reportable activity to the compliance function that files reports, control failures to the risk owner, and serious matters to senior management and, where governance calls for it, the board (Chapter 2.1). Escalation is not a transfer of responsibility; the case owner keeps the case. External reports fall into three groups: regulatory notification to the supervisor of a significant event or control failure, decided by compliance and senior management with counsel; suspicious activity reports to the financial intelligence authority, filed by the designated financial crime function; and law-enforcement reports to start or support a criminal investigation, decided by the investigations lead with counsel. Which are mandatory in a given place is a matter for the regime and legal advice (Module 7). The facts given to the supervisor, the financial intelligence authority and the police should agree with each other and with the case file. 6.5.2  Suspicious activity reporting, and where fraud meets AML A suspicious activity report (SAR) is a confidential report an institution files with its financial intelligence authority when it suspects that a transaction involves crime. Regimes differ in detail, but the shape is the same. The trigger is knowledge or suspicion, or reasonable grounds for suspicion, that funds come from crime, that a transaction is designed to evade reporting rules, or that it has no apparent lawful purpose after review. A designated function files, a time limit runs from the moment the institution first has the facts, urgent matters are raised with law enforcement directly, records must be kept, and the law protects a good-faith reporter. The numbers belong to the current rules; Chapter 7.3 explains the US bank rule in principle. A short example shows how an analyst uses the idea. A bank sees an account that receives six payments in a day from unrelated senders, each described as an invoice, and sends most of the money abroad within the hour. The account holder has no business history. The analyst asks whether the bank knows, suspects or has reason to suspect illegal funds, evasion or no apparent lawful purpose, and whether there is a reasonable explanation after review. If not, the case goes to the compliance function, which decides and files; the analyst’s job is a clear file. 135 Fraud is a source of dirty money, and money laundering is how fraud proceeds are moved and hidden. A mule account is where the two meet (Chapter 3.3), so a fraud investigator and a financial crime investigator often look at the same event from two ends. A fraud case can therefore make a transaction reportable even when the institution is not the victim and no loss is proved: the test asks about knowledge or suspicion of illegal sources, evasion or no lawful purpose, not who bears the loss. A compliance function applies it case by case. Some authorities ask filers to use indexing terms in the narrative so that reports on a typology can be found; a term is an indexing aid and does not change the reporting test. Confidentiality is the other point of contact. Because a report and its existence are confidential, customer-facing staff should follow the compliance function’s instruction on what may be said and never hint that a report has been made or is being considered. 6.5.3  Sharing fraud information lawfully Fraudsters work across institutions: a mule account at one bank pays another, and a stolen identity is used at several lenders. Institutions that cannot share will always be a step behind, but sharing personal data without a lawful basis is itself a breach. Cifas, the UK fraud-prevention membership body, runs the National Fraud Database on the basis of legitimate interests, with fraud prevention as a supporting purpose under data protection law. Its filing standard asks for reasonable grounds to believe that fraud or financial crime was committed or attempted, with clear, relevant and rigorous evidence, conduct that meets a defined Case Type, and, in general, that the member rejected, withdrew or terminated a product on the basis of fraud. Its eight principles are reciprocity, purpose limitation, transparency, lawfulness, fairness, accuracy, integrity and data minimisation. Individuals can request access and challenge a record through the member who filed it, and an entry does not stay for ever. In the United States, a provision of the USA PATRIOT Act gives financial institutions a voluntary route to share information with each other, with a safe harbour for sharing within its terms (Chapter 7.3). Whatever the route, SAR confidentiality still applies, so sharing is about the underlying facts about a beneficiary and its payments, and the compliance function decides what is shared and with whom. The principles common to every route are a lawful basis, a stated purpose, only the data needed, accuracy, security and a way for individuals to challenge. SCENARIO — THE CUSTOMER WHO ASKED DIRECTLY Larchmont Bank has restricted the account of Mr Pavel Novak, whose account received several scam payments from different people in a single week. The compliance function has told the fraud team that it is dealing with the matter and has instructed staff not to discuss the case with the customer. Mr Novak telephones the branch, angry that his card no longer works. An agent tempted to calm him says, “Don’t worry, it is just paperwork”; another suggests saying nothing has been reported. Mr Novak asks directly: “Have you reported me to anyone?” Question.  What should the agent say? Answer.  Neither suggestion is sound. A report and its existence are confidential, so the agent should not confirm or hint that a report has been made. Saying that nothing has been reported would be false, and a false statement damages the bank’s credibility and could mislead the customer. The agent should follow the compliance function’s instruction on what may be said. 136 A sound answer describes the outcome and the next step without the mechanism: “The account is restricted while we check some recent activity. I cannot discuss the details of our checks, but I will make sure the right team knows you called and that someone contacts you by a set time.” The agent refers the call to compliance and records the contact without speculation. SCENARIO — THE FILING THAT WAS TOO THIN A fraud analyst at Delmar Credit wants to file a customer on the Cifas National Fraud Database. The customer applied for a loan and was declined on a hunch that the income documents looked odd. Nothing was verified and no one asked the customer about them. The analyst argues that it is better to file now, because the customer might apply elsewhere, and that a wrongly filed entry can always be removed later. Question.  Does the analyst’s proposed filing meet the standard? Answer.  No. The standard requires reasonable grounds to believe fraud or financial crime was committed or attempted, supported by clear, relevant and rigorous evidence, and conduct that meets a defined Case Type. A decline on a hunch, with nothing verified, is not rigorous evidence. Fairness and accuracy also cut against filing and correcting later, because the entry can cause real harm in the meantime. The sound step is to investigate: compare the documents with independent sources, contact the applicant for an explanation, and record the findings. File only if the evidence then shows reasonable grounds; otherwise close the case without filing. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — The three external routes are regulatory notification, suspicious activity reporting and law-enforcement reporting, and the facts in each must match. — The SAR test looks at knowledge or suspicion of illegal funds, evasion or no lawful purpose, and it does not wait for a proven loss; the principles of the US bank rule are in Chapter 7.3. — A fraud case can be reportable even where the institution is not the victim; the compliance function decides case by case. — Follow the compliance function’s instruction on SAR confidentiality, never hint to a customer that a report has been made, and share underlying facts only through a lawful route. — Cifas filings require reasonable grounds and rigorous evidence, and information sharing needs a lawful basis, such as legitimate interests or the US voluntary sharing route. 137
