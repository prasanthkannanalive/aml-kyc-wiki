# Account takeover and unauthorised payments

Account takeover is the mirror image of an APP scam. Here the genuine customer does not make the payment; a criminal does, using the customer’s access. The institution’s task is to tell the criminal from the customer at the moment of login and at the moment of payment, and to do so without locking out the customer who has simply bought a new phone. This chapter explains how takeover works, how access is obtained at awareness level, what the sequence looks like in session data, how authentication and deepfakes change the picture, and where the line between unauthorised and authorised blurs.


**3.2.1** What account takeover is and how it differs from a scam Account takeover, often shortened to ATO, is the use of a genuine customer’s account by someone who is not the customer. UK Finance describes it as a criminal taking over another person’s genuine card account, and the UK fraud-prevention body Cifas uses the term facility takeover. The US Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) lists account takeover fraud as a key term for suspicious activity reports. The idea is the same in each: the account is real, the customer is real, but the person acting is not the customer. The key legal and practical consequence is that the payments are unauthorised. The customer did not instruct them. That puts takeover in the same family as stolen card fraud rather than scams, and the rules on who bears the loss are generally different. Module 7 sets out those rules. The analyst needs only to recognise the category, because the way the case is handled, the questions asked of the customer and the loss treatment all depend on it. Takeover is a process, not an event. A typical sequence has four phases, and each looks different from inside the institution. **3.2.2** How access is gained, at awareness level This section describes what analysts need to recognise, not how the techniques are carried out. Criminals obtain access to a customer’s account in a handful of ways, and most involve a person, not a flaw in the bank’s code. Phishing and its relatives. Phishing is a message, usually an email or a text, that tricks the customer into entering their login details on a fake site or handing over a code. Smishing is the same by text message, and vishing is the same by voice call. The FBI has warned that vishing can 54 use AI-generated audio to impersonate senior officials. The lever is almost always urgency or authority: “your account will be closed”, “a payment has been blocked”. SIM swap and number porting. A mobile phone number is moved to a SIM card the criminal controls, so calls and texts, including codes, arrive at the wrong handset. Cifas has reported a rise in unauthorised SIM swap cases, and the institution’s best defence is to treat a recent SIM change, where the telecoms provider will share that signal, as a risk indicator. Remote access. A caller persuades the customer to install software that lets them see and control the screen, often as part of a “refund” or “security” story. The customer sits and watches while the criminal uses their own device. This is the place where takeover and scam meet, and Topic 3.2.5 returns to it.


**3.2.3** The takeover as the institution sees it The institution can often detect a takeover only through behaviour in session and afterwards. Read the sequence, not each event on its own, because most events are innocent individually: customers do change phones, travel and forget passwords. The strongest pattern is a chain. A password-reset request, followed by a login from a device the bank has never seen, followed by a change of contact details, followed by a new payee and a payment out, is far more telling than any single step. The speed of the chain matters, as does the time of day compared with the customer’s habits. Speed and timing carry meaning. A reset at three in the morning, followed within minutes by a new device and a payee, fits a criminal working through a checklist. The same four events spread over a fortnight, with the customer’s usual device used in between, are more likely to be a customer who changed phones. The analyst should therefore ask three questions of any chain. How fast did the steps follow each other? Do they fit this customer’s habits for time of day and device? And did anything protective happen, such as a successful call-back to the number already on file? **3.2.4** Authentication, strong customer authentication and deepfakes Authentication is how the institution confirms that the person acting is the customer. It rests on factors of three kinds: something the customer knows, such as a password; something they have, such as a phone or a security key; and something they are, such as a fingerprint. Strong customer authentication, abbreviated SCA, requires two of these kinds and requires the elements to be 55 independent, so that the breach of one does not compromise the others. SCA makes stolen details alone insufficient, but fraud shifts towards payments that SCA exempts and towards scams that trick the user into initiating the payment. Chapter 1.3 explains how to read the evidence. The weakness to understand is the customer who is tricked. A one-time code protects against someone who does not have the phone. It does not protect against a customer who has been talked into reading the code aloud. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology separates ordinary authenticators from phishing-resistant ones in its digital identity guidelines. A phishing-resistant authenticator is bound to the real website or application, so a fake page cannot use it. Moving customers to them is a strong preventive step, because there is nothing to read out. Deepfakes add a new challenge. A deepfake is synthetic audio, image or video produced with generative artificial intelligence that appears to show a real person. Red flags include identity documents that disagree, a customer who declines multi-factor authentication or claims technical faults during a live check, and a new account that quickly moves money to higher-risk payees. No single flag proves illicit activity. Voice and video alone are weak proof of identity, and a convincing voice on a call must never override a failed check.


## Scenario — THE CALLER WHO SOUNDS RIGHT

At Larchmont Bank, a caller says she is Hana Verlaan and has lost her phone. She sounds distressed and gives her full name, address and date of birth correctly. She asks the contact-centre agent, Ravi Menon, to change the mobile number on her account to a new one and to send a new security code there. The agent’s screen shows that the account had a password-reset request two hours earlier, from a device not seen before. The caller says she is in a hurry because she needs to pay a deposit that afternoon. The agent finds her voice natural and her answers confident. **Question.** What should Ravi do? **Answer.** He should not change the number on the strength of the call.

The details the caller gave are the kind that appear in data breaches and on social media, so correct answers do not prove identity. The earlier password reset from an unknown device, followed by a request to change the contact number and urgency about a payment, matches the first three phases of a takeover. Ravi should follow the institution’s recovery procedure, which should require a stronger check than knowledge of personal details, such as an in-branch visit, document verification or a call-back to the number already on file. He should tell the fraud team about the earlier reset. If the genuine customer has lost her phone, a short delay costs little. If the caller is a criminal, the delay may protect the whole balance.


**3.2.5** Controls, response and the grey zone Controls against takeover follow the phases. Before access: phishing-resistant authentication where possible, protection against credential stuffing (the automated trying of lists of stolen login details against many accounts), warnings to customers that the bank will never ask for a code, and trained contact-centre staff. During the session: device and behavioural checks, step-up authentication for risky actions, and a cooling-off period on sensitive changes such as new payees straight after a contact-detail change. After a suspect event: notification through every known channel, the ability to freeze quickly, and clear routes for customers to report a problem at any hour. The grey zone is where the line between unauthorised and authorised blurs. If a criminal logs in using stolen credentials, the payment is unauthorised. If a criminal persuades the customer to log in and make the payment, it is an APP scam. If the criminal persuades the customer to install 56 remote access software and then makes the payment while the customer watches, the case sits between them: the customer did not press the button but did enable the access, and how such a case is classified is a matter for Module 7.


## Scenario — TWO REPORTS, ONE MORNING

Two customers of Delmar Credit report losses on the same morning, and the intake clerk has a long queue and a template that offers only two choices, authorised or unauthorised. Customer one, Idris Cole, says he did not make a payment at all: his phone stopped working the previous evening, and when he got a replacement he found transfers out of his account. Customer two, Tilly Brandt, says a caller from “the fraud department” asked her to install a program so they could “check her security”, watched her screen for twenty minutes, and she later found money gone. The intake clerk proposes to log both as unauthorised because neither customer typed a payee. **Question.** Is the clerk’s classification sound for both? **Answer.** Not for both.

Mr Cole’s pattern fits a takeover, possibly through a SIM swap, and it is reasonable to treat his payments as unauthorised pending the investigation. Ms Brandt’s case cannot be logged as unauthorised on the intake facts alone: she enabled remote access after a deception, so it is a mixed case and must go to a trained investigator before it is classified. Labelling both as unauthorised on the basis of “no one typed a payee” confuses who pressed the keys with who was deceived. The right step is to record the facts in each customer’s own words before classifying, then pass both cases to a trained investigator who knows the applicable rules. The classification can be refined later; the first hours cannot be recovered.


## What to take from this chapter
- Account takeover means a criminal acting as the genuine customer, so the payments are generally unauthorised and the loss rules differ from scams
- A takeover runs in four phases: obtain access, test, lock in by changing contact details or devices, and cash out
- Read the sequence, not the single event; a reset, a new device, a profile change and a new payee in a short window together are a strong signal
- Strong customer authentication makes stolen details alone insufficient, but it does not stop a customer who is tricked into reading out a code, and phishing-resistant authenticators address that gap
- Account recovery, password reset and contact-detail change must be at least as well protected as login, and staff are part of the authentication chain
- A convincing voice, face or set of personal details is not proof of identity, and deepfakes weaken them further
- Establish the facts in the customer’s own words before classifying a case as unauthorised or authorised, because mixed cases such as remote access exist.
