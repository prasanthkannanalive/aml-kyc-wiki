# Red flags and rules

A red flag is an observable sign that a fraud may be under way. A rule turns one or more red flags into an automatic check. Rules are the oldest and still the most common detection tool because they are fast, transparent and easy to explain to a customer, an auditor or a regulator. They are also blunt. This chapter explains what a red flag does and does not prove, how rules are built and governed, and which signals belong to which fraud type. It also shows, with a short worked example, why a rule that fires too often is usually a rule with a loose condition and not a rule with a bad idea behind it.


**5.2.1** What a red flag is, and what it is not A red flag is a reason to look closer. It is not proof. A new payee, a large payment or a login from a new device is something many honest customers do every week. The official FinCEN alert on deepfake-enabled fraud makes the point directly: no single red flag proves illicit activity, and an institution should consider the flags in context. Context is what turns a weak signal into a strong one. A payment to a new payee is common. A payment to a new payee, from a device never seen before, within an hour of a changed phone number, for most of the account balance, is rare. An analyst asks how many independent signals are present, how unusual each is for this customer, and whether the story they tell hangs together. Two signals that are each fairly common among honest customers are much rarer together, and in confirmed account takeover they often appear together, so the pair says far more than either alone.


**5.2.2** How rules are built A detection rule has four parts: the conditions that must be true, the parameters that set how tight those conditions are, the action to take, and the owner who is responsible for it. A condition might be “payee is new”, “device is new” and “amount is high relative to this customer’s history”. The parameters decide what counts as high or as recent. The action might be a silent flag, a step-up challenge or a hold. Rules fall into a few families. Static rules apply a fixed test, such as a payment to a country the institution does not serve. List-based rules compare an event with a list, such as known compromised devices or accounts already reported as mules. Comparison rules measure an event against the customer’s own history or their peer group. Sequence rules look for events in a particular order within a time window, such as a contact change followed by a new device and then a payment. Velocity checks are a common comparison rule. Velocity means how many times something happens, or how much value moves, in a period of time. A velocity check might count failed logins on one account, applications from one device, cards used at one merchant, or payments sent in an hour. High velocity is a signal because fraud is often done fast, before the victim or the institution notices.

## Scenario — THE NEW PAYEE RULE

Wexmoor Bank has a rule that holds any outbound payment above a set amount to a payee added in the last 24 hours. Nearly all alerts turn out to be genuine: customers paying builders, landlords and online sellers. A product manager proposes switching the rule off because it causes friction without catching much. The fraud analyst, Daniel Okoro, looks at the cases that were fraud over the past quarter and finds that almost all had a second feature in common: the login came from a device the bank had not seen before. Among the genuine payments the rule held, very few came from a new device, because honest customers usually pay from the phone they always use. **Question.** What should Daniel recommend? **Answer.** He should recommend keeping the signal but changing the rule.

A new payee on its own is a weak signal that most honest customers also produce. A new payee combined with a new device is far rarer among honest customers and far more common in the confirmed frauds he found. Turning the rule into a compound condition keeps its power to catch takeover fraud while cutting the volume of alerts on ordinary payments. The tempting error is to treat a noisy rule as a bad rule. Often the signal is sound and the condition is too loose. Before changing it, Daniel should test the new version against past data, including genuine payments, to see how many frauds it would still catch and how many alerts it would remove.


**5.2.4** Strengths, weaknesses and rule governance Rules are fast, can be written the day a scheme is seen, and are easy to explain to a customer. They also have weaknesses. Each rule covers a pattern someone has already thought of, so rules lag behind new schemes. Rule sets also grow as each incident adds a rule, and old rules are rarely removed. Governance keeps a rule set healthy. Each rule needs a named owner, a written reason, the typology it covers, its parameters, the date it was last tested and a review date. Changes need testing before release and a record of who approved them. Rules that no longer catch fraud, or catch only what another rule already catches, should be retired. A rule with no owner and no stated purpose looks like clutter even when it guards against a rare, costly pattern.


## Scenario — THE RULE NOBODY OWNS

During a review, Mira Castellan, a fraud strategy manager at Larchmont Bank, finds hundreds of active detection rules. For a quarter of them no owner is recorded, and for many the original reason is not documented. Several generate alerts every week, and analysts close nearly all of them as genuine without comment. A colleague suggests deleting every ownerless rule that produces no confirmed fraud, to cut the queue quickly. Mira notes that two of the ownerless rules were added shortly after a large loss, though nobody on the current team was there at the time. **Question.** Is deleting them at once the right approach? **Answer.** Not at once. A rule that finds no confirmed fraud may be useless, but it may also be doing its job as a deterrent or as cover for a rare, severe scheme.

Without an owner or a stated purpose, nobody can judge which. Deleting on the basis of alert history alone risks removing the only control for an uncommon but costly typology. A sound approach is to find an owner for each rule, document its purpose, then test it against past data. Rules that are redundant or have no supporting rationale are retired through the normal change process, with a record of the decision. Where a rule is doubtful but cannot be tested yet, it can run in monitor-only mode, producing no customer impact, while evidence is gathered. This reduces the queue without creating blind spots.


## What to take from this chapter
- A red flag is a reason to look closer, not proof, and its meaning depends on context
- Combinations of independent signals are far more informative than any single signal
- A rule has conditions, parameters, an action and an owner, and rules come in static, list-based, comparison and sequence families
- A velocity check counts events or value over a time period and is usually set by customer segment
- Rules are fast and explainable but lag behind new schemes and tend to accumulate without being retired
- Every rule needs an owner, a documented purpose, tested parameters and a review date.
