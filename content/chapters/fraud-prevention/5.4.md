Alert management and tuning Every rule and model ends in an alert, and every alert needs a person or a process to deal with it. Most fraud teams spend more effort on the alerts that turn out to be genuine than on the frauds they catch. This chapter explains how to read alert volumes, how to measure a detection system in plain terms, what false positives really cost, how to design queues, and how to change rules without creating blind spots. The numbers in the worked examples are invented for teaching. 5.4.1  Alert volumes and the funnel Alerts form a funnel. At the top are all the transactions or events the system sees. Some small fraction generates an alert. Of those, analysts or automated checks close most as genuine, and a few become cases. Of the cases, some are confirmed as fraud, and of those, some lead to a recovery or a report. 5.4.2  Precision, recall and why accuracy misleads Two measures describe how well a detection system works, and both can be explained without mathematics. Precision answers: of the alerts raised, what share were real fraud? Recall answers: of all the real fraud that happened, what share did the system alert on? Precision is about the quality of the queue. Recall is about what slips through. A third idea, the false positive, is simply an alert on something genuine. Overall accuracy, the share of all decisions that were right, is a poor guide in fraud work. Fraud is rare. Suppose one transaction in two thousand is fraud. A system that approves everything and never raises an alert is right almost all the time, and it catches no fraud at all. For this reason fraud teams look at precision and recall, usually for each typology and channel separately, and watch how they move over time. A rule that raises 100 alerts of which 20 are fraud has a precision of 20 per cent; if 80 frauds happened that month, its recall is 25 per cent. Both figures are needed, and neither says what to do on its own. 5.4.3  A worked example, with invented numbers These numbers are invented to show the method. A bank sees 1,000,000 payments in a month, and 500 of them are fraud. The bank can run its rule at two settings. At the loose setting, the rule raises 10,000 alerts. Of these, 300 are fraud. Precision is 300 divided by 10,000, which is 3 per cent. Recall is 300 divided by the 500 frauds, which is 60 per cent. There are 9,700 false positives, and 200 frauds were missed. At the tight setting, the rule raises 4,000 alerts. Of these, 240 are fraud. Precision is 240 divided by 4,000, which is 6 per cent. Recall is 240 divided by 500, which is 48 per cent. There are 3,760 false positives, and 260 frauds were missed. Now add costs, also invented. Each alert takes an analyst ten minutes. The loose setting needs about 1,667 hours of review and the tight setting about 667, so the tight setting saves about 1,000 hours. At an assumed 30 currency units per hour, that saves 30,000 units. But the tight setting misses 60 more frauds, and at an assumed average loss of 1,000 units per fraud that costs 60,000 units. On these figures the tight setting is more expensive overall, even though its precision is twice as good. The result could change with other numbers, and customer friction would also 111 count. The point is the method: compare settings on alerts, frauds caught, review cost and the loss from misses, together. SCENARIO — THE BETTER-LOOKING RULE At Oakridge Bank, a manager reviews two versions of a card rule. Version one raises 8,000 alerts a month and catches 400 frauds. Version two raises 2,500 alerts and catches 250 frauds. The manager, Helen Marsh, notes that version two has much higher precision and wants to adopt it because the analyst team is overloaded. Finance gives her two planning figures: a review costs about 5 currency units of analyst time, and the average card fraud that gets through costs about 400 units. Question.  On these figures, which version is cheaper, and what method should Helen use to decide? Answer.  Version one is cheaper, and Helen should compare total cost, not precision alone. Version two removes 5,500 alerts, which saves 27,500 units of review time. But it catches 150 fewer frauds a month, and 150 times 400 is 60,000 units of loss. Version two therefore costs about 32,500 units more each month before customer friction is counted. The method is to set the review time saved against the losses added, using the review cost per alert, the average loss per fraud and ideally some measure of customer friction. There is also a middle route. Helen could keep version one’s coverage but sort the alerts by risk, so that the highest-scoring get a full review and the lowest get a faster, lighter check or an automated customer message. This keeps recall while easing the load. The error to avoid is optimising the metric that is easiest to improve. 5.4.4  The cost of false positives A false positive is cheap to define and expensive to carry. The first cost is analyst time. The second is customer friction: a declined card, a held payment, a call from the bank, or a locked account. Customers who are repeatedly disturbed lose trust and learn to ignore warnings, which weakens the controls that rely on them. The aim is not zero false positives, which would mean missing fraud, but fewer unnecessary ones, handled faster, for example by an app message asking the customer to confirm a payment, with closure codes feeding the rule owner. 5.4.5  Queues, feedback and tuning governance Queue design decides how limited analyst time is spent. A good queue is prioritised, so that the highest-risk and most time-sensitive alerts come first. It is segmented by typology or channel, so that analysts build expertise. It has a route for escalation, so that a complex case goes to an experienced investigator, as Chapter 6.1 describes. Feedback is what makes detection improve. Every closed alert should record an outcome and a reason: confirmed fraud, genuine, customer error, or unclear. These outcomes return to the rule owners and the model team as the evidence for tuning. Tuning is the adjustment of thresholds, conditions and score cut-offs. It is also where good intentions create risk, because a change that improves the queue can quietly open a gap. Each change therefore needs a documented reason and an owner. It is tested on past data before release, approved by someone other than its author, and released in a way that can be rolled back. Its effect is checked afterwards against both alert volume and fraud caught. 5.4.6  Case study: The queue that was cut Composite case, fictional. Halvern Savings is a mid-sized retail bank. In the spring, its payment-fraud queue grew faster than the team, and the backlog reached several days at its worst. The head of operations, Gareth Lindqvist, set the fraud team a target of bringing the backlog under 112 control within a month. The team lead, Susan Achterberg, was under pressure from her manager and from customers who complained about held payments. Monday to Wednesday: A fraud strategy analyst reviewed the highest-volume rule, which held outbound payments to new payees above a set amount. Most alerts closed as genuine. The analyst raised the amount threshold substantially and added an exception for payees who had been paid before from that customer’s account. The change was made directly in the rule system at the end of a long Monday, and the change request recorded only the aim of shortening the queue, not what the new setting might miss. No second person reviewed it, and no wider test on past data was run. By Thursday the queue had fallen sharply, and Gareth reported the improvement upward. The following two weeks: A wave of account takeover began, in which criminals took over customer sessions and added new payees. The higher threshold meant that many of these payments no longer met the rule, and the exception for known payees let more through, because some receiving accounts had been paid before and so counted as familiar. The measure the team was watching was queue length, and it stayed short. Week three: Customers began to ring in. The complaints team noticed a rise in reports of payments customers did not make. A senior investigator, Wanjiru Kamau, linked the complaints and found that all the payments had passed through the changed rule. She showed that the earlier setting would have held the great majority of them. The rule was restored to its earlier setting within hours. The bank contacted customers, sent recall requests to the receiving institutions, reviewed every payment since the change and made the reports its obligations required (Chapter 6.5 and Module 7). Recovery was partial, as it usually is once money has moved on. What it cost: the customer losses the bank reimbursed were the largest part, followed by weeks of staff time reconstructing payments by hand, recall requests that recovered little, and lost trust among customers. Halvern then checked every other rule changed in the previous year, found two more loosened in the same way without testing, and rebuilt both. It also introduced a monthly sample of payments below the alert threshold, read by an analyst who had not set the threshold. Lessons: First, tuning is a change to a control and needs the same discipline as any other change: a documented reason, a test on past data, independent approval, a planned release and a check afterwards. Second, the measure a team watches shapes what it does, so queue length cannot be the only measure; indirect measures of missed fraud, such as fraud found by other routes and a sample of unalerted payments, would have shown the gap early. SCENARIO — WHAT SHOULD HAVE HAPPENED ON MONDAY Return to Halvern Savings. On the Monday, the strategy analyst has found that the new-payee rule is the largest source of alerts and wants to reduce them. Susan Achterberg asks her team what the right first step is before any threshold is changed. Three suggestions are made. The first is to raise the threshold now and monitor the queue. The second is to run the proposed new settings in monitor-only mode alongside the live rule and compare what each would have caught on the past month’s data. The third is to switch the rule off for a week and see what happens. Question.  Which is the sound first step, and why? Answer.  The second. Running the proposed settings in monitor-only mode, and testing them against past confirmed fraud, shows how many frauds the new version would have missed before any customer is exposed. It costs a little time and no risk. The change can then be approved on evidence, with an independent reviewer, and released with a planned check afterwards. 113 The first option changes a live control on the evidence of a shorter queue, which is the mistake that led to the loss. A sound practitioner also asks whether the queue can be eased in other ways while the analysis runs, such as sorting by risk or automating customer confirmation of low-risk payments. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — Alerts form a funnel, and a team should measure every step, with a sudden jump in alert volume treated as a signal to investigate. — Precision is the share of alerts that were real fraud, recall is the share of real fraud that was alerted, and overall accuracy misleads because fraud is rare. — Loosening a threshold raises recall and lowers precision, and the right setting balances missed-fraud losses against review cost and customer friction. — False positives cost analyst time and customer trust, and the aim is fewer unnecessary ones, not none. — Good queues are prioritised, segmented and timed, and closure codes feed evidence back to rule owners and model teams. — Tuning is a control change and needs a reason, a test on past data, independent approval, rollback and a check afterwards. 114
