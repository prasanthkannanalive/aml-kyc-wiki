Recovery and loss mitigation Once fraud has happened, the question becomes how much of the loss can be reduced. Recovery is rarely complete and often slow, and the first hours decide most of it. This chapter covers payment recall and freezes, card disputes and chargebacks, insurance, restitution and civil recovery, and working with law enforcement. Processes, scheme rules, time limits and legal remedies differ by firm, payment type and country, so the chapter explains concepts and reasoning. 6.4.1  Speed, recall and freezes Stolen money moves: funds land in a first account, are split, and are moved on quickly through further accounts or into cash or crypto-assets. Each hop makes recovery harder. Two ideas are worth separating. A payment recall is a request, sent from the paying institution to the receiving institution, asking that a payment be returned. It depends on the payment scheme’s rules and on the receiving institution’s willingness and ability to act. A freeze or restriction is an action by the institution that holds the account, stopping funds from leaving it while the matter is checked. The paying institution cannot freeze another institution’s account; it can only alert that institution and ask it to act. Because of this, the quality of the alert matters. A good alert to the receiving institution names the payment, the amount, the time, the account, the reason for the concern and a contact who can answer within the hour. Vague alerts are triaged as low priority, so institutions that deal with each other often should have standing contacts. The receiving institution then acts on its own authority, subject to its own legal duties and any law-enforcement request. 6.4.2  Chargebacks and disputes A chargeback is the process by which an issuing bank, on behalf of its cardholder, asks to reverse a card transaction because of a problem, and the merchant’s bank may defend or accept it. Detailed rules belong to the card schemes and change, so only the idea is described here. The cardholder disputes a transaction. The issuer reviews it and, if the claim fits a recognised reason, sends the dispute through the scheme. The merchant, through its bank, can respond with evidence that the sale was valid, such as proof of delivery or of authentication, and the scheme settles it or the dispute escalates. The issuer may credit the cardholder temporarily meanwhile. For fraud work, two points matter. First, the reason for the dispute must be right. A transaction the cardholder did not make is a different dispute from one the cardholder made but did not receive what was promised, and the evidence needed differs. Second, evidence decides. An issuer that can show authentication, and a merchant that can show delivery to the cardholder’s address, are better placed to defend a dispute (Chapter 3.4). Cardholders sometimes dispute transactions they made (friendly fraud, Chapter 3.4); treat that as a hypothesis to test, never a default. 6.4.3  Other recovery routes and law enforcement Not every loss comes back through the payment system. Insurance responds to some fraud losses but usually requires prompt notice, co-operation and preserved evidence, and a late notice or destroyed record can void a claim. Restitution lets a court that convicts an offender order compensation, and civil recovery lets the institution or a customer sue the fraudster or those who 132 received the proceeds, sometimes with early orders freezing or disclosing assets. Both are slow and depend on the offender having assets. Law enforcement may be the only body able to compel evidence, arrest offenders, and in some cases seize assets. The institution’s role is to report accurately, preserve evidence, and respond to lawful requests (Chapter 6.5). Practical co-operation rests on four habits. Report early when a matter involves ongoing harm or organised activity, using the channel the police prefer. Give a clear package: summary, timeline, account and device identifiers, and a named contact. Respond to formal requests through a single team that checks the legal basis, since disclosing customer data without a proper basis breaches data protection duties. And keep the customer informed, within what the police allow. SCENARIO — THE RECALL THAT WAITED FOR TUESDAY A customer of Larchmont Bank, Jun-ho Park, tells the branch on a Friday afternoon that he has paid his savings to a fraudster posing as a conveyancing solicitor. The branch plans to ask the fraud team for a recall on Tuesday, since Monday is a public holiday and recalls usually go in a batch. The branch manager says timing makes no difference, because the money has probably gone already. Mr Park asks whether anything can be done before the weekend ends. Question.  What is wrong with this plan? Answer.  The plan treats a time-critical step as a batch task. The first hours decide most recovery: if the money may already be moving, delay makes recovery less likely, and if it has not moved, a prompt alert could freeze it. The paying institution cannot freeze the receiving account, but it can alert the receiving institution at once so that it can act on its own authority. The sound plan is to escalate to the out-of-hours contact on the Friday, send the recall and an alert to the receiving institution straight away with full details, record the times, tell Mr Park honestly that recovery is uncertain, and report to the police. SCENARIO — THE DISPUTE AND THE LABEL Delmar Credit’s cardholder, Aisha Rahman, disputes a purchase from an online electronics seller, saying she did not make it. The issuer’s dispute team has the authentication record, which shows a successful step-up check on her registered phone, and a delivery record to her home address. The team lead wants to reject the dispute and mark Aisha a probable first-party fraudster. A colleague suggests first checking who else has access to her phone or receives parcels at that address. Aisha says she was at work. Question.  Can the team label Aisha a first-party fraudster on this evidence, and what should it do first? Answer.  No, and the colleague’s approach is the sound one. The authentication and delivery records weigh against an unauthorised-transaction claim, but they do not show who held the phone or who received the parcel. Moving straight to a label of first-party fraud skips the step of testing the explanation, and a wrong label can harm the customer and expose the institution to a complaint. The first step is to put the evidence to Aisha and ask about access to her phone and the delivery address. What follows depends on her answer. If a household member made the purchase, it is a customer conversation, not a fraud finding. If she cannot account for the records, the institution decides on the evidence, records its reasons and explains the outcome and her right to complain, with no label beyond what the evidence supports. 133 WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — Recovery depends on speed: act in minutes, not in batches, and keep out-of-hours contacts that work. — A recall is a request to the receiving institution; a freeze is that institution’s own action, so the quality of the alert decides how it is treated. — A chargeback turns on the right dispute reason and on evidence such as authentication and delivery records. — Insurance, restitution and civil recovery are slower routes with conditions and limits; insurers expect prompt notice and preserved evidence. — Law enforcement can compel evidence and seize assets, so report early with a clear package and respond to formal requests through one team. 134
