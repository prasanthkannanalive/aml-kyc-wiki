# Authorised push payment scams

In an authorised push payment scam, the customer does everything right in the technical sense. They log in, pass every check and send the money themselves. The fraud lies in why they sent it. This makes the scam hard to catch with controls built to spot a stranger using a stolen credential, and it explains why the industry has had to learn to read behaviour and context instead. This chapter explains how the scam runs, the psychology behind it, its main variants, what the institution can see, and which controls are realistic.


**3.1.1** What an authorised push payment scam is An authorised push payment scam, usually shortened to APP scam, is one in which a person is tricked into authorising a payment to a fraudster. “Push” means the customer instructs their bank to send money out, unlike a “pull” such as a card payment taken by a merchant. UK Finance, the UK banking industry body, describes it as a payment where either the recipient is not who the customer thought, or the purpose of the payment was fraudulent. The contrast with unauthorised fraud is the starting point for everything in this chapter. In unauthorised fraud, the account holder did not agree to the payment and a third party made it. In an APP scam, the account holder did agree. They were deceived, but the instruction is genuinely theirs. Module 7 explains how each regime treats the loss. UK Finance splits APP scams into two families. In a malicious payee scam, the customer pays the person they meant to pay, but that person never intended to deliver what was promised. Purchase, investment, romance and advance fee scams sit here. In a malicious redirection scam, the customer intends to pay a genuine party, such as a builder, a supplier or their own bank, but is steered to pay a fraudster’s account instead. Impersonation of bank staff, police or officials, invoice and mandate scams, and chief executive officer fraud sit here. **3.1.2** How a scam runs, and the levers it pulls An APP scam can be described in five stages. The bank sees nothing of the first two, and only hints at the third, so it usually has clear evidence only in the last two. Knowing the stages shows where the institution can intervene. The fraudster is not exploiting a weakness in the bank’s systems. They are exploiting ordinary human responses, and a handful of levers account for most of the effect. Urgency is the most common. The victim is told that the money is at risk now, that the offer ends today, or that the police need help before the suspect escapes. Urgency removes the time to pause or check. Authority is the second. The caller claims to be from the bank’s fraud team, the police, a tax office or a company the victim deals with. Trust is the third. In romance scams the fraudster invests weeks in becoming someone the victim wants to help. Fear, greed and hope are the fuel: fear of losing savings, hope of a better life, the wish to help someone who seems to need it. A fourth lever deserves its own mention: isolation. Fraudsters tell victims not to discuss the payment with family, and above all not to tell the bank the real reason. Coaching the victim to give a false payment purpose, such as “paying family”, is the fraudster’s defence against the bank’s controls, and it means a customer’s stated reason cannot be taken at face value.


**3.1.3** The main variants Each variant uses the same stages with a different story, and an analyst who knows the story can recognise it in a customer’s behaviour. Impersonation of bank staff or police. The victim receives a call or message, often with a number that appears to belong to the bank, claiming their account is under attack. They are told to move money to a “safe account” or to help an investigation by sending funds to a named account. The story turns the victim’s wish to protect their money into the means of losing it. UK Finance classifies this as malicious redirection because the victim believes they are dealing with their own bank or the authorities. The tell-tale feature is that a genuine bank never asks a customer to move money to keep it safe. Invoice and mandate scams. The victim, an individual or a business, is about to pay a genuine invoice, a solicitor’s completion funds or a regular supplier, and receives a message saying the bank details have changed. The message may come from a compromised email account or a convincing copy of one. The payer sends the money to the new account. The fraud is usually discovered when the genuine payee asks where the money is. Chapter 3.6 covers the corporate version in depth. Purchase, romance and advance fee scams are malicious payee scams: the customer pays a fraudster who never intends to deliver the goods, the relationship or the promised reward, and the sign is a payment to a stranger justified by a story the customer cannot verify.


**3.1.4** What the institution can see The institution sees a payment and a session, and knows a customer. A scam leaves traces in all three, and the analyst should think of red flags in those three groups. Payment behaviour is about the instruction itself. A new payee, followed at once by a large payment. A payment far above the customer’s normal size, or draining most of the balance. A series of payments in quick succession to the same new payee, or an unusual build-up of money into the account, such as savings moved in from elsewhere just before an outgoing payment. A payee whose name does not match the account, or a payee account that is recent. Payments to a payee type the customer has never used, such as a crypto-asset exchange. Session behaviour is about how the customer uses the channel. Logging in while on a phone call, which some mobile applications can detect. Remote access software running on the device is a strong signal that someone else is guiding the session. Customer behaviour is what staff and customer-contact channels hear. A customer who is agitated, secretive or reading from a script. One who cannot explain who the payee is or why the money is going there. One who says they were told to ignore the bank’s warnings. One who gives a payment purpose that does not fit, such as “family” for an account opened last week.


## Scenario — THE CALM CUSTOMER WITH A SCRIPT

Maribel Okafor, a retired customer of Larchmont Bank, has used mobile banking for years to pay small bills. On a Tuesday morning she tries to send nearly all of her savings to a new payee at another bank, with the purpose “family help”. The application records that she has been on a call for the whole session and that she copied the account details from her messages. The payee name does not match the account name. A payment-hold analyst, Tomasz Reyes, phones Mrs Okafor, who says she is happy and in a hurry, that nobody told her to do this, and that she does not want the bank interfering. **Question.** Should Tomasz release the payment because the customer is calm, consistent and insists it is her own decision? **Answer.** No.

Her calm and insistence do not outweigh the signals: a first-ever payment of this size, the draining of savings, a new payee whose name does not match, a call running throughout the session and a payment purpose that does not fit her history. Coached victims commonly say what Mrs Okafor said, because the fraudster has rehearsed it with them. A sound practitioner keeps the payment held, within whatever the institution’s terms allow, and moves from yes-or-no questions to open ones: who is the payee, how did you meet them, who else knows. If the customer still cannot explain, the case goes to a specialist, and the bank considers contacting a trusted contact where the customer has nominated one. The aim is to give the customer a moment away from the voice on the other line.


**3.1.5** Realistic controls No single control stops an APP scam, because the customer’s own hand makes the payment. The institution’s task is to add the right amount of friction at the right moment, and to leave genuine payments alone. The controls below work in layers. Customer warnings. A warning that names the scam type matching the payment purpose is generally judged more effective than a generic “beware of fraud” banner, because the customer recognises the story. The evidence on what works is still limited, so an institution should test its warnings and measure the result rather than assume. Warnings work best at the moment of payment, are short, and ask the customer to stop and do something specific, such as call a number they find themselves. Generic warnings that appear on every payment are soon ignored. Confirmation prompts and dynamic friction. For payments the system scores as risky, the institution can slow the customer down: a prompt that asks them to confirm the purpose, a warning tailored to the answer, a delay before release, or a request to use a different channel. The more specific the risk, the more friction is justified. Payee checks. Some payment systems offer a check that compares the name the customer typed with the name on the receiving account, and returns a match, a close match or a no match. The check catches mistyped details and some diverted invoices. A match shows only that the typed name agrees with the account name, not that the account holder is the intended payee in substance, so a match is reassuring but never proof of safety. Nor does the check stop a customer who has been talked into ignoring a warning. The rules are in Chapters 7.1 and 7.2. Holds and delays. The institution can hold a flagged payment while it speaks to the customer. A hold gives the customer time and gives staff a chance to ask questions. Where a hold is permitted at all depends on the payment scheme and the law, which Module 7 covers.


## Scenario — A WARNING FOR EVERY PAYMENT

Delmar Credit, a small payments firm, adds the same warning to every outgoing faster payment: “Be aware of scams. Only pay people you trust.” Many Delmar customers pay the same few people every week, so most of them see the same words several times a month. After six months the fraud team finds that most customers who later reported scams had clicked through the warning in a couple of seconds, and the warning has not changed the number of scam reports. The team can also see which payments were to new payees and which were not. The head of operations, Priya Nair, proposes to make the warning longer and to add a second tick-box confirmation on every payment. **Question.** Is a longer warning with a second tick-box the right fix? **Answer.** No.

The evidence shows that the generic warning has been learned and ignored, and adding more text and another tick-box to every payment will teach the same habit faster while irritating genuine customers. Friction applied equally to all payments is poor design because it does not match the warning to the risk. The better answer is to target. Ask for the payment purpose, use it to show a short warning about the matching scam type, and reserve slower steps, such as a delay or a staffed conversation, for payments with several risk signals. Delmar should then measure whether the targeted warnings change what customers do, using the outcomes of held payments and later reports, and adjust.


## What to take from this chapter
- An APP scam is a payment the customer genuinely authorised after being deceived, so the usual test of whether the customer made the instruction does not separate it from a legitimate payment
- The two families are malicious payee, where the payee is the fraudster, and malicious redirection, where the customer intends to pay a genuine party or their own bank
- Scams can be described in five stages, and the institution has clear evidence only in the last two, so behaviour and context must carry the detection
- Urgency, authority, trust and isolation are the levers, and coaching the victim to give a false payment purpose is the fraudster’s defence against bank questions
- Red flags fall into payment behaviour, session behaviour and customer behaviour, and no single one proves a scam; the combination does
- Controls work in layers: tailored warnings, dynamic friction, payee checks, holds where permitted and trained staff, with generic warnings on every payment the weakest of these.
