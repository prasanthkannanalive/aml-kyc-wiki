Dealing with customers Every fraud case has a person at its centre, and how the institution speaks to that person shapes the outcome as much as the technical work does. A frightened victim who is handled badly may stop co-operating, complain, or be scammed again. A customer who is wrongly suspected may leave and tell others. This chapter covers first contact, vulnerable customers, holds and delays, explaining decisions, and complaints and claims. Customer handling is practitioner method and varies between firms. 6.3.1  First contact and what to say First contact is usually a call, and it has one job: establish who you are speaking to, learn what happened, and stop further harm. Use a verified channel. If the institution calls the customer, it should not ask for passwords, full card numbers or one-time passcodes, because that is exactly what a fraudster would do. Offer a call-back on the number printed on the card. A sound opening has five parts. Say who you are and why you are calling. Check identity using the institution’s standard process. State what you have seen in neutral words: “We noticed a payment to a new payee and wanted to check it with you.” Ask an open question: “Can you tell me what you know about it?” Then listen. Scam victims often defend the payee because they have been coached; do not argue, take the account as given. Avoid language that places blame early, such as “why did you do that?”, and avoid reassurance you cannot back up, such as “we will definitely get it back”. A truthful sentence serves better: “We will do everything we can, and I will tell you what happens at each step.” Scripts work best as guides with required points. 6.3.2  Vulnerable customers In this guide, a working description is that a vulnerable customer is one who, because of their circumstances, is especially exposed to harm or to being treated unfairly. Causes include illness, bereavement, mental health, cognitive decline, low confidence with technology, or a life event such as redundancy; it can be passing. It is not a label on a person, and age alone is not vulnerability: an eighty-year-old who is clear, unhurried and acting on a settled plan is making a decision, not showing a weakness. In fraud work vulnerability matters in two ways. First, vulnerable customers are targeted, because scammers look for people who can be rushed, isolated or confused. Second, how the institution treats them is judged more closely, by regulators, by ombudsmen and in rules about who bears the loss. Chapter 7.1 describes how the UK reimbursement regime treats vulnerability in practice. Signs to watch for in a conversation include confusion about recent events, repeating the same story, saying someone else is directing the conversation, a third person audibly prompting in the background, unusual urgency, and distress. None is proof. Each is a reason to slow down and adapt. Adapting means speaking more slowly, using plain words, offering to continue with a trusted person present, agreeing a call-back time, not pressing for decisions, and, with the customer’s agreement, flagging the account so they need not repeat their circumstances. A vulnerable customer is still entitled to make decisions, and consent is normally the end of the matter. But consent is a safe basis for releasing a payment only if it is genuinely the customer’s own. 129 When there are signs that someone else is directing the customer, such as a script, an instruction to hide the payment from the bank or a voice prompting in the background, the instruction may not reflect the customer’s own free decision. Whether and for how long an institution may delay or refuse a payment against instruction depends on the account terms and the law (Module 7). The practical rule is to use the scam-intervention procedure, keep any delay as short as the checks need, and escalate rather than decide alone. SCENARIO — THE CUSTOMER WITH A SCRIPT Delmar Credit’s fraud team calls Mr Gerald Whitcombe, aged eighty-one, about a large transfer to a new payee. He says the payment is fine and the payee is a government officer who is helping him with a tax matter. He sounds anxious and says he has been told not to discuss it with the bank, and a second voice can be heard giving instructions. The agent’s script says to confirm consent and release the payment if consent is given. Question.  What should the agent do? Answer.  The agent should not simply release the payment on the strength of consent. Mr Whitcombe’s age is not the reason. The signs are those of a coached victim of an impersonation scam: a story about officials, an instruction not to speak to the bank, urgency and a prompting voice. Consent given under that influence may not be his own. The script is a guide, and the situation calls for the scam-intervention procedure, at a calm pace and with care for his dignity. The agent should hold the payment briefly, for only as long as the checks need, and escalate the same day to a senior colleague who can decide within the account terms and the law (Module 7), avoiding accusation: “Many people have been contacted by someone pretending to be an official, and we want to be certain this is safe.” Offer to call him back on a number he knows, and consider contacting a trusted person with his consent. 6.3.3  Holds, explaining decisions, complaints and claims A hold, a restriction or a declined claim affects the customer immediately. A hold should be proportionate to the risk, applied for the shortest time that lets the check be done, and reviewed on a schedule. A hold with no owner and no review date becomes a complaint. Tell the customer what is happening in plain words: what has been held, why in general terms, what the customer needs to do, and when they will next hear from you. Do not disclose the details of detection rules, because that helps fraudsters, and do not tip off a person about a report that the law keeps confidential (Chapter 6.5). Describe the outcome and the next step without describing the mechanism: “We have restricted the card while we check some recent activity; here is how to get a replacement.” If a claim is declined, give the decision, the main reason in language the customer can follow, the evidence it rested on where it can be shared, and what the customer can do next, including how to complain and, where one exists, how to take the matter to an independent body. Complaints deserve the same care. Most customers who complain about fraud handling are complaining about delay, lack of information, being disbelieved or being passed between teams, so a complaint is useful data. A sound process acknowledges it quickly, assigns someone not involved in the original decision, looks at the evidence afresh, answers each point and feeds common causes back to the process owner (Module 7 covers regulatory time limits). Claims are decided on written criteria applied consistently: was the payment authorised, did the customer follow the required steps, is the customer within the scope of a scheme. Some claims will turn out to be false, but none is assumed false; each is tested against the evidence. 130 SCENARIO — THE HOLD NOBODY REVIEWED Larchmont Bank places a hold on the account of a small business owner, Sofia Duarte, after an alert on several incoming payments. The case owner moves to another team and the case is not reassigned. Eleven days later Sofia calls, unable to pay her suppliers. The agent sees only a restriction code and “under review”, and is tempted to say it is routine and will be lifted soon. Question.  What should the agent do, and what does the case show about the process? Answer.  The agent should not promise a lifting date that nobody has set. The sound response is to apologise for the lack of contact, say truthfully what can be said, escalate immediately to find the owner or reassign the case, and agree a time by which she will be called back, logging the contact and the complaint. The agent should not speculate about the reason or the outcome. The process failure is that a hold had no owner, no review date and no communication plan. Every hold needs all three. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — First contact uses a verified channel, never asks for credentials or passcodes, states what was seen in neutral words and starts with an open question. — Vulnerability is a state, not a label, and signs such as confusion, urgency or a prompting voice are reasons to slow down and adapt, not proof of anything. — A customer’s consent given under coaching or pressure may not be their own, so a suspicious payment is held only briefly and the decision is escalated. — Every hold needs an owner, a review date and a communication plan, and the customer should be told what is happening in plain words. — Explain decisions with the outcome, the main reason, the next step and the route to complain, without disclosing detection rules or confidential reports. — Complaints are data about the process; claims are decided on written, consistent criteria and a claim is tested against the evidence and never assumed to be false. 131
