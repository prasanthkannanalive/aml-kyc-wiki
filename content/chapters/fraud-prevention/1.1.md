# What fraud is and what it is not

Fraud is easy to describe in a sentence and hard to separate from its neighbours. A wrong payment, a bad loan, a stolen card and a laundered balance can all look the same on a loss report. Yet each needs a different response, a different owner and sometimes a different legal route. This chapter gives the ideas that most legal systems share about fraud, then separates fraud from error, theft, money laundering and credit loss, and ends with the difference between civil and criminal proceedings.


**1.1.1** The elements common to fraud offences Laws differ from country to country, and this guide does not explain any single statute. Even so, it uses a four-part working model, which most fraud definitions combine in some form. An analyst who can name the four parts can read almost any case file, and can see what evidence is missing. The first is deception. Someone makes a false statement, hides a fact that they had a duty to disclose, or abuses a position of trust. The deception can be aimed at a person, such as a customer or a clerk, or at a process, such as an online application form. The second is dishonesty or intent. The person knew what they were doing and meant to mislead. A mistake is not enough. The third is loss, or the risk of loss. Someone else is, or could be, worse off. In many systems the offence is complete even if the money never moves, which is why attempted fraud still matters. The fourth is benefit. Intent is almost never admitted, so it is shown through conduct: what the person did, how often, what they hid and what they gained. A case note should record evidence against each element; a note that says only “fraud suspected” gives an investigator nothing to test. Evidence should be kept so that it would stand in any proceeding: a criminal case, where the state prosecutes to a high standard of proof, or a civil claim, where the firm sues to recover money to a lower one. Do not promise a customer a criminal outcome, and do not let a slow prosecution hold up what the firm can do at once, such as stopping further loss. This guide teaches how fraud works in practice and is not legal advice.


**1.1.2** Fraud and error Fraud and error can produce an identical loss. A supplier is paid twice. A customer sends money to the wrong account number. What separates them is intent, and intent cannot be read from the loss alone. Analysts infer intent from patterns. A single duplicate payment, noticed and reversed within days, points to error. The same duplicate repeated across months, just under an approval limit, points elsewhere. Useful signs are repetition, concealment, a benefit to the person handling the item, and a failure to correct once the problem is noticed. That last sign matters. An honest error can turn into something else after discovery. A customer who is credited money by mistake and spends it knowing the bank got it wrong, or a supplier who keeps an overpayment and denies receiving it, has moved from accident to conduct that needs a different label. The reverse mistake is just as costly. Calling an error “fraud” can freeze accounts, put a customer on a shared fraud database and bring staff discipline. Until the evidence is in, the right wording is “suspected”, and the label should be revisited when facts change. Contrast two cases. In one, a clerk keys the wrong invoice number and the supplier returns the money within a week: nothing was hidden and nobody gained. In the other, a clerk pays the same invoice twice every month for six months, just under the approval limit, to a supplier whose contact is the clerk’s relative. The amounts are similar. The evidence is not.


**1.1.3** Fraud, theft, money laundering and credit loss Four other terms are often confused with fraud. Each describes a different problem, and mixing them up sends cases to the wrong team. Theft is the taking of property without the owner’s consent. No deception is needed. Fraud, by contrast, gets the owner to hand property over, or gets a system to release it, by deceiving someone. A thief who takes a card from a bag has stolen; the person who later uses the card details to buy goods online is committing fraud with the stolen data. Money laundering is the concealment of where criminal proceeds came from, so that they can be used as if they were clean. Fraud creates proceeds; laundering hides them. The two meet in mule accounts, which receive and move fraud proceeds and are covered in Chapter 3.3. Credit loss arises when a borrower fails to repay a loan that was granted in good faith. It is a normal cost of lending. It becomes a fraud question when the loan should never have been granted on the true facts, for example because the borrower lied about income, or never meant to repay. The boundary case that matters most is the loan that defaults early. If it is booked as credit loss without a fraud review, the firm never learns that its application checks were beaten. Classification is a control in its own right.


## Scenario — THE DOUBLE PAYMENT

Delmar Credit pays its suppliers through an automated batch. During a month-end rush, a payments clerk, Anil Rao, releases a payment of GBP 4,800 to Castlegate Print Works twice, after the screen froze and he pressed the release button again. Castlegate’s accounts team does not report the second payment. Two months later a reconciliation finds it. Castlegate says the money arrived and was “applied to future work”. Anil says he has never worked with Castlegate staff outside the invoice process. A colleague suggests the loss should be written off as an operational error and the case closed. **Question.** Should the case be closed as an error? **Answer.** Not yet. The duplicate release looks like an error on Anil’s side, and nothing suggests he gained. But the supplier’s conduct is different.

Castlegate received money it was not owed, did not tell Delmar, and now describes it as a prepayment. Whether that is an honest misunderstanding or a decision to keep the money depends on what Castlegate knew and when, and that has not been established. A sound practitioner records the loss as an operational error on the payment side, and separately asks for the overpayment back in writing. If Castlegate repays, the case closes cleanly. If it refuses or goes silent, the facts change and the case can be reassessed for dishonest retention. The common error is to treat the whole matter as a clerk’s mistake and never test what the recipient did once it noticed.


## Scenario — THE EARLY DEFAULT

Larchmont Bank approves a personal loan for a new customer, Mara Voss, after an online application. Mara makes no repayments at all. The credit team writes the loan off as credit loss after the standard collection period. A fraud analyst later notices that the income stated on the application matches no pay records, that the employer’s phone number is a mobile that now rings out, and that the loan proceeds were moved on within a day to an account at another bank. The credit team says the file is closed and a write-off is a credit matter, not a fraud matter. **Question.** Is the credit team right that this is only credit loss? **Answer.** No. Credit loss describes a borrower who meant to repay and could not.

Here the application contained statements that appear to be false, the evidence points to intent through the unreachable employer and the same-day movement of funds, and there is a clear benefit. That is deception, probable intent, loss and benefit, the four ideas from Topic 1.1.1. The write-off is an accounting step; it does not decide what the event was. A sound practitioner reopens the file as a suspected application fraud, records the evidence against each element, and asks whether other applications share the same employer or destination account. The loss should be reclassified in management reports too. The common error is to let the accounting treatment decide the risk classification.


> **Exam trap:** The single most-tested distinction in fraud is intent. Fraud and error can cost the company the same amount — the difference is that fraud requires deliberate deception. When a question asks "is this fraud?", look for evidence of intent: repetition, concealment, personal benefit, and failure to correct when challenged. Until the evidence supports a label, use "suspected" — the label triggers freezes, database entries and staff action.

## What to take from this chapter
- This guide uses four working ideas, which most fraud definitions combine in some form: deception, dishonesty or intent, loss or risk of loss, and benefit
- Fraud and error can cost the same; the difference is intent, which is inferred from repetition, concealment, benefit and failure to correct
- Use the word “suspected” until the evidence supports a label, because the label triggers freezes, database entries and staff action
- Theft takes property without deception, fraud obtains it by deception, laundering hides the origin of proceeds, and credit loss is honest non-repayment
- A defaulted loan that was obtained on false statements is fraud booked as credit loss, and misclassification hides the problem
- Preserve evidence for both criminal and civil routes, and do not wait for a prosecution before stopping loss.
