The fraud lifecycle and the language of loss Fraud teams talk in a compact vocabulary: gross loss, net loss, attempts, false positives, basis points. These words appear in every management information (MI) pack and every committee paper. Misusing them leads to bad decisions. This chapter describes the four stages of the fraud lifecycle, defines the main terms of loss and detection with worked examples, shows how to read a fraud MI pack, and applies it all to a composite case. 1.5.1  Prevent, detect, respond, recover Fraud management is often described as four stages that follow one another around each event. They also repeat: what the firm learns from one case should feed the next prevention step. Prevent means stopping the fraud before it starts, by making it harder, less rewarding or more likely to be noticed, for example through customer authentication, application checks and access controls. Detect means finding fraud that has got through or is under way, through rules and models, staff reviews, tips and reconciliations. Respond means acting once fraud is suspected or confirmed: holding a payment, blocking a card, contacting the customer, investigating and reporting as the rules require. Recover means getting money back and repairing harm: asking the receiving bank to return funds, claiming against insurers or third parties, reimbursing customers where the rules require it, and fixing the weakness found. The stages trade off: stronger prevention adds friction for honest customers, and a programme that spends only on detection finds fraud too late. They also give a way to read a single case. If a stolen card is used at a retailer, prevention is the authentication step the fraudster passed, detection is the rule that fires on the third purchase, response is the block and the call to the customer, and recovery is the chargeback claim. If the loss is large, ask which stage failed first, because the fix differs: a prevention failure needs a stronger check, a detection failure a better signal, a response failure faster action, and a recovery failure better evidence and quicker claims. 1.5.2  Gross loss, net loss, attempted and successful fraud Four terms recur in every report. Attempted fraud is a try that was stopped before money left or credit was granted. Successful fraud is a try that got through and caused a loss at the time. Counting both shows the pressure on the firm; counting only successful fraud shows what leaked. Rising attempts alongside steady successful fraud suggest controls are holding. Gross loss is the full amount lost to successful fraud before anything is recovered or reimbursed by anyone else. Net loss is gross loss after recoveries, insurance and similar offsets, and it is the figure the firm actually bears. Firms differ on what they deduct, so a pack should always state the definition. Reimbursing a customer is a separate matter: the firm pays the customer and bears the loss itself, and Module 7 explains how the rules allocate it. A worked example with invented figures follows. The share stopped and the share recovered tell different stories about prevention and about response: the firm recovered 16% of its gross loss (40,000 divided by 250,000), which says how well it responded after the event, while the 78% stopped says how well it prevented. Timing is a trap. Customers often report fraud days or weeks after the event, and recoveries arrive later still, so a pack read soon after month end understates gross loss for that month. SCENARIO — THE TWO BIGGEST NUMBERS At the fraud committee of Delmar Credit, the head of fraud presents the quarter’s results. Gross loss to fraud, including the amounts the bank reimbursed to customers, was GBP 1,000,000. Recoveries from receiving banks and insurers were GBP 150,000, and customer reimbursements accounted for GBP 600,000 of the gross figure. A director argues that “the real loss is only GBP 400,000, because the bank refunded GBP 600,000 out of its fraud reserve rather than losing it”. The finance lead says the quarter’s net loss is GBP 850,000. The head of fraud is unsure which of them is right. Question.  Which view of the loss is correct, and what is wrong with the director’s argument? Answer.  On the definition in Topic 1.5.2, net loss is gross loss less recoveries, so GBP 850,000 is the net loss. The director has confused reimbursement with recovery. Reimbursements are part of the gross loss and part of the bank’s cost, not an offset to it. Money paid from a reserve is still money the firm has lost; the reserve only changes when the cost is recognised in the accounts. The head of fraud should present gross loss, recoveries, net loss and customer reimbursements as separate lines, each defined. Netting off money from any source other than a recovery shrinks the figure without shrinking the loss. 1.5.3  False positives, customer friction and basis points Detection systems raise alerts. An alert is a flag that a payment, account or application deserves a closer look. A true positive is an alert on genuine fraud. A false positive is an alert on legitimate activity. A false negative is fraud that raised no alert. A true negative is legitimate activity that raised none. The detection rate is the share of all fraud that the system caught. The false positive ratio is the number of false alerts for each true one, so a ratio of 20 to 1 means twenty genuine items reviewed for each fraud found. Precision is the share of alerts that were right. There is always a trade-off. If the system flags more, it catches more fraud and creates more false positives; if it flags less, there are fewer false alerts and more missed fraud. Chapter 5.4 covers how to tune a threshold with evidence. False positives are costly in two ways. Each takes analyst time. And each genuine customer whose payment is held or card declined experiences customer friction: delay, inconvenience and sometimes lost business. A fraud function that treats friction as free will over-alert. A rule raises 2,000 alerts in a month, of which 100 are fraud: precision is 5%, and the false positive ratio is 19 to 1. If the team can review only 1,000 alerts in no particular order, about half the fraud sits unreviewed. Ranking the queue by risk finds more with the capacity the team has. A basis point (bp) is one hundredth of one per cent. Fraud teams use basis points of loss to compare losses with the volume of business, so that a growing firm is not judged against a smaller one. The formula is: loss divided by volume, multiplied by 10,000. The volume must match the loss: card fraud losses are divided by card spend, payment fraud by payment value, lending fraud by balances lent. Suppose a firm loses GBP 600,000 to card fraud in a month during which 27 cardholders spend GBP 2,000,000,000. The loss is 3 bps. If the next month brings 4 bps, the firm needs to ask whether the loss grew, the spend shrank, or both. 1.5.4  Reading a fraud MI pack Management information (MI) is the regular report that tells committees how fraud is doing. Begin with definitions: is “loss” gross or net, attempted or successful, and does the pack include scams where the customer authorised the payment? Next check the denominator and period. Is a movement in loss a real change or a change in volume? Recent months are almost always understated. Then look at the shape, not just the total. Break it down by product, channel, typology and size, because a flat total can hide a doubling in one channel offset by a fall in another. Then read the detection and friction measures next to the loss: falling losses with a surge in false positives and complaints could mean the firm has simply tightened controls and is stopping genuine customers. Finally, a good pack ends with actions, owners and dates. 1.5.5  Case study: The quiet quarter at Larchmont Bank Composite case, fictional. Larchmont Bank launched an instant-payments feature in its mobile app at the start of a quarter. Its fraud strategy team had tested the controls in a pilot, and the launch went ahead on schedule with the same payment rules the bank used for its older, slower channels. Weeks 1 to 4. The first monthly MI pack showed fraud on the new feature at a very low level, only a few small cases. The head of fraud told the committee that the launch had been a success. In the same month the contact centre logged a slow rise in calls from customers who said they had been tricked into sending money to a “safe account”, but these were logged as complaints and not passed to fraud operations. Fraud operations noticed more new payees set up late at night, and first payments made within minutes of the payee being created. Alert volume rose, but the team treated it as normal launch noise. Weeks 5 to 8. The second pack showed the first month’s loss had nearly tripled, because customer reports of scams arrive days or weeks after the payments, and the earlier month was now complete. An analyst, Rhea Okonkwo, pointed out that the first pack had shown a figure for a month that was only partly reported. She asked the analytics team to compare attempted-fraud counts, which were available at once, across channels. Attempts were rising faster on the new feature than elsewhere, and most successful cases involved payments to accounts at other banks opened a short time before. The receiving accounts looked like mule accounts, a point Chapter 3.3 covers. What the bank missed. Three early signals had been available. The complaints about “safe account” calls were leading indicators and sat in the wrong queue. The pilot had tested for stolen-credential fraud, the risk the bank knew, and had not tested for customers being persuaded to pay. And the first MI pack treated the latest month as complete. Together they let the problem grow for about a month with no one owning it: the contact centre held the complaints, fraud operations the payee behaviour, and analytics the attempt counts, and no one held all three views. Response. The head of fraud convened an incident call. The bank added a short delay and a plain-language warning to first payments to new payees, tightened limits on the new feature for new customers, and asked contact centre staff to pass all scam-related calls to fraud operations. Investigations began a review of the receiving accounts and passed the details to the AML team. 28 The bank asked the receiving banks to hold and return funds, a step that works best within hours, and recovered only part of the loss. Customers were treated under the reimbursement rules, which Module 7 sets out. The bank bore the net loss on cases it could not recover, the staff time of an incident, complaints and a slower rollout. No new technology was needed to see the first signs; they were in data the bank already held. Lessons. Include leading indicators, such as attempts, complaint themes and new-payee behaviour, in the MI pack for any new channel. Test new products against scams where customers are deceived, not only against stolen credentials. Mark the latest period as provisional. Give every signal an owner, and route customer reports of scams to the fraud team on the day they arrive. Set a review point soon after launch where the bank decides, on stated measures, whether to tighten controls. SCENARIO — THE DECISION AT WEEK EIGHT It is the end of week eight at Larchmont Bank. Rhea Okonkwo has the second MI pack in front of her. It shows a low net loss for the instant-payments feature in the current month, which is incomplete, and a rise of almost threefold for the previous month, which is now complete. Attempts on the new feature are rising faster than on older channels. The contact centre has logged a theme of “safe account” calls. A colleague argues that the bank should wait for the current month to complete before deciding whether to tighten the controls, so that the decision is based on full data. Question.  Should Rhea wait for the month to complete? Answer.  No. The low current-month figure reflects late reporting, not safety, and the completed month already shows the trend. Attempts and complaint themes are leading indicators that are available now. Waiting for complete loss data means accepting another month of loss to gain precision the decision does not need. The cost of a temporary control, such as a short delay on first payments to new payees, is small compared with the growth the data already shows. Rhea should escalate with the evidence, propose time-limited controls with a review date and named measures, and mark the current month as provisional. Holding action until the lagging figure confirms what the leading figures already show would repeat the bank’s first-month mistake. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — The four stages of the fraud lifecycle are prevent, detect, respond and recover, and what is learned in one case should feed the next prevention step. — Attempted fraud was stopped before loss, successful fraud caused a loss, gross loss is before recoveries and net loss is after recoveries. — Reimbursing a customer is how a firm bears a loss; it is not a recovery and does not reduce net loss. — A false positive is an alert on legitimate activity, and every false positive costs analyst time and causes customer friction. — Basis points of loss equal loss divided by volume, multiplied by 10,000, and the volume must match the loss. — Read an MI pack by checking definitions, denominators, period completeness, breakdowns and friction measures before the totals. — Recent periods in a fraud report are almost always incomplete, and leading indicators such as attempts and complaint themes should drive early action. 29 MODULE 2 The fraud risk management programme Fraud controls bought one at a time tend to protect last year’s problem.
