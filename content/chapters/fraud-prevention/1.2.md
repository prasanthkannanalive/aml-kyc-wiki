# Why people commit fraud

Controls are designed against people, so a fraud professional needs a working theory of motive. Two models are used throughout the field: the fraud triangle and the fraud diamond. Both were developed for occupational fraud, committed by employees against their employers, but the logic helps with customers and outsiders too. This chapter sets out both models, explains the behavioural red flags that the ACFE reports, and shows how to turn the models into control choices without profiling people.


**1.2.1** The fraud triangle The fraud triangle is linked to the criminologist Dr Donald Cressey, whose research on embezzlers led him to call them “trust violators”: people who held a position of trust and broke it. The ACFE describes the triangle as three elements which, when all present, make fraud highly likely. The first element is pressure, often described as an unshareable financial need. The person has a problem, such as debt, a failing business or a lifestyle they cannot fund, that they feel unable to tell anyone about. The second is perceived opportunity. The person believes they can take the money, and not be caught, because of weak checks, unwatched access or too much trust. What counts is what the person believes about the controls. The third is rationalisation. The person finds a way to see the act as acceptable: “I am only borrowing it”, “they underpay me”, “no one is hurt”. Rationalisation lets someone who sees themselves as honest act dishonestly. Element What it means What an institution can influence Pressure A need the person feels they cannot share Pay and workload, support for staff in difficulty, a culture where problems can be raised Opportunity A believed chance to act without being caught Access rights, segregation of duties, supervision, review and monitoring Rationalisation A story that makes the act acceptable Tone from the top, clear rules, consistent discipline, fair treatment The three sides of the fraud triangle and where the institution has influence. An example shows the three sides together. A refunds clerk with a gambling debt feels pressure he cannot share. He alone sets up and releases customer refunds and nobody reviews the log, which is the opportunity. He tells himself he will repay from his next bonus, which is the rationalisation. Take away any one side and the case becomes far less likely. An institution can seldom see the first and third sides in a particular person. It can see and change the second.


**1.2.2** The fraud diamond The fraud diamond adds a fourth element to the triangle. It was proposed by David T. Wolfe and Dana R. Hermanson in The CPA Journal. The new element is capability: the personal traits and skills that let a person recognise an opening and use it, more than once, without being stopped. The argument is that pressure, opportunity and rationalisation can all be present and nothing happens unless the person can act on them. Capability helps explain why many insider cases come from people who design, run or review the process. You may see references to a “fraud pentagon” in other literature. This guide does not use it. Consider two clerks with the same debt and the same unwatched access. One does not understand the payment system well enough to see how an adjustment could be hidden. The other helped to 17 configure it and knows which exceptions are reviewed. The second has capability, and is the one more likely to act and to keep going. Roles that combine deep system knowledge, authority and little supervision, such as system administrators and long-serving reconciliation staff, deserve independent review. A model is useful when it changes what the institution does, so ask of any process which sides are in play and which the firm can reach. Pressure is reached by realistic targets and safe speaking-up channels. Opportunity is reached by segregation of duties, dual authorisation and access reviews. Rationalisation is reached by visible, consistent discipline. Capability is reached by rotation, mandatory leave and independent testing of high-trust roles. A refund desk where agents refund unapproved up to a set value, and two long-serving agents alone understand the macros, can be fixed without looking at anyone’s finances: approval above a lower value, a weekly refund report reviewed outside the desk, and rotation of the macro owners. Controls should apply to roles and processes, not to personal circumstances. Two cautions apply to both models. They describe individuals who commit occupational fraud, not organised criminal groups, where fraud is an enterprise and motive is simpler. And they explain cases after the event; they cannot identify who will commit fraud, and watching every staff member in debt would be unfair, intrusive and, in many places, unlawful.


**1.2.3** Behavioural red flags A behavioural red flag is a change in conduct, or a feature of a person’s circumstances, that is associated with a higher chance of fraud. The ACFE reports that most perpetrators in its occupational fraud study showed at least one such flag. The flags the ACFE links to the largest losses include: — excessive internal pressure, for example from targets or from a manager — past legal problems — an unusually close relationship with a vendor or customer — refusing to take vacation — a “wheeler-dealer” attitude, meaning a person who bends rules and deals freely Each flag maps to a side of the triangle or diamond. Excessive pressure is the pressure side. A refusal to take leave often signals a fear that someone covering the job will find something, which links to opportunity and concealment. A very close tie to a vendor or customer creates both opportunity and a way to justify the act. The discipline is to treat a flag as a reason to look, never as proof. Honest staff also work too hard, hold difficult histories and get on well with suppliers. A flag justifies a review of the person’s controls and activity, done under the firm’s policy and with proper care for fairness and privacy. It does not justify an accusation, and it does not replace evidence.


## Scenario — THE MANAGER WHO NEVER TAKES LEAVE

At Larchmont Bank, Hollis Grant has managed the supplier payments desk for eleven years. He has not taken more than two consecutive days off in all that time. He personally onboards new suppliers and is on first-name terms with several of them. A new internal auditor notes that Hollis seems under heavy pressure from a restructuring that may merge his desk with another. The head of operations says Hollis is the most loyal member of staff and that raising questions would insult him. No payment has been identified as wrong. **Question.** What should the auditor do? **Answer.** The auditor should recommend a review of the desk’s controls and recent supplier activity, not an investigation of Hollis.

Several recognised flags are present together: leave avoidance, close supplier relationships, concentrated control over onboarding, and pressure. None proves anything, and the absence of a known wrong payment does not clear the desk, since a scheme that is working would not show up that way. A sound approach is to apply controls that do not depend on suspicion: enforce a period of continuous leave during which someone else runs the desk, separate supplier setup from payment release, and sample payments to recently added suppliers. That treats Hollis like everyone else and tests the process. The common error is to let loyalty or the fear of giving offence decide whether a control is applied.


## Scenario — THE FRAUD NOBODY COULD HAVE PREDICTED

After a teller at a Delmar Credit branch is found to have diverted customer cash over several months, the review team meets. A senior manager says the case shows that fraud cannot be prevented: the teller had personal debts, no policy could have known about them, and the bank has no right to inspect staff finances. He proposes to close the review with a note that the loss was unforeseeable. The teller worked alone at the cash drawer and her work was never independently counted. She kept part of the cash from some customer deposits and entered a matching reversal on the till against each one, so her drawer balanced each day. Nobody else approved reversals. **Question.** Is the manager right that nothing could have been done? **Answer.** No.

He is right that the bank could not have seen the pressure side, and wrong to stop there. The triangle has two other sides that the bank controls. Opportunity was wide: one person handled the cash with no independent count. Rationalisation is also open to influence through tone and consistent enforcement. The review should focus on opportunity because it is the side the firm can change. A sound review notes that the drawer balanced only because the same teller made the matching reversals, and introduces independent cash counts, dual approval of reversals and unannounced checks. The common error is to treat motive as the root cause when the cheapest fixes sit on the side the firm owns.


## What to take from this chapter
- The fraud triangle, linked to Dr Donald Cressey, has three elements: pressure, perceived opportunity and rationalisation
- The fraud diamond, from Wolfe and Hermanson, adds capability, the traits and skills that let a person act on an opening
- The fraud pentagon is not used in this guide, so it is not tested here
- The models explain occupational fraud and are not predictive tools, so they must not be used to profile individuals
- The ACFE’s highest-loss red flags are excessive internal pressure, past legal problems, a close vendor or customer relationship, refusing vacation and a wheeler-dealer attitude
- A red flag justifies a review of controls and activity, never an accusation on its own
- Opportunity is the side of the triangle an institution controls most directly, so most preventive design targets it.
