Where fraud sits in a financial institution Fraud is not a single job or a single department. It arrives from three directions, touches several other risk disciplines, and is handled by a mix of specialist teams. When a case lands on the wrong desk, or nobody owns the gap between two teams, losses grow. This chapter classifies fraud by where it comes from, compares fraud with its neighbouring risks, describes the roles in a fraud function and the measures used to manage it, and places fraud among the three lines. 1.4.1  Fraud by origin: first-party, third-party and insider The first useful question about any fraud is: who is the source? Three answers cover most cases. First-party fraud has its origin in the customer. The genuine account holder, using their own identity, lies on an application, misuses a facility, or plans from the start not to repay. Definitions of “first-party” vary between firms and bodies, so treat this as the working meaning used in this guide. A customer who is the victim of a scam is not a first-party fraudster, even though they authorised the payment. Third-party fraud has its origin outside the firm and outside the customer relationship. An outsider impersonates a customer, takes over an account, uses stolen card details, or deceives a customer into paying them. The firm and the genuine customer are both victims. Insider fraud has its origin in staff or other people with trusted access, such as contractors. An employee diverts funds, manipulates records, or helps outsiders. Origin Who acts Who is deceived Examples First-party The genuine customer The firm False income on a loan application; planned non-repayment Third-party An outsider The customer, the firm, or both Account takeover; card fraud; impersonation scams Insider Staff or trusted contractors The firm, sometimes customers Diverting funds; abusing system access; helping outsiders Fraud classified by origin. Hybrids exist. An insider may help an outsider, and a customer may knowingly lend an account to a mule operation. The origin label must follow the evidence and change when facts change. A loan that defaults in the first month is first-party if the borrower lied about income in their own name, third-party if an outsider used a stolen identity, and has an insider element if a loan officer altered the file. That is why the origin label sits on the case, not on the product. Anti-money laundering (AML) asks whether the firm is being used to hide the proceeds of crime; mule accounts are where the disciplines meet. Cyber security asks whether the firm’s systems and data are protected from attack. A breach of customer data may be the first stage of a later fraud. Credit risk asks whether borrowers will repay; fraud asks whether the borrower, or the application, was real. Operational risk covers loss from failed processes, people, systems and external events, and fraud is one of its causes. 23 The practical lesson is to share information early. A fraud team that sees a mule pattern tells the AML team; a cyber team that finds stolen credentials tells the fraud team; a credit team that sees a spike in first-payment defaults asks the fraud team to review the applications. A single campaign can touch all five: stolen customer data, scam calls built on it, accounts opened with false documents to receive the money, and rapid onward movement. If each team keeps its own log, nobody sees one campaign; if they share indicators such as phone numbers, device identifiers and beneficiary accounts, each team can use the others’ findings. SCENARIO — WHOSE CASE IS IT? A customer of Delmar Credit, Ines Walker, tells the contact centre that she paid a GBP 1,800 deposit for a rented flat she found on a listings site. She transferred it to the “landlord” after exchanging messages for a week. The flat did not exist and the landlord has stopped replying. The contact centre logs the matter as “customer error” and sends it to complaints. A fraud team member says it is a fraud case, but a colleague argues it is first-party fraud because Ines authorised the transfer herself. Question.  How should the case be classified by origin? Answer.  It is third-party fraud. The origin is an outsider who advertised a flat that did not exist and deceived Ines into paying. Ines is a victim, not a perpetrator, and the fact that she authorised the payment does not make her the source of the fraud. It is also not “customer error”, because the loss resulted from deliberate deception by someone else. The log should be corrected. A sound handler routes the matter to the fraud team as an authorised-payment scam, starts the process of contacting the receiving bank, and tells the AML team about the receiving account. Origin means who started the deception, not who pressed the button, and “authorised by the customer” is not the same as “caused by the customer”. 1.4.3  Roles and performance measures in a fraud function Fraud functions differ in size and naming, but most contain the same kinds of work: strategy and policy, which sets the approach and risk appetite; operations, which reviews alerts, contacts customers and decides to pay, hold or decline; investigations, which builds cases on complex, repeated or insider fraud; analytics, which builds and tests rules and models; intelligence, which tracks external threats and shares information; claims and recoveries; and fraud risk oversight, which challenges the front line. In a small firm one person may do several. The function is managed by measures that tell management whether the team is doing its job well, or only quickly. Loss measures are defined in Chapter 1.5. Detection measures show how much fraud the controls find and how long a fraud ran before it was noticed. Efficiency measures show alert volumes and time from alert to decision; rewarding speed alone can lead staff to close alerts without looking. Quality measures check a sample of closed alerts, because a fast queue with poor quality is worse than a slow queue with good quality. Customer measures show how many genuine people had a payment held and how many complained. Recovery measures show how much was recovered. No single measure is enough, and a good pack shows them in groups so that one improving number cannot hide a worsening one. 1.4.4  Where fraud sits among the three lines Many financial institutions describe their risk management as three lines. The first line is the business and operational teams that own risks and run controls day to day. The second line is a set of oversight functions, such as risk and compliance, that set frameworks and challenge the first line. The third line is internal audit, which gives independent assurance to the board. This 24 vocabulary comes from the Institute of Internal Auditors. The IIA text does not mention fraud, so the way it is applied to fraud in this guide is our own application and not IIA wording. In practice, fraud operations, which reviews alerts and stops payments, sits in the first line. Fraud risk oversight, which tests whether the first line’s controls work and whether risk is within appetite, belongs in the second. Internal audit, in the third, tests both. Firms differ: some put fraud strategy in the second line. The key point is separation. The people who design and run a control should not be the only people who judge whether it works. Chapter 2.5 covers who owns fraud risk in detail. For now, remember the test: ask who runs the control, who challenges it, and who checks that the challenge is real. If one team answers all three, the structure is weak. The same logic governs suspected insider fraud, covered in Module 4. If staff activity looks suspicious, the line manager is not the first person to be asked, because the manager may be involved or may warn the person. The matter goes to a team independent of that line, normally investigations. SCENARIO — THE BRANCH MANAGER AND THE ALERT An analyst in the fraud operations team at Larchmont Bank notices that a series of small cash withdrawals from dormant customer accounts, all processed at one branch, were approved by the branch manager, Corin Ashby. The customers have not been contacted, and one of them is known to be abroad. There are about forty such accounts at the branch. Each withdrawal carries a signature that the branch holds on file. The analyst’s team leader suggests asking Corin directly whether he can explain them, since he is the person who knows the branch best. Question.  Is asking the branch manager the right first step? Answer.  No. The pattern suggests possible insider fraud, and the branch manager is the person who approved the transactions. Asking him first tells him that the activity has been seen, and he could remove or alter records. Investigating a possible insider should be done by a team independent of the line the person works in, usually the investigations function, with human resources and legal involvement as the firm’s policy requires. The analyst should record what was observed, limit who is told, and escalate to investigations under the insider-fraud procedure, which can check records and contact customers in a controlled way. In an insider case the line of reporting and the line of independence are not the same, and the analyst should follow the second. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — First-party fraud originates with the customer, third-party fraud with an outsider, and insider fraud with staff or trusted contractors. — A customer who authorises a payment after being deceived by a caller is the victim of third-party fraud, not a first-party fraudster. — Origin labels must follow the evidence and change when collusion or new facts appear. — Fraud overlaps with AML, cyber security, credit risk and operational risk, and early information sharing between these teams prevents silos. — Performance measures should be read in groups covering detection, efficiency, quality, customer impact and recovery, because one improving number can hide a worsening one. — In a descriptive three-lines view, fraud operations sit in the first line, fraud risk oversight in the second and internal audit in the third; this is an application of the model, not IIA wording. 25
