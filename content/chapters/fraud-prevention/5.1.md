# Detection strategy

A detection strategy is a decision about where in a customer’s journey an institution looks for fraud, what it can know at that moment, and what it is able to do about it. Buying tools comes second. A bank that monitors card payments well but ignores logins, or checks payments overnight when the money left in seconds, has a strategy gap that no model can close. This chapter sets out the moments at which detection can happen, the actions available at each, and the data a strategy depends on.


**5.1.1** The moments at which fraud can be detected Fraud can be spotted at six moments: when an account is applied for, when a customer logs in, when a payment is set up, while a payment is in flight, after it has been sent, and in a later look-back over many accounts. Each moment gives the institution different information and different options. At application, it knows who the applicant claims to be and little else. At login, it knows the device, the location signals and the authentication result. At payment, it knows the payee, the amount and the customer’s history. The earlier the moment, the more choices exist. A payment stopped before release costs nothing. A payment recalled after release depends on the receiving institution and on how much of the money is still there. A look-back finds mule accounts and rings, but the money has usually gone. The practitioner’s rule is simple: put the strongest controls where the action is still reversible, and use the later moments to learn and to catch what the earlier ones missed. **5.1.2** Real time, near real time and batch Detection runs at three speeds. Real-time detection decides while the customer waits, in a fraction of a second for a card authorisation or within the payment flow for a bank transfer. Near-real-time detection runs within minutes, often on a stream of events, and suits actions such as holding a payment that has not yet settled. Batch detection runs on a schedule, such as overnight, and suits analysis that needs a full day of data or heavy computation. There is a trade-off between speed and depth. A real-time decision has milliseconds to work with and can use only data already to hand: recent history, device information, lists and a score. A batch process can join many sources and run complex analysis, but it cannot stop a payment that 102 settled hours earlier. For payments that cannot be recalled easily, such as instant bank transfers, batch-only monitoring is a late warning, not a control. A batch result that is more informed than the real-time one should be used to improve tomorrow’s real-time rule and to alert the customer.


**5.1.3** The action ladder: more than approve or decline A well-designed strategy does not offer only two outcomes. It offers a ladder of responses, matched to how strong the suspicion is and how costly the friction would be. Low suspicion may only add a silent flag for later review. A little more suspicion may trigger a warning screen that tells the customer about a known scam pattern. Higher suspicion may require stronger authentication, a delay of the payment, a call to the customer, or a hold for analyst review. The top rung is a decline or a block. Every rung has a cost. Friction annoys honest customers, and a customer who is challenged too often learns to click through warnings without reading them. A sound strategy sends most payments up the ladder only as far as the evidence justifies, and keeps the higher rungs for combinations of signals that are rare among honest customers.


## Scenario — THE OVERNIGHT FILE

Harrow Mutual, a regional bank, offers instant account-to-account transfers through its mobile app. Its fraud team runs a detailed monitoring job every night that joins payment, device, login and complaint data and is accurate at spotting accounts that receive money from several unrelated senders. For months the nightly alerts have identified mule accounts, but the money has always left those accounts by the time the analyst opens the case. The head of fraud proposes to buy a second, more sophisticated nightly tool to improve accuracy. **Question.** Is a more accurate nightly tool the right response? **Answer.** No. The weakness is timing, not accuracy. The nightly job already identifies the right accounts.

It identifies them after the funds have gone, because instant transfers settle in seconds and cannot easily be pulled back. A second batch tool would find the same accounts equally late. A sound practitioner would take what the batch job learns and push it into the real-time path. For example, accounts flagged as likely mules can go onto a watch list that the payment flow checks at once, so that money arriving into or leaving them is held or reviewed. Another option is a near-real-time rule that looks for a burst of unrelated inbound payments followed by rapid outbound movement. The batch job stays, but as the source of intelligence, not the control.


**5.1.4** Coverage, data and silos A strategy is only as good as its coverage. Coverage means that for each typology in the fraud risk assessment (Chapter 2.2), there is at least one detection point with the data and the action it needs. The simplest way to test this is a matrix with the typologies down the side and the six moments across the top. Empty cells show where nothing is watching. Coverage also depends on data. Fraud signals are spread across systems built for other purposes: onboarding, digital banking, card processing, payments, complaints and customer service. When these do not talk to each other, a pattern that spans them is invisible. A new device, a changed phone number and a large payment to a new payee may each pass in their own system and look alarming together.


## Scenario — THE THREE QUIET SYSTEMS

At Tessel Credit Union, a member’s phone number is changed through the call centre on Monday. On Tuesday the member’s online banking is accessed from a device never seen before. On Wednesday, a payment much larger than the member’s usual goes to a new payee. The call-centre system, the digital-banking system and the payments system each hold one of these events. Each system has its own rules, and each event on its own was below that system’s alert level. The member lost the funds. The fraud manager says the rules in each system worked as designed and the loss was unlucky. **Question.** Is that a fair conclusion? **Answer.** No. Each rule worked as designed, but the design left a gap between systems.

The three events are the classic sequence of an account takeover: contact details changed, a new device, then an out-of-pattern payment. No single system could see the sequence because none held all three events, so the strategy had no point at which the combined pattern could be detected. The fix is to build coverage for the typology, not for the system. That means feeding the three event types into one place where a rule or model can look for the sequence within a short time window, and agreeing what action follows, such as a hold on the payment and a call to the member on the previously registered number. Calling it unlucky hides a design flaw that will repeat.


## What to take from this chapter
- Detection can happen at application, login, payment set-up, in flight, after release and in look-back, and the earlier moments leave more choices
- Real-time, near-real-time and batch detection suit different jobs, and batch alone is a late warning for payments that cannot be recalled
- A strategy needs an action ladder from silent flag to block, with friction weighed against the cost of a missed fraud
- Intelligence found in batch analysis should be pushed into the real-time path, for example through watch lists
- Coverage is tested with a matrix of typologies against detection moments, and empty cells show where nothing is watching
- Fraud signals sit in separate systems, so a strategy needs a shared view that can see sequences across them.
