# The risk-based approach: foundations and risk scoring

The risk-based approach — RBA — is the organising principle of modern AML. Instead of applying identical controls to every customer, the firm identifies where its money-laundering and terrorist-financing risk actually sits and concentrates its resources there. FATF Recommendation 1 requires countries to assess risk; Basel's d505 guidance and the EBA risk-factor guidelines translate that into what banks must do.

## Why risk-based, not zero tolerance

A firm cannot verify everyone equally — the cost of full EDD on every retail customer would be absurd and would drive legitimate business away. The RBA asks three questions:

- **Where is the risk?** Which customers, products, channels and jurisdictions expose the firm to ML/TF?
- **How much risk?** Rate it — inherent risk before controls, residual risk after.
- **What controls are proportionate?** Match the depth of CDD, monitoring and review to the rating.

## The risk-scoring model

Most firms express RBA as a scoring model: weighted factors summed into a rating. Typical inputs:

- **Customer factors** — entity type, ownership complexity, PEP status, adverse media.
- **Geographic factors** — FATF grey/black lists, national high-risk lists, corruption indexes, conflict zones.
- **Product/service factors** — cash intensity, anonymity features, cross-border capability, speed of settlement.
- **Channel factors** — face-to-face vs remote onboarding, third-party reliance, agent networks.

Scores roll up into tiers — commonly low, medium, high, sometimes very high — each mapped to a control profile: standard CDD for low, enhanced monitoring for medium, full EDD with senior approval for high.

## Inherent vs residual risk

Two concepts examiners probe constantly:

- **Inherent risk** — the risk the customer presents before any controls. A foreign PEP running an opaque trading company scores high inherently.
- **Residual risk** — what remains after controls are applied. If EDD, monitoring and senior approval genuinely reduce exposure, residual risk is lower — but only if the controls actually operate.

A high inherent risk is not a failure; an unmanaged high residual risk is.

## Where RBA fails in practice

The most common failure is a model that exists on paper but not in operations: risk scores that are never refreshed, overrides with no rationale, high-risk customers on standard monitoring because the system was never configured for them. Examiners test whether the model's outputs actually change behaviour — do high-rated customers get more review? Do low-rated customers in high-risk corridors stay low-rated forever?

## Takeaways

- RBA concentrates controls where ML/TF risk is highest — it is required by FATF R.1 and detailed in Basel d505 / EBA guidelines.
- Risk scoring combines customer, geographic, product and channel factors into tiers.
- Distinguish inherent risk (before controls) from residual risk (after).
- The model only works if scores drive real control differences and are refreshed on schedule.
