# Nominee directors, bearer shares and intermediaries

Some ownership and control arrangements exist precisely to hide who is really behind a company. Nominee directors, bearer shares and professional intermediaries are the standard toolkit — and each is a specific red flag requiring specific handling.

## Nominee directors and shareholders

A nominee is a person or company that holds a directorship or shareholding on behalf of someone else. The nominee's name appears on the registry; the real principal is hidden behind a nominee agreement.

Why it matters:
- The registry shows the nominee, not the beneficial owner.
- The nominee may be a professional who sits on hundreds of companies — a signature of a formation agent, not an operator.
- Nominee arrangements are legal in many jurisdictions but are inherently opaque.

What to do: identify the underlying principal. A nominee arrangement without disclosure of the real owner is a red flag; with disclosure and verification, it can be managed — but the principal must be identified and risk-assessed like any other beneficial owner.

## Bearer shares

Bearer shares are shares owned by whoever physically holds the share certificate — no register of owners exists. They are the ultimate anonymity tool: the company genuinely does not know who its shareholders are.

Status: most jurisdictions have abolished or restricted bearer shares, but they persist in some offshore centres and legacy structures. FATF guidance treats bearer shares as inherently high-risk because beneficial ownership cannot be established.

If a customer has bearer shares: treat as a significant red flag. Either the shares are immobilised/converted to registered form (many jurisdictions now require this), or the structure cannot be adequately understood — enhanced scrutiny or decline.

## Professional intermediaries

Company formation agents, trust companies, law firms and corporate service providers often appear in ownership chains. They are not automatically suspicious — they are a normal part of corporate services — but they add a layer of intermediation that must be understood.

Key questions:
- Is the intermediary acting for a disclosed client, or is the client itself hidden?
- Does the intermediary's jurisdiction cooperate with information requests?
- Is the intermediary subject to AML regulation itself, or operating in a gap?

## The pattern to watch

The dangerous combination is not any single arrangement — it is the stack: nominee director + bearer shares + trust + secrecy jurisdiction + professional intermediary. Each layer is explainable; the combination is designed to make the beneficial owner unfindable. When you encounter multiple opacity mechanisms in one structure, treat the structure itself as the risk.

## Takeaways

- Nominees hide the real principal — identify and verify the underlying owner.
- Bearer shares make ownership unknowable by design — immobilise or treat as high-risk.
- Intermediaries add layers — understand who they serve and whether they are regulated.
- Multiple opacity mechanisms stacked together = the structure is the red flag.
