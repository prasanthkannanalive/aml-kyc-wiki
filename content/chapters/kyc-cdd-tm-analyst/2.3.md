# Domestic vs foreign PEPs: classification and risk

Politically exposed person status is one of the most consequential risk classifications in AML — and one of the most misunderstood. The FATF defines a PEP as someone entrusted with a prominent public function, but the practical work is in the classification: domestic vs foreign, and the associated persons who inherit the risk.

## Who is a PEP

The FATF definition covers:

- **Heads of state and government** — the most senior positions.
- **Senior politicians** — ministers, members of parliament, senior party officials.
- **Senior judiciary** — supreme court justices, constitutional court members.
- **Senior military and police** — commanders, chiefs of defence.
- **Senior state-owned enterprise executives** — where the SOE is significant to the economy.
- **Senior international organisation officials** — UN, IMF, World Bank leadership.

The threshold is "prominent public function" — not every civil servant, but anyone whose position gives them the power to direct public resources or make decisions affecting others.

## Domestic vs foreign PEPs — and the UK change you must know

The distinction matters because the risk profile differs:

- **Foreign PEPs** — hold office in a foreign country. Higher inherent risk because the firm's ability to verify source of wealth is weaker, the jurisdiction may have weaker rule-of-law, and the distance makes ongoing monitoring harder.
- **Domestic PEPs** — hold office in the country where the firm operates. Still elevated risk, but the firm can verify domestic sources more easily.

**The UK position changed materially in 2024.** Under Money Laundering Regulations 2017 reg 35, as amended with effect from **10 January 2024**, UK domestic PEPs — and their relatives and close associates — must be treated as **lower risk** than foreign PEPs unless other risk factors indicate otherwise. The FCA's final guidance **FG25/3 (July 2025)** spells this out in detail, including for family members and close associates. In practice this means: a UK MP or senior civil servant still gets EDD-grade attention where warranted, but the presumption of maximum risk attached to every foreign PEP no longer applies symmetrically — the risk assessment must be genuinely differentiated, not boilerplate. Firms that still treat every domestic PEP identically to every foreign PEP are both over- and under-inclusive: over-inclusive on low-risk domestic cases, under-inclusive if the domestic designation becomes an excuse to skip checks.

Outside the UK, most regimes still require EDD for both categories, with foreign PEPs typically receiving the strictest treatment — senior approval, enhanced monitoring, more frequent review.

## The classification test

When a PEP alert fires, the analyst must answer:

1. Is this person actually a PEP? Verify the position, not just the name.
2. Domestic or foreign? Which country — and under the applicable regime, does domestic status change the required treatment (as it now does in the UK)?
3. Is the position current, former, or imminent? PEP status has a tail — **at least 12 months** after leaving office under FATF-aligned regimes, then extended on a risk basis; some regimes and firms apply longer.
4. Does the customer have a relationship with the PEP? Family, business, political?

## RCAs and entity ownership

PEP risk extends beyond the individual — but the exact scope is regime-specific, so check your rules rather than assuming:

- **Relatives and close associates (RCAs):** under UK MLR, family members are the spouse or civil partner, children (and their spouses/civil partners) and parents. "Siblings" and other relatives are not automatically RCAs — close associates require evidence of close connection. FATF guidance is broader; domestic rules govern.
- **PEP-owned entities:** Wolfsberg's PEP guidance keys off **beneficial ownership or material control** — typically the applicable beneficial-ownership threshold (25% or lower where risk warrants, 10% in some regimes), not a fixed 50% figure. The 50% rule belongs to sanctions ownership analysis; do not import it into PEP work. An entity controlled by a PEP through nominees or layered holdings inherits the risk even below ownership thresholds.

## Common classification failures

- Treating a domestic PEP as standard risk because "it's only domestic" — or, post-2024 UK rules, treating UK domestic PEPs with a lower bar than the risk assessment justifies.
- Missing RCAs — the account is in the spouse's name, not the official's.
- Applying the sanctions 50% ownership rule to PEP entity analysis instead of the beneficial-ownership threshold.
- Assuming "sibling" or "business partner" automatically makes someone an RCA — the definition is narrower and evidence-based.
- Assuming PEP status ends on the day they leave office.

## Takeaways

- PEPs = prominent public function holders; foreign PEPs typically carry higher inherent risk than domestic.
- **UK since Jan 2024 (MLR reg 35, FCA FG25/3): domestic PEPs and RCAs are lower-risk than foreign PEPs unless other factors apply** — risk assessments must reflect this.
- RCAs are regime-defined (UK: spouse/civil partner, children + their partners, parents) — verify, don't assume.
- PEP entity risk keys off beneficial ownership/control thresholds (25%/10% style), not the sanctions 50% rule.
- PEP status has a post-tenure tail — at least 12 months, then risk-based.
