# Trade-based money laundering red flags

Trade-based money laundering — TBML — uses the legitimate trade in goods and services to move value across borders while disguising the criminal origin of funds. It is one of the hardest typologies to detect because it hides inside normal commercial activity — invoices, shipping documents, letters of credit.

## How TBML works

The core mechanism is mispricing: invoices that do not reflect the real value of the goods.

- **Over-invoicing** — the exporter invoices for more than the goods are worth; the importer pays the inflated amount; the difference is criminal proceeds cleaned through the trade payment.
- **Under-invoicing** — the exporter invoices for less than the goods are worth; the importer pays less; the difference is retained offshore or settled separately.
- **Multiple invoicing** — several invoices for the same shipment, each for a portion, obscuring the total value.
- **Phantom shipments** — invoices and shipping documents for goods that were never shipped at all.

## The FATF/Egmont red flags

The FATF/Egmont TBML risk indicators report identifies patterns analysts should watch:

- **Price anomalies** — unit prices significantly above or below market rates for the stated goods.
- **Unusual shipping routes** — goods routed through unnecessary jurisdictions, or transhipment points that add cost without commercial logic.
- **Document inconsistencies** — invoice dates after shipment dates, quantities that do not match shipping documents, descriptions that do not match the commodity.
- **Shell trading companies** — newly formed entities with no track record, no employees, no premises, but large trade volumes.
- **Unusual payment terms** — payment through third parties, payments to jurisdictions unrelated to the trade, advance payments for goods never delivered.
- **High-risk commodities** — cash-intensive goods (gold, precious stones, luxury items) are harder to value and easier to misprice.

## Why it is hard to detect

TBML does not generate transaction-monitoring alerts the way structuring or pass-through activity does. The "transaction" is a legitimate trade payment — the suspicious element is in the commercial documentation, not the banking activity. Detection requires trade-finance expertise, market-price benchmarks and document verification that most transaction monitoring systems do not provide.

## What analysts can do

- **Know the customer's trade profile** — what goods, what corridors, what typical values.
- **Flag price anomalies** — invoices significantly above or below market benchmarks for the stated commodity.
- **Check document consistency** — invoice vs bill of lading vs customs declaration.
- **Watch for shell traders** — new entities with large volumes and no verifiable business.
- **Escalate to trade-finance specialists** — TBML is not a generalist alert; it needs specialist review.

## Takeaways

- TBML hides in trade documentation — over/under-invoicing, phantom shipments, multiple invoicing.
- Red flags: price anomalies, unusual routes, document inconsistencies, shell traders, third-party payments.
- Detection requires trade-finance expertise — transaction monitoring alone will not catch it.
- Know the customer's trade profile; flag deviations from market benchmarks.
