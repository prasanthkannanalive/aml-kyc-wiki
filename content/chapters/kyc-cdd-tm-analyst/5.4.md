# Counterparty analysis and network mapping

An alert rarely involves a single account — it involves a network. The customer who received suspicious funds is connected to the sender, who is connected to a business, which is connected to a jurisdiction. Counterparty analysis is the discipline of mapping that network to understand the full picture.

## Why counterparties matter

Money laundering is a networked crime. The customer in your alert may be a mule, a front, or a legitimate business being used without its knowledge. The counterparty — who sent or received the funds — is often where the real story is.

Key questions about counterparties:

- **Who are they?** Known customer, external party, or unknown?
- **What is their risk profile?** High-risk jurisdiction, PEP-linked, sanctioned, or adverse media?
- **What is the relationship?** Arm's-length commercial, family, nominee, or unexplained?
- **Do they appear elsewhere?** Is this counterparty connected to other alerts or suspicious patterns?

## Building the network map

For a suspicious transaction flow, map:

- **Direct counterparties** — who sent to and received from the customer.
- **Second-degree connections** — the counterparties' own counterparties, where visible.
- **Common patterns** — do multiple alerts share the same counterparties or corridors?
- **Geographic clusters** — do flows concentrate in specific high-risk jurisdictions?

Network mapping is not about building a definitive graph — it is about identifying the nodes that matter: the high-risk counterparty, the repeated pattern, the connection to a known typology.

## Red flags in counterparty analysis

- **Unexplained counterparties** — parties the customer cannot identify or explain.
- **High-risk jurisdictions** — flows to/from FATF-listed or conflict countries.
- **Circular flows** — money that leaves and returns through different paths.
- **Concentration** — a customer's activity dominated by a single counterparty or small group.
- **Layering chains** — funds passing through multiple accounts in rapid succession.
- **Shared counterparties across alerts** — the same external party appearing in multiple customers' suspicious activity.

## When the network is the finding

Sometimes the individual alert is not suspicious on its own — but the network it belongs to is. Three customers each sending small amounts to the same external entity, none individually suspicious, may together reveal a funnel account or a collection point for criminal proceeds. This is where transaction monitoring patterns and counterparty analysis combine.

## Documentation

Network analysis findings must be documented: which counterparties were checked, what was found, how they connect to the alert, and what the analysis concluded. An examiner should be able to see the reasoning, not just the conclusion.

## Takeaways

- Alerts involve networks, not single accounts — map counterparties at every hop.
- Key questions: who are they, what is their risk, what is the relationship, do they recur?
- Shared counterparties across multiple alerts can reveal patterns invisible at the individual level.
- Circular flows, concentration and layering chains are network-level red flags.
- Document the map — the reasoning must be auditable.
