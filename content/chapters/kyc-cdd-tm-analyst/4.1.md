# Monitoring Systems: Rule-Based vs Behavioural vs ML

Once a customer is onboarded, the second line of defence begins: transaction monitoring — the system that watches every payment, transfer and cash deposit and asks whether it fits what we know about the customer.

## Rule-based vs behaviour-based monitoring

Most systems combine two approaches:

- **Rule-based** alerts fire when a transaction matches a defined condition — an amount over a threshold, a structuring pattern (multiple deposits just below a limit), activity in a high-risk country, or a velocity spike (many transactions in a short window).
- **Behaviour-based (or anomaly) monitoring** builds a profile of what is normal for each customer or customer segment and alerts when actual activity diverges — a dormant account that suddenly moves large sums, or a salary account receiving business-like turnover.

Rules are transparent and easy to audit; models catch subtler patterns but are harder to explain. Mature programmes use both, and every alert — however generated — lands in the same triage queue.

## Thresholds and tuning

The threshold question is where junior analysts get burned. Set a rule too tight and the queue drowns in false positives; too loose and real laundering walks through. Tuning is a continuous discipline:

- Thresholds should reflect the customer's expected activity, not a round number picked once.
- Structuring rules typically look at aggregate amounts over a rolling window, not single transactions.
- Velocity rules catch pass-through behaviour — money in, money out within hours.
- Every tuning change should be documented: what was changed, why, and what happened to alert volumes and SAR conversion rates afterwards.

Examiners ask a pointed question: "show me the tuning history." A rule that has never been reviewed in three years is a finding.

## The alert lifecycle

Every alert moves through a lifecycle: generated → triaged → investigated → dispositioned → (possibly) reported. Each stage has its own discipline:

- **Triage** decides whether the alert has enough substance to investigate — most alerts die here, correctly.
- **Investigation** gathers the facts: customer profile, transaction history, open-source checks, KYC refresh.
- **Disposition** is the documented decision: false positive, suspicious (report filed), or escalated.
- **QA review** samples dispositions to check the work was actually done.

## What makes a good monitoring programme

- Alerts are dispositioned by people who understand the typology, not just the rule text.
- False-positive rates are tracked per rule and reviewed regularly.
- Coverage is verified — you can demonstrate which products, channels and jurisdictions are actually monitored.
- The system is tested — scenarios are run through it to confirm alerts fire as designed.

> **Why it matters:** Rule-based and behavioural monitoring fail differently. Rules are explainable but only catch patterns someone predicted; models catch novel patterns but can't always explain why they fired. Regulators expect you to know which approach produced each alert — "the model said so" is not a defensible disposition. When tuning, remember: every threshold change must be documented with a rationale, or it becomes an exam finding.

## Takeaways

- Rule-based alerts match defined conditions; behaviour-based alerts flag deviation from the customer's normal.
- Thresholds must be tuned against expected activity and reviewed on a schedule — document every change.
- Every alert follows the same lifecycle: triage, investigate, disposition, QA.
- A monitoring programme is judged on coverage, tuning discipline and disposition quality — not alert volume.
