# PEP EDD Workflow and Ongoing Monitoring

Every AML regime in the world reduces to one obligation: before you provide a service that moves or stores money, you must know who your customer is, what they do, and whether the relationship makes sense. That obligation is customer due diligence — CDD — and it is the foundation everything else stands on.

## The three questions CDD answers

CDD is not a document checklist. It answers three questions:

- **Who is this customer?** Legal identity — verified against reliable, independent source documents.
- **What are they doing?** The nature and purpose of the relationship — what products they will use, what activity you should expect.
- **Does it make sense?** Whether the customer, the stated purpose and the expected activity are consistent with each other and with your risk assessment.

A file that answers the first two questions but not the third is a file that will fail at the first alert.

## The risk-based approach

No serious regime requires the same depth of checks on every customer. The standard is risk-based: the depth of CDD should match the money-laundering and terrorist-financing risk the customer presents. FATF Recommendation 1 requires countries to identify, assess and understand their ML/TF risks and apply resources accordingly — and that logic flows down to the firm level.

In practice this means:

- A domestic salaried employee opening a current account gets standard CDD.
- A cash-intensive business, a non-resident, a complex corporate customer or a customer from a higher-risk jurisdiction gets more — sometimes a lot more.
- A PEP, a correspondent bank or a customer acting on behalf of others almost always lands in enhanced due diligence territory.

## Customer identification basics

For individuals, CDD means verifying name, date of birth, address and a government-issued identifier — and recording how each was verified. For entities, it means verifying legal name, registration number, registered address and the people who control or own it.

Two details analysts miss:

- **Verification must be against independent sources.** A passport photo page is one source; confirming the document is genuine, unexpired and belongs to the applicant is another step. In remote onboarding, this is where liveness checks, database verification and document authentication tools come in.
- **The expected-activity profile is part of CDD.** You are not just collecting documents — you are recording what the customer says they will do, so that transaction monitoring later has something to compare actual behaviour against. A customer who cannot or will not describe their expected activity is itself a red flag.

## Ongoing CDD

CDD is not a one-time event. Trigger events — a change of ownership, a new product, a PEP status change, a sanctions hit, unusual activity — require refresh. And most regimes require periodic review on a risk-based schedule: low-risk retail customers may be reviewed every few years, high-risk customers annually or more often.

## What CDD is not

CDD is not sanctions screening (though screening is part of onboarding), not transaction monitoring, and not a guarantee. It is the firm's documented, risk-proportionate answer to "how do we know who this customer is?" — and examiners judge the quality of that answer by whether it was followed, not just written down.

## Takeaways

- CDD answers three questions: who, what, and does it make sense.
- Depth is risk-based — the customer's risk profile decides how much verification is enough.
- Verification needs independent sources; collecting documents is not the same as verifying identity.
- Record the expected-activity profile at onboarding — monitoring is useless without a baseline.
- CDD is ongoing: refresh on trigger events and on a risk-based review schedule.
