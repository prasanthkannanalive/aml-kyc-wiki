# Trusts and foundations: mapping legal arrangements

Trusts and foundations are the most opaque legitimate structures in AML — and the FATF has specific guidance for them because they are also among the most abused. A trust separates legal ownership from beneficial enjoyment by design; that is what it is for. The AML question is whether you can identify who actually benefits.

## Why trusts are high-risk

A trust has three core roles:

- **Settlor** — the person who creates the trust and puts assets into it.
- **Trustee** — the person or company that legally holds and manages the assets.
- **Beneficiary** — the person or class of persons who benefits from the trust.

A fourth role — **protector** — can exist with power to remove or replace trustees. In many jurisdictions, **appointers** can also direct distributions.

The opacity is structural: the trustee is the legal owner, but the settlor and beneficiaries may be hidden behind the trust deed, which is often confidential. A customer can be the settlor of a trust that owns their company, which owns the account — the person in front of you may have no visible connection to the money.

## Foundations: the continental variant

Foundations (common in civil-law jurisdictions and offshore centres) are separate legal entities — like companies — but can have no owners in the conventional sense. They are governed by a charter and managed by a council or board. Beneficial ownership must be identified through who established the foundation, who controls it, and who benefits from it.

## The mapping process

For a trust or foundation customer:

1. **Obtain the trust deed or foundation charter** — the governing document.
2. **Identify all parties** — settlor(s), trustee(s), protector(s), beneficiaries (named or class), appointers.
3. **Identify controllers** — who has power to amend the trust, remove trustees, or direct distributions.
4. **Identify beneficiaries** — who actually receives the benefit, whether named individuals or a defined class.
5. **Map the asset flow** — what assets are in the trust, how they got there, and how distributions are made.

## Red flags in trust structures

- Settlor is a PEP or from a high-risk jurisdiction.
- Beneficiaries cannot be identified or are defined so broadly as to be meaningless.
- The trust was created recently, just before a large transaction.
- Distributions go to people with no apparent relationship to the settlor.
- The trustee is in a secrecy jurisdiction and refuses to provide information.

## When the trust cannot be mapped

If the trust deed is unavailable, the parties cannot be identified, or the jurisdiction will not cooperate — the firm faces the same choice as with any opaque structure: documented good-faith effort, enhanced controls, or decline. Some regimes require that trusts be registered on public or accessible registers precisely to solve this problem; check whether the relevant jurisdiction has such a register.

## Takeaways

- Trusts separate legal ownership (trustee) from benefit (beneficiary) — map settlor, trustee, protector, beneficiaries and controllers.
- Foundations are entity-like but may have no conventional owners — identify founders, controllers and beneficiaries.
- Recent trusts, PEP settlors, unidentifiable beneficiaries and secrecy-jurisdiction trustees are red flags.
- Check for trust registers — many jurisdictions now require registration.
- Unmappable structures = documented effort + enhanced controls or decline.
