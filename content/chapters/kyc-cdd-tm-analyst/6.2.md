# Narrative Writing: Structure, Facts and Plain Language

When an alert resolves into genuine suspicion, the analyst's work becomes a document: the suspicious activity report — SAR in the US, STR in the UK, EU and many other jurisdictions. This is the only output of the entire AML programme that leaves the building, and it is written for an audience that was not there: the investigator, the prosecutor, the examiner reading it years later.

## What the report must contain

Every serious regime requires the same core elements:

- **Who** — the customer(s) involved: names, account numbers, identifiers, relationship to the institution.
- **What** — the specific transactions: dates, amounts, currencies, counterparties, channels.
- **When and where** — the period covered and the jurisdiction(s) involved.
- **Why suspicious** — the facts that give rise to suspicion, not just a label.
- **What the institution did** — the checks performed, the analysis undertaken, the decision to report.

The narrative is the heart of it. Everything else is structured data; the narrative is where you explain why these facts matter.

## Writing a narrative that holds up

A defensible SAR narrative has four properties:

- **Chronological.** Take the reader through the activity in order — account opened, first unusual transaction, pattern emerging, escalation — so the story builds.
- **Factual.** State what happened with exact figures and dates. "Received USD 48,000 and forwarded USD 46,500 within three hours on three consecutive days" is evidence; "the customer appears to be laundering money" is a conclusion you cannot yet prove.
- **Complete.** Include what you checked and what remains unknown. Examiners and investigators specifically look for the gaps — a narrative that acknowledges uncertainty is more credible than one that papers over it.
- **Plain.** No jargon, no speculation, no accusations. The report documents suspicion, not guilt — the customer has not been convicted of anything, and the language must reflect that.

## Deadlines and tipping off

SAR/STR deadlines are statutory and short — in many regimes, days not weeks (UK: knowledge or suspicion should be reported as soon as practicable; US FinCEN: 30 days). Internal escalation routes must be built so the deadline is never at risk because an alert sat in a queue.

The second rule is absolute: **never tip off the customer**. Telling a customer they are under investigation, that a report has been or will be filed, or even that their account is being reviewed for suspicious activity can itself be an offence. This constrains every customer-facing interaction during an open investigation — including seemingly innocent questions from relationship managers.

## Common narrative failures

- Vague language: "unusual activity" without specifying what was unusual.
- Conclusions without facts: asserting laundering without showing the transactions that suggest it.
- Missing context: not explaining why the activity deviates from the customer's expected profile.
- Internal jargon that means nothing outside your institution.
- Reporting late because the internal escalation chain was too slow.

## Takeaways

- A SAR/STR needs who, what, when/where, why-suspicious and what-you-did — plus a narrative that ties it together.
- Write chronologically, factually, completely and plainly — suspicion, not guilt.
- Deadlines are statutory and short; escalation routes must protect them.
- Never tip off the customer — it is an offence in most regimes.
