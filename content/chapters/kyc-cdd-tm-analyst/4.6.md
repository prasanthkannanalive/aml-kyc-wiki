# Alert coverage, testing and system assurance

A transaction monitoring system is only as good as its coverage — and coverage is the thing most often assumed rather than verified. This chapter covers how to test whether your monitoring actually catches what it should, and what examiners look for when they ask "show me the system works."

## What coverage means

Coverage is the answer to three questions:

- **Which customers are monitored?** Every customer should be in scope — not just high-risk ones. A system that monitors only flagged customers has a blind spot.
- **Which products and channels are monitored?** Cash, wires, cards, crypto, trade finance — each channel has different typologies and needs its own rules.
- **Which jurisdictions are covered?** Cross-border flows need monitoring regardless of where the counterparty is.

Coverage gaps are the most common systemic failure: the system monitors domestic retail accounts but not the correspondent banking book, or covers wires but not cash deposits.

## Testing the system

A monitoring system must be tested — not assumed to work:

- **Scenario testing** — inject known suspicious patterns and verify alerts fire correctly.
- **Rule testing** — verify each rule produces expected output on test data.
- **End-to-end testing** — confirm alerts reach the queue, are assigned, and are dispositioned within SLA.
- **Regression testing** — after any system or rule change, verify nothing broke.

Testing should be documented: what was tested, when, what the results were, what was fixed.

## Model risk management

For banks, monitoring models fall under model risk management — the same discipline applied to credit risk models. Key elements:

- **Validation** — independent review of model design and performance.
- **Ongoing monitoring** — tracking model performance over time.
- **Change management** — documented process for model changes.
- **Governance** — clear ownership and accountability for the model.

Even rule-based systems benefit from this discipline — a rule is a model, just a simpler one.

## Common coverage failures

- New products launched without monitoring rules.
- Acquired portfolios not integrated into the monitoring scope.
- Channels (e.g., crypto, new payment rails) added without typology coverage.
- Rules disabled during system upgrades and never re-enabled.
- Customers migrated to new systems and dropped from monitoring scope.

## The assurance question

When an examiner asks "how do you know the system works?", the answer is not "we tested it once at implementation." The answer is: documented ongoing testing, coverage maps showing what is monitored, performance metrics per rule, and evidence that gaps are identified and remediated.

## Takeaways

- Coverage = all customers, all products/channels, all jurisdictions — verify, don't assume.
- Test scenarios, rules and end-to-end flow; document results.
- Monitoring models need validation, ongoing monitoring and change management.
- New products, acquisitions and channel additions are the most common coverage gaps.
- "We tested it at implementation" is not an assurance answer — ongoing evidence is.
