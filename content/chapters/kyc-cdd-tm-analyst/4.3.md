# Structuring and smurfing detection

Structuring — also called smurfing — is the deliberate breaking of a large transaction into smaller ones to evade reporting thresholds. It is one of the oldest and most common money-laundering typologies, and one of the most reliably detectable — if the monitoring rules are tuned to see it.

## What structuring looks like

The classic pattern:

- Multiple cash deposits on the same or consecutive days.
- Each deposit just below the reporting threshold (e.g., $9,500 when the CTR threshold is $10,000).
- Often across different branches, accounts or days to avoid pattern detection.
- The total across the period would have triggered reporting if done as one transaction.

The signature is not any single deposit — it is the aggregate pattern over time. A single $9,500 deposit is legal; ten of them in a week is structuring.

## Why it works (and why it fails)

Structuring works because threshold-based reporting systems look at individual transactions. It fails because modern monitoring looks at patterns — velocity, frequency, aggregate amounts over rolling windows. The analyst's job is to see the pattern the launderer is trying to hide.

## Detection rules that work

Effective structuring detection requires:

- **Rolling-window aggregation** — sum deposits over 7, 14, 30 days, not just per transaction.
- **Threshold proximity analysis** — flag deposits within a band below the threshold (e.g., 80–99% of the limit).
- **Frequency analysis** — multiple deposits in short periods, even if each is small.
- **Cross-account/cross-branch aggregation** — the same customer or related parties depositing across multiple accounts.
- **Velocity rules** — deposits followed quickly by withdrawals or transfers (placement then layering).

## Differentiating structuring from legitimate activity

Not all sub-threshold deposits are structuring. Legitimate explanations:

- A cash-intensive business banking daily takings.
- A customer splitting income across accounts for budgeting.
- Seasonal or irregular income patterns.
- A customer who genuinely does not know the threshold and is coincidentally below it.

The difference is pattern and intent: structuring shows deliberate threshold avoidance — amounts clustering just below the limit, timing designed to evade detection, no legitimate business explanation. Coincidental sub-threshold activity lacks the pattern.

## Investigation approach

When a structuring alert fires:

1. Pull the full transaction history — all accounts, all channels, all time periods.
2. Map the aggregate — total deposited vs total withdrawn/transferred.
3. Check the customer's profile — does the activity match expected cash flows?
4. Look for related parties — family members, business associates depositing similarly.
5. Check source of funds — where did the cash come from?

## Takeaways

- Structuring = breaking large sums into sub-threshold transactions; detection requires pattern analysis over time.
- Key signals: threshold proximity, frequency, rolling-window aggregation, cross-account patterns.
- Legitimate sub-threshold activity lacks the deliberate pattern — differentiate by consistency and business rationale.
- Investigation requires full history, aggregate analysis, related-party checks and source-of-funds review.
