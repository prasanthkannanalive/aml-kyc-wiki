# Tipping-off and SAR confidentiality

The single most dangerous mistake in AML work is telling the customer they are under investigation. Tipping-off — disclosing that a SAR has been or will be filed, or that the customer is suspected of money laundering — is a criminal offence in most jurisdictions, and it can turn a compliance success into a prosecution.

## What tipping-off is

Tipping-off occurs when someone connected to the institution discloses to the customer (or anyone who might tell them) that:

- A SAR/STR has been filed or is being considered.
- The customer is suspected of money laundering or terrorist financing.
- Their account is being investigated for suspicious activity.
- Even indirect hints — "we are reviewing your account for unusual activity" in a way that reveals the reason.

The prohibition applies to everyone who knows — analysts, MLROs, relationship managers, IT staff, senior executives. It is not just an analyst rule.

## Why it is prohibited

Tipping-off defeats the purpose of the SAR regime:

- The customer can move funds, destroy evidence, or tip off accomplices.
- It undermines the investigation before law enforcement can act.
- It exposes the institution and the reporter to retaliation.
- It converts a compliance process into a warning system for criminals.

## The legal framework

- **UK POCA 2002** — tipping-off is a criminal offence; the SAR regime is built on strict confidentiality, though POCA contains narrow permitted-disclosure exceptions.
- **US Bank Secrecy Act** — SAR confidentiality is protected by statute; unauthorised disclosure is prohibited, with defined exceptions.
- **EU AMLDs** — tipping-off prohibitions across member states.
- **Home Office Circular 022/2015** — provides detailed UK guidance on SAR confidentiality and tipping-off.

Some jurisdictions create limited exceptions — for example, disclosures to law enforcement or within the institution's own compliance chain — but these are narrow and specific.

## The practical traps

Tipping-off does not require malicious intent — it happens through innocent questions:

- A relationship manager asks "why are you asking for these documents?" and the analyst explains too much.
- A customer calls to ask why their transaction was delayed and the staff member reveals the real reason.
- A customer notices their account is restricted and asks directly; "compliance reasons" may be enough to tip them off.
- An analyst discusses the case in a shared workspace or on an unsecured channel.

## How to avoid it

- **Standard responses** — prepare approved language for customer queries about delays or document requests.
- **Need-to-know** — restrict case information to those who need it for the investigation.
- **Secure channels** — discuss cases only in approved systems, not email, chat or personal devices.
- **Training** — ensure everyone who might interact with the customer understands the prohibition.
- **Escalate uncertainty** — if you are unsure whether something constitutes tipping-off, ask compliance.

## The consequences

- **Criminal prosecution** — tipping-off is an offence for the individual, not just the institution.
- **Regulatory enforcement** — fines, enforcement action, exam findings.
- **Civil liability** — customers may sue if they suffer loss due to mishandled information.
- **Reputational damage** — "the bank tipped off a money launderer" is a headline that ends careers.

## Takeaways

- Tipping-off = disclosing SAR status or suspicion to the customer; it is a criminal offence.
- It happens through innocent questions, not just malice — train for the traps.
- Standard responses, need-to-know, secure channels and escalation prevent it.
- Consequences: criminal prosecution, regulatory enforcement, civil liability, reputational damage.
- When in doubt, ask compliance — the cost of asking is zero, the cost of guessing is prosecution.
