# Adverse media screening: signal vs noise

Adverse media screening is the process of checking whether a customer or counterparty has been mentioned in negative news — fraud, corruption, sanctions, criminal investigations, regulatory enforcement. The challenge is not finding mentions; it is separating material risk from background noise.

## What adverse media covers

- **Financial crime** — fraud convictions, money laundering investigations, sanctions designations.
- **Corruption** — bribery allegations, embezzlement, abuse of public office.
- **Regulatory enforcement** — fines, licence revocations, consent orders.
- **Criminal proceedings** — arrests, charges, convictions for any serious offence.
- **Civil litigation** — major fraud claims, insolvency proceedings.
- **Terrorism and proliferation** — terrorism financing allegations, weapons-related charges.

## The noise problem

Adverse media generates enormous false-positive volume:

- **Common names** — "John Smith" matches hundreds of unrelated news stories.
- **Historical events** — a 20-year-old news story may be irrelevant to current risk.
- **Resolved matters** — charges dropped, cases dismissed, fines paid — still appear in searches.
- **Unrelated context** — a company mentioned in a story about something else entirely.
- **Opinion vs fact** — editorials, commentary and allegations that were never proven.

The analyst's job is filtering — not just finding mentions, but assessing whether they are relevant, material and current.

## The assessment framework

For each adverse media hit, ask:

1. **Is it the same person/entity?** Verify identity — name, location, role, dates.
2. **Is it relevant?** Does the story relate to financial crime, corruption, or risk-relevant conduct?
3. **Is it material?** Does it involve serious allegations or proven wrongdoing, not minor disputes?
4. **Is it current?** How old is the story? Has the matter been resolved?
5. **What is the source?** Reputable news outlet vs blog vs social media post.

## Documenting the assessment

Every adverse media review needs documentation: what was found, why it was or was not relevant, and the conclusion. "No adverse media found" is only meaningful if you document what you searched and how you filtered.

A common examiner criticism: firms run adverse media checks at onboarding but never refresh them — a customer who was clean at onboarding may have been named in a fraud investigation two years later.

## Adverse media vs PEP screening

They are different controls: PEP screening identifies politically exposed persons; adverse media identifies anyone in negative news. A PEP may have no adverse media; a non-PEP may have extensive adverse media. Both are needed, and they feed different risk assessments.

## Takeaways

- Adverse media = negative news screening; the challenge is filtering signal from noise.
- Assessment: same entity? relevant? material? current? credible source?
- Document what you searched, what you found, and why you concluded what you did.
- Refresh adverse media on review cycles — onboarding-only checks go stale.
- Adverse media and PEP screening are complementary, not interchangeable.
