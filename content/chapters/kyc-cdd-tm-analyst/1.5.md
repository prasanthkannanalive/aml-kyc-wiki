# Perpetual KYC vs periodic refresh cycles

The debate in AML operations: should KYC be a one-time onboarding event with periodic refreshes, or a continuous, event-driven process? The industry answer is converging on perpetual KYC — pKYC — but the transition is messier than the vendors suggest.

## The traditional model: periodic refresh

Under the periodic model, each customer gets a refresh on a fixed schedule based on risk tier: high-risk annually, medium every two years, low every three to five. Between reviews, the file sits static unless a trigger event fires.

Strengths: predictable workload, clear audit trail, simple to staff.
Weaknesses: a customer can go material years between reviews; trigger events depend on the firm noticing them; refresh backlogs are chronically late in most banks.

## The pKYC model: continuous, event-driven

Perpetual KYC treats the customer file as a living object. Instead of calendar refreshes, the file updates whenever something changes:

- **Identity data changes** — new ID issued, address change, name change.
- **Risk-relevant events** — PEP status, adverse media, sanctions list changes, ownership changes.
- **Behavioural drift** — activity diverges from the expected profile.
- **Document expiry** — IDs and corporate documents have validity periods; expiry triggers refresh.

The goal: the file is always current enough that a reviewer could rely on it at any moment — not just on the anniversary of onboarding.

## What pKYC actually requires

- **Data feeds** — registry updates, sanctions lists, adverse-media sources, PEP databases wired into the KYC platform.
- **Trigger logic** — defined events that automatically flag a file for refresh.
- **Dynamic risk scoring** — the risk rating recalculates as factors change, not just at review.
- **Analyst workflow** — refreshes routed to the right queue with the right evidence requirements.

The Oracle, PwC and Themis/encompass papers on pKYC all converge on the same point: pKYC is less about technology and more about redesigning the operating model so refresh work is event-driven and continuously prioritised.

## Practical reality

Most firms run a hybrid: pKYC triggers for high-risk customers and material events, periodic cycles for low-risk portfolios where continuous monitoring is not cost-justified. The exam question is not "do you do pKYC?" but "how do you decide what gets refreshed when, and can you prove it?"

## Takeaways

- Periodic refresh = fixed schedule by risk tier; pKYC = continuous, event-driven updates.
- pKYC needs data feeds, trigger logic, dynamic scoring and routed workflows.
- Most firms run hybrid models — high-risk gets continuous, low-risk gets periodic.
- The defensible answer is a documented refresh policy, not a technology claim.
