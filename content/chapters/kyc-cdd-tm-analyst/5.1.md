# The Investigation Workflow and Case Management

The alert queue is where AML theory becomes daily work. A good analyst clears alerts accurately and quickly; a bad one either rubber-stamps false positives or escalates everything — and both failures end the same way, in an exam finding or a missed SAR.

## What triage actually is

Triage is not "is this suspicious?" — it is "does this alert contain enough substance to justify investigation?" Most alerts are false positives, and the analyst's job is to recognise them quickly and accurately, not to investigate everything equally.

A triage decision looks at:

- **The rule that fired** — what condition was met, and how strong is that signal for this customer type?
- **The customer profile** — does the activity match what we recorded at onboarding? A cash-intensive retailer depositing daily is different from a salaried employee doing the same.
- **The transaction details** — amounts, timing, counterparties, geography, frequency.
- **History** — has this customer alerted before? Were previous alerts dispositioned as false positives, and has anything changed since?

## Building the narrative before you decide

The single most important triage habit: reconstruct the story the transactions tell before deciding anything. For a structuring alert, that means looking at the sequence — deposit, deposit, deposit, each just below the threshold, on consecutive days, from the same branch. For a pass-through alert, it means tracing where the money went after it arrived.

Analysts who decide from the alert summary alone miss what the transaction pattern actually shows. Pull the underlying transactions every time.

## False positives vs escalation

A false-positive disposition is not "nothing happened" — it is a documented explanation of why the activity, though unusual, has a legitimate explanation: the customer is a dealer who banks daily takings, the international wires match a documented import business, the structuring-looking deposits are a customer splitting pension income across accounts for a stated reason.

Escalation is for alerts where the facts do not resolve cleanly: the customer cannot explain the activity, the pattern repeats, counterparties are unknown or high-risk, or the activity matches a known typology. Escalated alerts go to L2 with a complete file — timeline, checks performed, open questions.

## Documentation that survives

Every disposition needs a rationale a stranger could audit later: what you checked, what you found, why you concluded what you did. "Looks fine" is not a rationale. "Customer is a registered cash business; deposits match declared daily takings; no structuring pattern across 90 days" is.

## Common triage failures

- Closing alerts on the summary without pulling transactions.
- Treating repeat alerts as independent events instead of a pattern.
- Escalating without context, or closing without explanation.
- Ignoring the customer's stated expected activity when it was recorded at onboarding.

## Takeaways

- Triage asks "is there enough substance to investigate?", not "is this suspicious?"
- Reconstruct the transaction story before deciding — pull the underlying payments.
- False-positive dispositions need a documented legitimate explanation, not just a label.
- Escalate with a complete file; close with a defensible rationale.
