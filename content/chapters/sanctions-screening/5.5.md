Thresholds and tuning Every screening system has a number that decides what you see. This chapter covers what that number does, why it cannot be optimised away, who should own it, and how tuning is tested — because a threshold changed without testing is a control changed without evidence. 5.5.1  The trade-off Set the threshold high and you see few alerts, most of which are genuine. Set it low and you see many, most of which are not. There is no setting that produces only genuine alerts, because the underlying similarity distributions of matching and non-matching pairs overlap. The two errors have opposite characters. A false positive costs analyst time, delays a customer, and — in volume — buries genuine alerts in noise. A false negative processes a prohibited transaction and is a violation. That asymmetry argues for a low threshold, and institutions accordingly set them low. But the argument has a limit that is often ignored: an alert queue so large that alerts are worked superficially produces false negatives too, through inattention rather than through the threshold. There is a point at which lowering the threshold reduces detection, and it is reached earlier than most institutions assume. The measures worth knowing by name are recall — the proportion of genuine matches the system surfaces — and precision, the proportion of surfaced alerts that are genuine. Lowering a threshold raises recall and lowers precision. The judgement is about where on that curve the institution should sit, and it is a risk decision, not a technical one. 5.5.2  Thresholds are not one number Mature configurations do not apply a single threshold to everything, and understanding the segmentation explains why two similar alerts can behave differently. Thresholds vary by list, because the consequences differ. An SDN List match warrants a lower threshold than an internal watch list. They vary by data type. Individual names, entity names, vessel names, addresses and document numbers each behave differently, and a threshold appropriate for a long corporate name is wrong for a two-syllable personal name. They vary by screening context. Customer onboarding screening operates on rich data with no time pressure and can afford a low threshold; real-time payment screening operates on sparse data against a clock and cannot. And they vary by algorithm, since a phonetic score and an edit-distance score are not on the same scale even where both are expressed out of one hundred. The practical point for an analyst is that the score in front of you means something specific to the configuration that produced it, and comparing scores across contexts is meaningless. 5.5.3  Tuning as governance Changing a threshold changes what the institution detects. It is therefore a governance act, not a system administration task, and regulators treat it as one. 92 Three requirements follow. A change must be justified in writing, on evidence, with the risk implications stated. It must be approved at a level with authority to accept the resulting risk — which is not the operations team feeling the alert volume. And it must be documented so that the configuration in force on any past date can be established, because a regulator examining a missed alert will ask what the settings were at the time. The pressure to tune is usually operational: the queue is too large, the team cannot clear it, service levels are breached. Those are real problems and raising the threshold is one of several possible answers — the others being more analysts, better data, improved alert presentation, and reducing duplicate alerts. Choosing the threshold because it is the fastest lever, without recording that reasoning, is how institutions end up unable to explain their own configuration. 5.5.4  Testing: above and below the line A threshold cannot be assessed by looking at the alerts it produces, because the alerts it produces are by definition the ones above it. The question is what sits below. Below-the-line testing samples matches scoring beneath the threshold and reviews them as if they had alerted. If genuine matches are found there, the threshold is too high and the finding is direct evidence of missed detection. It is the single most informative test of a screening configuration and the one most often omitted. Above-the-line testing samples alerts that were raised and closed, and re-reviews the decisions. It measures analyst accuracy rather than threshold accuracy, and the two are frequently confused in testing programmes. Synthetic testing injects known variants of designated names — transliterations, transpositions, truncations, reorderings — and measures whether the system detects them. It is the only way to test detection systematically rather than waiting for a real match to occur, and a well-constructed synthetic set is a substantial asset. All three should be periodic, documented, and repeated after any material change to the engine, the list source, the data feeding it or the threshold itself. A test result is a statement about a configuration at a point in time, and it expires. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — No threshold produces only genuine alerts.
