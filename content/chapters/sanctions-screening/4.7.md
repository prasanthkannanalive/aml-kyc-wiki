# What screening cannot see

This chapter states plainly what the ownership rules mean for the limits of a screening system, and what an institution has to do instead. It is short, and it is the part of the module most worth acting on.


**4.7.1** The structural gap A name screening system matches strings against lists. Entities caught by ownership rules are not on lists. Therefore a name screening system cannot detect them. This is not a tuning problem, a data quality problem or a vendor problem — it is a category limit, and no threshold adjustment addresses it. The gap is not marginal. For a heavily designated individual with an extensive commercial network, the number of entities caught by ownership may exceed the number of designations by an order of magnitude. The honest description of a payment screening control is therefore narrower than most institutions state: it detects designated parties named in the transaction, and it does not detect entities blocked by ownership. Both halves belong in any accurate account of what the control does.


**4.7.2** What closes it Four controls, in rough order of value. Beneficial ownership at onboarding, held in structured form. Ownership recorded as free text in a document is not searchable and will not answer a question asked three years later. Recorded as structured shareholder data with percentages and dates, it can be queried against every new designation. Designation-triggered ownership review. When a new designation is published, the meaningful question is not only whether the name appears in payments but whether it appears anywhere in held ownership records. This is a scheduled process run against the customer base, not a screening event, and it is the control that would have caught the logistics company in Chapter 4.1. Periodic refresh, risk-weighted. Ownership changes and records go stale. Refresh frequency should follow risk, and any customer with a chain terminating in an opaque jurisdiction is high-risk for this purpose regardless of its transaction profile. Third-party ownership data, used as a prompt. Commercial providers publishing derived ownership assessments give a useful starting point, particularly for counterparties you do not onboard and therefore hold no CDD on. Treat the output as a question to investigate, not as an answer.


**4.7.3** The counterparty problem One gap deserves separate statement because institutions systematically underestimate it. You hold beneficial ownership records for your customers. You hold none for the counterparties on their payments — the beneficiary of an outbound wire, the ordering customer on an inbound one, the parties named in a letter of credit. For those parties, name screening is genuinely all you have, and everything in this module says name screening does not detect ownership-blocked entities. The exposure is real and largely unmitigated by any control most institutions operate. What can be done is partial and worth doing anyway: third-party ownership data on higher-value or higher-risk counterparties, attention to counterparties in jurisdictions associated with the relevant designations, and treating repeated payments to an unfamiliar counterparty as a prompt for enquiry rather than as an established pattern. What should not be done is to describe the control as complete. If your institution's sanctions risk assessment states that screening detects designated parties without qualifying that ownership-blocked entities are outside its reach, the assessment overstates the control — and that is a finding waiting to be made by somebody else.


**4.7.4** What the control framework should say This module has argued that screening cannot detect ownership-blocked entities. The conclusion for an institution is not despair but accuracy, and accuracy has a specific form. A sanctions risk assessment should state the ownership exposure as its own risk, not as a subset of screening. It is a different risk with a different control and a different failure mode. It should name the controls that address it: structured beneficial ownership capture at onboarding, designation-triggered review of held ownership data, risk-weighted refresh, and third-party ownership data for counterparties on which no CDD is held. It should state the residual exposure honestly. No institution holds beneficial ownership for its payment counterparties, and for those parties the ownership risk is largely unmitigated. That sentence appears in very few risk assessments and is true of nearly all of them. And it should say what would change the position. Better registry access, a structured ownership feed, or a decision to restrict certain counterparty types are all real options with real costs, and naming them turns an acknowledged gap into a decision somebody can take.

- Ownership-blocked entities are not on lists, so name screening cannot detect them. This is a category limit, not a tuning problem

- Structured beneficial ownership data is the control that matters. Free text in a document cannot be queried
