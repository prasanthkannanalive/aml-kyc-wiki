Reach: primary, secondary and the US nexus This chapter answers the question every non-US institution asks: does American law actually apply to us? The answer has two parts, and they are frequently run together in a way that produces either complacency or paralysis. 2.4.1  Primary sanctions and who is a US person Primary sanctions prohibit conduct by persons subject to the imposing jurisdiction. In the US context, the term US person is defined and it is broader than most people assume. It covers United States citizens and lawful permanent residents wherever in the world they are located. It covers entities organised under the laws of the United States or any jurisdiction within it, including their foreign branches. And it covers any person physically present in the United States, of any nationality, for the duration of that presence. Foreign subsidiaries are the point of divergence between programmes. Under most OFAC programmes a foreign-incorporated subsidiary of a US parent is not itself a US person. Under the Iran and Cuba programmes it is caught: entities owned or controlled by US persons are subject to the prohibitions, which is why multinational groups operate separate Iran policies. The employee dimension catches institutions out. A US citizen working in a London office is a US person, and if that individual approves, facilitates or advises on a transaction with an SDN, they have engaged in prohibited conduct personally — and the institution has facilitated it. Some banks address this by excluding US persons from certain approval chains, a control that only works if the person's status is known. 2.4.2  Secondary sanctions Secondary sanctions apply to persons outside the jurisdiction, and they work differently in kind rather than in degree. They do not prohibit the foreign party from acting. There is no offence, no penalty and no legal obligation. Instead the authority provides that if a foreign person engages in specified conduct with a sanctioned target, the United States may designate that foreign person itself, or impose restrictions on its access to the US financial system. The mechanism is conditional exposure rather than direct prohibition, and the distinction is real. A European bank processing a euro payment between two European parties commits no offence under US law by doing so. If the payment materially assists a party subject to secondary sanctions, the bank risks being designated — which would remove its access to the dollar and effectively end its ability to operate internationally. This is why institutions with no US nexus whatever screen OFAC lists as a matter of course. Not because US law binds them, but because the consequence of secondary exposure is severe enough that the commercial decision makes itself. Secondary provisions are concentrated in particular programmes — Iran, North Korea, Russia and Cuba most prominently — and the conduct that triggers exposure is defined in the relevant authority. It is not a general doctrine that any dealing with any sanctioned party creates secondary risk, and an analyst who treats it as one will escalate constantly. Knowing which programmes carry secondary provisions changes the urgency of an alert. 34 2.4.3  The dollar nexus The most common route to US jurisdiction is the currency, and it catches transactions with no other American connection. A payment denominated in US dollars is, in almost all cases, cleared through a correspondent bank in the United States. Dollar clearing runs through the US banking system because that is where dollar accounts ultimately sit. At the moment the payment passes through that correspondent, it is being handled by a US person within US jurisdiction, and primary sanctions apply to that leg. Two non-US parties, neither a US person, transacting for a purpose with no American connection, therefore create US jurisdiction over the payment simply by denominating it in dollars. The enforcement record makes the point better than any explanation. The largest OFAC settlements in history have overwhelmingly involved non-US banks whose only connection to the United States was dollar clearing. BNP Paribas settled for a total approaching nine billion dollars in 2014 in relation to payments involving Sudan, Iran and Cuba cleared through New York. Standard Chartered, HSBC, Commerzbank, Société Générale and others have settled on the same basic pattern. In most cases the aggravating factor was not the underlying payments alone but the practice of removing identifying information from payment messages so that the US correspondent could not see the sanctioned party — the subject of Chapter 9.5. 2.4.4  Other routes to a nexus The dollar is the most common route but not the only one, and an analyst should know the others because a euro payment is not automatically outside US reach. US-origin goods, software and technology retain US export jurisdiction when re-exported, and services connected to them can create a nexus. An item manufactured in the United States and shipped from Rotterdam to a third country is still subject to US rules on its onward movement. A US person anywhere in the transaction chain creates one: a US-incorporated subsidiary as a party, a US-resident director approving the facility, a US-citizen relationship manager advising on structure. US infrastructure creates one: US-based servers processing the instruction, US cloud services holding the data, US-domiciled clearing or settlement systems. And in some programmes, the involvement of US-origin financial products or the use of US markets is sufficient. The practical discipline is to establish the nexus explicitly rather than assume its absence. A disposition on a euro payment between two European entities should state positively that no US nexus was identified and on what basis — currency, parties, goods, approvers, infrastructure — rather than passing over the question in silence. Silence is indistinguishable from not having asked. 35 SCENARIO — THE EURO PAYMENT THAT WAS NOT OUTSIDE US REACH A German manufacturer instructs a euro payment from its German bank to a supplier in a third country. Neither party is a US person, the currency is not the dollar, and the goods were manufactured in Germany. Screening alerts because the supplier's beneficial owner appears on the SDN List. The analyst is preparing to record that no US nexus applies and that the payment should be assessed under EU measures alone. Question.  What should the analyst establish before writing that? Answer.  Several things, any of which would change the answer. Whether the German bank routes the payment through a correspondent that touches a US institution, which can happen even for euro payments depending on the correspondent chain. Whether the goods incorporate US-origin components or technology subject to US export jurisdiction. Whether any US person sits in the approval chain — a US-citizen relationship manager, a US-incorporated group entity providing the guarantee. Whether the manufacturer is a subsidiary of a US parent and whether the applicable programme reaches owned-or-controlled entities. If all of those come back negative, the conclusion is sound and should be written with the grounds stated. What is not acceptable is reaching the conclusion by noticing the currency and stopping. Note separately that the EU analysis is not the easier one here — the beneficial owner being designated raises the ownership and control question covered in Module 4, and the EU and UK tests differ. They create the risk that the foreign party is itself designated and loses dollar access. — Dollar clearing is the most common nexus: two non-US parties in dollars still touch a US correspondent. — The largest OFAC settlements involved non-US banks connected to the US only by dollar clearing, aggravated by stripping identifying data from payment messages. — State the nexus positively in the disposition, or state that none was identified and on what basis.
