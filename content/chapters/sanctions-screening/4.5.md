# Working through a structure

Everything above is theory until you are looking at a corporate chart with eleven boxes and a designation that fired on one of them. This chapter is about method: how to work a structure, what to establish in what order, and where the answer usually turns out to be.


**4.5.1** Establish the date first Before any ownership question, fix two dates: when the designation took effect, and when the ownership arrangement you are examining came into being. This matters because ownership changes, and because changes made after a designation are viewed very differently from those made before. A transfer of shares from a designated person to a family member three weeks after designation is evidence of evasion. The same transfer three years earlier is likely ordinary estate planning. It also matters for the transactions you are reviewing. If the entity became blocked on the designation date, every dealing after that date requires examination and every dealing before it was lawful. Establishing the boundary tells you the size of the problem. Where ownership records are undated or the effective date of a transfer is unclear, that uncertainty is itself a finding worth recording. An ownership chain that cannot be dated cannot be relied on to show the position at any particular moment.


**4.5.2** Work upward, not downward The instinct when handed a structure is to start at the designated person and trace their holdings outward. That is the wrong direction for an alert. Start at the entity in front of you — your customer, or the counterparty on the payment — and work upward through its shareholders, then their shareholders, until you reach natural persons or a point where the trail stops. Working upward answers the question you actually have, which is whether this entity is blocked. Working downward from the designated person answers a different question — what else they own — which is useful for a network investigation and not for a disposition. At each level, record three things: who the shareholders are, what proportion each holds, and the source and date of that information. A chain assembled from three registries and a news article is not the same evidence as one from audited filings, and the disposition should say which it was.


**4.5.3** Sourcing the chain Working upward is the method; finding the data is the difficulty. Five sources, each with a characteristic failure. Your own CDD file. The best source when it exists, because it was collected with identification in mind and usually names natural persons. Its failure is age: ownership recorded at onboarding may be years out of date, and the question you are asking is about today. Public corporate registries. Free, authoritative where they exist, and enormously variable. Some publish full shareholder and beneficial ownership data; some publish directors only; some publish a company name and nothing else. Access to several EU beneficial ownership registers 72 was restricted following a 2022 Court of Justice ruling on privacy grounds, which removed a source practitioners had come to rely on. Commercial ownership databases. Assemble graphs from registries, filings, news and litigation. Broad coverage, genuine value, and two limitations: the data is only as good as the registries beneath it, and a provider's assessment that an entity is caught is evidence rather than authority. Regulatory and enforcement material. Designation notices, panel of experts reports, enforcement settlements and court filings frequently name entities and describe structures in detail. Underused, and often the only place a network is described as a network. The customer. Fast, creates a record, and produces an answer that may be false. A false written representation is nonetheless a materially better position than no enquiry, and refusal to answer is itself a finding.


**4.5.4** Where the trail stops Chains frequently end before they reach a natural person, and how you handle that determines whether the analysis is honest. The common terminations are: a shareholder in a jurisdiction with no public registry; a nominee shareholder or corporate services provider holding on behalf of an undisclosed principal; a trust or foundation, where the beneficiary is not a matter of record; and bearer instruments, now rare but not extinct. None of these establishes that the entity is clean. They establish that the question cannot be answered from available sources, which is a different finding and must be recorded as such. A disposition reading 'no designated ownership identified' is misleading where the truth is 'ownership above the second level could not be determined'. The correct handling depends on materiality and on what else is present. An unresolvable chain on a low-value one-off payment with no other indicators may be acceptable with the limitation recorded. The same chain on a long-standing relationship, or accompanied by any other red flag, is a reason to ask the customer directly and to escalate if the answer does not come. The one thing that is never correct is to record an unanswerable question as an answered one.


**4.5.5** The circumvention question Every major regime separately prohibits participating in activities the object or effect of which is to circumvent restrictive measures. That prohibition operates independently of whether the underlying transaction would be permitted. In ownership terms, the pattern to recognise is a structure that reduces a designated interest to just below a threshold shortly after designation. A holding moving from 60 per cent to 49 per cent, with the balance transferred to a spouse, an employee or a newly incorporated entity, is a structure whose object appears to be to escape the rule. The arithmetic answer after such a transfer may well be that the entity is not owned within the meaning of the rule. The circumvention prohibition exists because the arithmetic answer is not the only question. Related patterns include: transfers to parties with no apparent capacity to fund the acquisition; retention of economic benefit through loan agreements, options or profit-sharing arrangements while formal title moves; and continuity of management, address and business relationships across a change of ownership that on paper is complete. As with control, this is an escalation. The analyst records the pattern and the dates; the conclusion that a structure is circumvention is a legal determination.


## Scenario — FORTY-NINE PER CENT, THREE WEEKS LATER

A customer's ownership record shows a designated individual holding 60 per cent. On review you find an updated register: his holding is now 49 per cent, with 11 per cent transferred to his long-standing personal assistant. The transfer is dated three weeks after his designation. The consideration recorded is nominal. The assistant is not a shareholder in any other company, and the individual remains chairman. **Question.** Is the entity blocked? **Answer.** The arithmetic says no — 49 per cent is below the threshold under both the US and EU ownership tests. That is where the analysis begins rather than ends. Three features point elsewhere. The timing places the transfer three weeks after designation, when the ownership consequence was known.

The consideration is nominal, which means no genuine transfer of economic value occurred. And the transferee is an employee with no independent means or history of shareholding, which is the classic nominee pattern. Separately, his continuing chairmanship engages the EU and UK control limb regardless of the shareholding. The correct disposition escalates on two grounds — potential circumvention under the anti-circumvention provision, and control under the EU and UK test — and records the dates, the consideration and the transferee's profile as the supporting facts. Recording 'ownership below threshold, released' would be arithmetically accurate and professionally indefensible.


## What to take from this chapter
- Fix the designation date and the date of the ownership arrangement before anything else. Transfers after designation are read differently
- Work upward from the entity in front of you, not downward from the designated person — that answers the question you actually have
- Record at each level who holds what, from what source, and dated. Registry filings and a news article are not equivalent evidence
- Chains stop at nominees, trusts and registry-free jurisdictions. That is an unanswered question, not a clean result
- Never record an unanswerable question as an answered one
- Circumvention is separately prohibited. A holding falling just below a threshold shortly after designation is the pattern to recognise
- Nominal consideration, a transferee with no means, and continuity of management across a paper transfer are the supporting indicators.
