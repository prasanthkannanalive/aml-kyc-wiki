# The OFAC list family

OFAC maintains several lists carrying materially different prohibitions. Treating them as one produces both false blocks and missed prohibitions, and it is the mistake most likely to appear in a regulatory finding about an otherwise competent function.


**2.3.1** The SDN List The Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons List is the principal list and the one most people mean by OFAC. The rule is simple and absolute: US persons may not deal with an SDN, and any property or interest in property of an SDN that comes within US jurisdiction or into the possession or control of a US person must be blocked. Blocking is not rejecting. A blocked payment is placed in an interest-bearing blocked account, reported to OFAC within ten business days, and held until a licence authorises its release. Returning the funds to the originator is not blocking — it is making funds available to a party that may itself be sanctioned, and it destroys the record. SDN entries carry identifying information of highly variable quality. A well-documented entry may include date and place of birth, nationality, passport and national identification numbers, addresses, and a list of aliases. A poorly documented one may be a name and a country. Vessels are designated in their own right with IMO numbers, and aircraft with tail numbers and serial numbers. The list is large — in the region of twelve thousand entries — and grows steadily. It is published in several formats, and the machine-readable versions carry structured fields that the human-readable PDF does not. An institution screening against a flattened text extract is discarding the identifying data it needs to discount false positives.


**2.3.2** The SSI List and sectoral sanctions The Sectoral Sanctions Identifications List operates on an entirely different principle and is the list most often misapplied. SSI parties are not blocked. Their assets are not frozen and dealing with them is not prohibited. The list identifies entities operating in specified sectors of the Russian economy — financial services, energy, defence — and applies narrow prohibitions defined by directive. Directive 1 restricts transacting in new debt of longer than fourteen days' maturity and new equity for designated financial institutions. Directive 2 restricts new debt of longer than sixty days for designated energy companies. Directive 3 restricts new debt of longer than thirty days for defence entities. Directive 4 restricts the provision of goods, services and technology in support of deepwater, Arctic offshore and shale projects. Everything not caught by the applicable directive remains lawful. An institution may maintain accounts for an SSI party, process its payments, and lend to it at maturities inside the limit. Both errors are common and both are serious. An analyst who blocks an SSI party outright has stopped lawful business and, if the party is a substantial bank, may have frozen an operational relationship without authority. An analyst who treats an SSI hit as clean because the party is not on the SDN List has missed a real prohibition, and new long-dated debt to a Directive 1 bank is a genuine violation.


**2.3.3** CAPTA and the Non-SDN lists The Correspondent Account or Payable-Through Account Sanctions List identifies foreign financial institutions subject to restrictions or prohibitions on maintaining correspondent or payable-through accounts in the United States. The restriction is specific to that account relationship. It is not a general block and it does not freeze the institution's assets. The Non-SDN Menu-Based Sanctions List identifies parties subject to menu-based sanctions under various statutes, where the imposing authority selects from a menu of possible restrictions. The entry states which have been selected, and they vary from party to party. The Non-SDN Chinese Military-Industrial Complex Companies List restricts US persons from purchasing or selling publicly traded securities of the listed companies. It is an investment restriction. It does not prohibit commercial dealings and does not block assets. The Foreign Sanctions Evaders List identifies parties determined to have violated or evaded US sanctions on Syria or Iran. Transactions with them by US persons are prohibited, but their property is not blocked — a distinction that matters when funds are already in hand. The pattern across all of these is that each carries a tailored restriction narrower than blocking, and each requires the analyst to know which restriction applies before a disposition can be written.


**2.3.4** What your screening system must surface The operational conclusion follows directly from the above, and it is worth stating as a requirement rather than an observation. A screening system must record which list produced a hit, and must surface that to the analyst in the alert. Where the list is directive-based, it must surface the directive. An alert that says only that a name matched, without which list and what that list prohibits, does not contain enough information for any analyst to reach the right answer. The disposition template must also differ by list. An SDN disposition records a block and a report. An SSI disposition records which directive was considered and why the specific transaction does or does not fall within it. A CAPTA disposition concerns an account relationship, not a payment. If your institution's alerts do not carry this, that is a finding worth raising. It is not a preference about screen design; it is the difference between a queue that can be worked correctly and one that cannot. ## Scenario — THE FIVE-YEAR LOAN TO A DIRECTIVE 1 BANK

A European bank's syndication desk is arranging a five-year facility. One participant is a Russian financial institution that appears on the SSI List under Directive 1. The relationship manager notes correctly that the institution is not an SDN, that the bank maintains a correspondent account for it lawfully, and that payments to it have been processed without issue for years. The desk proceeds. **Question.** Is there a problem? **Answer.** Yes, and it is the exact prohibition Directive 1 exists to impose. New debt of longer than fourteen days' maturity for a Directive 1 institution is prohibited for US persons, and a five-year facility is squarely within it.

Everything the relationship manager observed is true and none of it is relevant — the account, the payments and the absence of an SDN designation are all lawful and all beside the point. The specific question is whether this transaction is new debt above the maturity limit. Note also the second issue: whether the arrangement involves any US person, US dollar clearing or US-incorporated entity, because Directive 1 binds US persons. A purely European syndicate in euros raises a different question from one with a New York branch in it.


## What to take from this chapter
- SDN: full blocking. Property is frozen, reported within ten business days, and held until licensed. Returning funds is not blocking
- Machine-readable list formats carry identifying fields the human-readable versions drop. Screening a flattened extract discards what you need to discount
- SSI: not blocked. Each directive prohibits something narrow; everything else stays lawful
- CAPTA, Non-SDN MBS, NS-CMIC and FSE each carry tailored restrictions narrower than blocking
- The list — and for SSI the directive — must reach the analyst in the alert. Without it no disposition can be correct
- Disposition templates should differ by list. An SSI disposition that reads like an SDN one is evidence the distinction was not made.
