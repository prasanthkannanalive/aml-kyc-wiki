From embargoes to targeted measures The way sanctions are designed changed fundamentally around the turn of the century, and every difficulty in modern screening follows from that change. This chapter covers what came before, what forced the change, what replaced it, and what the replacement did to the compliance obligation. 1.2.1  The age of comprehensive embargoes For most of the twentieth century, a sanction meant an embargo: a near-total prohibition on trade and financial dealings with an entire country. Rhodesia from 1966, South Africa through the apartheid era, Iraq from 1990, Yugoslavia from 1992 — the instrument was the same and it was blunt by design. The theory of coercion was explicit. Impose sufficient economic pain on a population and the population, or the elite that depends on it, will compel the government to change course. The mechanism required the pain to be widespread; that was not a side effect but the operating principle. From a compliance perspective, comprehensive embargoes were straightforward. The test was jurisdictional. Is this shipment going to Iraq, is this payment connected to Yugoslavia. There were no lists of thousands of names, no transliteration problems, no ownership analysis. An institution could implement a comprehensive embargo with a country field and a competent operations team. 1.2.2  Iraq and the turning point United Nations Security Council Resolution 661, adopted in August 1990 following the invasion of Kuwait, imposed among the most comprehensive measures ever adopted: a near-total trade embargo, a freeze on state assets, and restrictions on almost all financial dealings. They remained substantially in force for thirteen years. The humanitarian consequences became the subject of sustained international argument. UNICEF and other agencies documented sharp deterioration in child mortality, nutrition and public health. The specific mortality figures were and remain contested — later analysis suggested the most widely cited numbers were inflated by data collected under the Iraqi government's supervision — but the political effect did not depend on the precise figure. The Oil-for-Food Programme, introduced in 1995 to permit controlled oil sales funding food and medicine, was intended as the answer. It became instead the subject of a major corruption investigation, with the Volcker inquiry finding widespread manipulation by the Iraqi government and by companies participating in the programme. The remedy had been captured by the party it was meant to constrain. By the late 1990s the position had become difficult to defend. Sanctions imposed to punish a government were falling on a population with no means of removing that government, and the mechanism designed to relieve them had been turned into a source of illicit revenue. Two senior UN humanitarian coordinators for Iraq resigned in protest within two years of one another. Whatever the true numbers, comprehensive sanctions had become politically unsustainable. 14 1.2.3  The design of targeted sanctions The response was developed through a series of intergovernmental processes in the late 1990s and early 2000s — the Interlaken process on financial sanctions, the Bonn-Berlin process on arms embargoes and travel bans, and the Stockholm process on implementation. The output was what is now called targeted or smart sanctions. The design principle inverted the old model. Instead of restricting an economy, restrict named individuals and entities. Freeze their assets, ban their travel, prohibit dealings with the companies they own or control, and embargo the specific goods they need. The cost falls on decision-makers rather than on a population, and the measures can be applied incrementally, expanded in response to specific conduct, and unwound selectively in a negotiated settlement. This is now the dominant form. The overwhelming majority of measures you will screen against are targeted. Comprehensive country regimes survive in a small number of cases — North Korea, and until recently Syria and Iran in substantial part — and even these operate through extensive lists of named parties rather than through a country field alone. 1.2.4  What targeting did to your job The shift solved a genuine humanitarian problem and created a genuine operational one. Every difficulty in modern screening is a direct consequence of it. Under a comprehensive embargo, the compliance question was: is this transaction connected to Country X. That is answerable from a structured field. Under a targeted regime, the question is: is any party to this transaction, or any party owning or controlling a party to this transaction, one of approximately forty thousand named individuals and entities across a dozen national lists — where names arrive in multiple transliterations from non-Latin scripts, where identifying data is often thin, where ownership may run through three jurisdictions, and where a vessel may have changed its name twice this year. That is not a harder version of the same question. It is a different question, and it is fundamentally an identification problem rather than a jurisdictional one. It also produced the false positive. A country field either matches or does not. A name match is a probabilistic judgement about whether two strings refer to the same human being, and at any threshold that discriminates usefully, most of what it surfaces will be wrong. Chapter 5 of Module 5 covers the tuning problem this creates; for now the point is that the alert volume you deal with is not a defect of your institution's system. It is the arithmetic consequence of the design decision taken in the early 2000s. 15 SCENARIO — THE SAME TRANSACTION, TWENTY YEARS APART Consider a single payment: a Swiss trading company pays a Dubai-based intermediary for a cargo of refined product, ultimately destined for a buyer in a country under sanction. Question.  What does the compliance function actually have to do, under a comprehensive regime and under a targeted one? Answer.  Under a comprehensive embargo the analysis is short. Is the destination the embargoed country? If yes, the transaction is prohibited regardless of the parties. The work is establishing destination, and the evasion problem is falsified documentation about where goods are going. Under a targeted regime the destination alone may prohibit nothing. The analyst must instead establish whether the Dubai intermediary is designated, whether it is owned or controlled by a designated party, whether the vessel carrying the cargo is listed or has been renamed, whether the ultimate buyer is designated, whether the product falls under a sectoral measure or a price cap, and whether any part of the payment chain creates a US nexus. Six questions instead of one, each requiring evidence, and five of them turning on identity rather than geography. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — Comprehensive embargoes restricted whole economies and required little more than a country check. — UNSCR 661 (Iraq, 1990) was the last major comprehensive regime and the reason the approach was abandoned. — Targeted sanctions were designed through the Interlaken, Bonn-Berlin and Stockholm processes to move the cost onto decision-makers. — Screening as a discipline exists because of that shift. Naming parties created the identification problem. — The false positive is arithmetic, not a defect. Name matching is probabilistic where a country field was binary. 16
