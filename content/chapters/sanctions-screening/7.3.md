# AIS and dark activity

Automatic Identification System data is the raw material of most maritime monitoring, and it is routinely over-read. This chapter covers what AIS actually is, what a gap in it means, and what it does not mean.


**7.3.1** What AIS is AIS is a collision-avoidance system. It was designed so that ships and shore stations could see one another, and it broadcasts identity, position, course, speed and voyage information on VHF at short intervals. It is mandatory under the SOLAS convention for vessels above three hundred gross tonnes on international voyages, and for all passenger ships. Coverage is therefore near-universal for the vessels that matter in sanctions work. Because it broadcasts openly, the signals can be collected — by coastal receivers and by satellite — and aggregated into the global vessel tracking picture that commercial providers sell. That aggregation is a by-product of a safety system, not its purpose. Two things follow from its purpose. The data is self-reported: a vessel transmits what her equipment is configured to transmit, and there is no independent verification of position. And the system was designed to be switched off, because a master may legitimately disable it where transmitting position would endanger the ship.


**7.3.2** Gaps A gap is a period during which no AIS signal is received from a vessel. Interpreting one correctly requires knowing why gaps happen. Legitimate reasons: equipment failure, which is common; satellite coverage limitations in remote ocean areas, though far less than it once was; congestion in busy waterways where signals collide; and deliberate disablement in piracy-risk areas, which is a recognised and lawful practice recommended in some waters. Evasive reasons: concealing a port call at a sanctioned terminal, concealing a ship-to-ship transfer, and concealing the true origin or destination of a cargo. The features that distinguish them are duration, location and pattern. A twenty-minute gap in the Singapore Strait is congestion. An eleven-day gap between the last position off Malaysia and the next off Fujairah is not equipment failure — it is a period long enough to complete a transfer, and the vessel has moved a considerable distance while dark. The strongest single indicator is a change in draught across a gap. A vessel that disappears laden and reappears in ballast has discharged; one that disappears in ballast and reappears laden has loaded. In either case cargo moved during a period nobody can see, and where the reappearance is in open water rather than at a terminal, a ship-to-ship transfer is the obvious explanation.


**7.3.3** Spoofing More sophisticated than switching off is transmitting false data, and this has become substantially more common. The techniques observed include broadcasting a fabricated position while the vessel is elsewhere; transmitting another vessel's identity, so that a clean ship appears to be in two places at once; and manipulating the reported IMO or MMSI so that the transmitted identity does not correspond to the hull. The detection signals are physical impossibilities: a vessel reporting a position it could not have reached at any achievable speed, two vessels reporting the same identity simultaneously, a track that crosses land, or a reported position inconsistent with satellite imagery of the actual berth. This is specialist analysis and no bank performs it in-house. What an analyst needs is the awareness that AIS data can be false as well as absent, so that a clean AIS track is not treated as confirmation. Absence of evidence and fabricated evidence look identical in a report that only checks whether a track exists.


**7.3.4** What a gap actually tells you Precision here matters because AIS findings frequently appear in escalations stated more strongly than the data supports. A gap is not evidence of a sanctions breach. It is evidence that the vessel's position was unknown for a period. Everything beyond that is inference, and the inference is stronger or weaker depending on duration, location, draught change and pattern. A single gap on a vessel with an otherwise ordinary history is weak. Seven gaps in twelve months, the longest of eleven days, with a laden-to-laden transition and a reappearance in a different sea area, is a pattern rather than an incident. The correct formulation in a file is what was observed and what it is consistent with, not what it proves. 'AIS gap of eleven days between a last position off Malaysia and a next position off Fujairah, laden on both sides, consistent with an undisclosed transfer' is a defensible statement. 'Vessel went dark to conduct a sanctions-busting transfer' is not, and an escalation written in those terms invites a reviewer to discount the whole analysis.


## What to take from this chapter
- AIS is a collision-avoidance system, self-reported and unverified, that the tracking industry aggregates as a by-product
- It was designed to be switchable, and disabling it is lawful in piracy-risk waters
- Gaps have legitimate causes — equipment failure, congestion, piracy risk — and evasive ones. Duration, location and pattern distinguish them
- A change in draught across a gap is the strongest single indicator: cargo moved while nobody could see
- Spoofing means AIS data can be false as well as absent. A clean track is not confirmation
- A gap evidences that a position was unknown. Everything beyond that is inference and should be written as such
- State what was observed and what it is consistent with. An overstated conclusion invites a reviewer to discount the analysis.
