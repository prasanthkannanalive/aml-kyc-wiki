Trusts, nominees and opaque structures The ownership rules assume you can see who owns what. This chapter is about the structures built so that you cannot, what each is for, which are ordinary and which are not, and how to record an answer you were unable to reach. 4.6.1  Nominee shareholders A nominee holds shares in their own name on behalf of somebody else, who is the beneficial owner. The arrangement is documented in a declaration of trust or a nominee agreement, which is private. It is entirely ordinary. Nominee structures are used for legitimate privacy, for administrative convenience in holding shares for many investors, and as a standard service of corporate service providers in many jurisdictions. It is also the simplest way to break an ownership trail. A register showing a corporate services company as the sole shareholder tells you who administers the holding and nothing about who owns it. The indicators that a nominee arrangement is concealment rather than convenience are contextual: a nominee who appears as shareholder of a very large number of unrelated companies; a nominee appointed shortly after a designation elsewhere in the structure; and a beneficial owner who was previously disclosed and is now not. The practical response is to ask. A corporate customer can be asked to identify the beneficial owner behind a nominee holding, in writing. Refusal to answer is itself a material fact. 4.6.2  Trusts A trust separates legal ownership from beneficial enjoyment. Trustees hold assets for beneficiaries, and the settlor is whoever put the assets in. Sanctions analysis of a trust is genuinely difficult because there are several parties, any of whom may be designated, and their relationships to the assets differ. A designated settlor may retain no interest at all. A designated beneficiary may have only a discretionary entitlement that the trustees are not obliged to exercise. And a protector — a person with power to remove trustees — may control the arrangement while appearing nowhere in its ownership. The questions worth asking are: who established it and when, relative to any designation; who the beneficiaries are and whether their entitlement is fixed or discretionary; who may appoint and remove trustees; and whether any designated person retains a power over the assets. Trusts established before a designation, with fixed beneficiaries and independent professional trustees, are ordinary. A discretionary trust established shortly after a designation, with a protector who is a family member of the designated person, is a structure whose purpose invites a question. 4.6.3  Foundations and equivalent vehicles Private foundations — Liechtenstein, Panama, and several other jurisdictions — are legal persons with no shareholders. They hold assets for stated purposes and beneficiaries, and are governed by a council rather than owned. 75 The absence of shareholders is the feature that matters here. An ownership test expressed in percentages has nothing to bite on: no one holds shares, so no one holds more than fifty per cent of them. That is why the EU and UK control limbs exist and why the US position, which tests ownership only, is narrower in this specific respect. A foundation controlled by a designated person is caught by the EU and UK tests through the control criteria, and is not caught by the Fifty Percent Rule at all. The analysis follows the control indicators from Chapter 4.3: who appoints the council, who may amend the statutes, who benefits in practice, and whether the entity's conduct suggests direction by a particular person. The scenario at the end of Chapter 4.3 is exactly this structure. 4.6.4  Bearer instruments and layered jurisdictions Bearer shares transfer by physical possession of the certificate, with no register of who holds them. They have been abolished or immobilised in most reputable jurisdictions and survive in a small number. Where they appear, ownership at any moment is not determinable from any record. Layering across jurisdictions achieves a similar result without any exotic instrument. A chain running through three jurisdictions, each of which publishes less than the last, ends in a company about which nothing is public. No single link is improper and the aggregate is opaque. The pattern worth noticing is direction of travel. A chain that runs from a transparent jurisdiction toward progressively less transparent ones is doing something a chain assembled for tax or regulatory reasons usually does not. Neither of these is evidence of sanctions evasion on its own. Both are reasons to treat the ownership question as unanswered rather than as answered negatively, which is the distinction the next topic is about. 4.6.5  Recording an answer you could not reach This is the most important paragraph in the chapter and it restates a point from Chapter 4.5 because it is where files most often fail. A chain that terminates at a nominee, a trust, a foundation or a registry-free jurisdiction has not been shown to be clean. It has been shown to be unresolvable from available sources. Those are different findings and only one of them is true. The correct record states where the chain stopped, why, what was sought, and from whom. 'Ownership traced to Alpha Holdings (BVI); BVI register does not disclose shareholders; customer asked to confirm beneficial ownership on 12 August, no response as at 20 August' is complete and honest. 'No designated ownership identified' is neither, because it describes a search and reads as a finding. It is the ownership equivalent of the null discount from Chapter 6.2, and it fails for the same reason. What to do about an unresolvable chain depends on materiality and on what else is present, and that is a risk decision rather than an analyst's. What is always the analyst's is recording it accurately, so that whoever takes the decision is taking it on the true position. 76 SCENARIO — THE CHAIN THAT ENDS IN A FOUNDATION A corporate customer is owned by a Cyprus company, which is owned by a Netherlands company, which is owned by a Liechtenstein foundation. The foundation has no shareholders. Its council comprises a Liechtenstein law firm and two individuals resident in the same country. Its stated purpose is the support of the settlor's family. The settlor is not named in any public record. A designated individual's known family name matches one of the council members' surnames. Question.  How far can this be taken, and what should the file say? Answer.  Ownership analysis stops at the foundation, and that is a structural stop rather than a failure of effort: a foundation has no shareholders, so a percentage test has nothing to measure. The US rule therefore reaches nothing here. The EU and UK control limbs may, and the questions are who appoints the council, who may amend the statutes and who benefits — none of which is public. The surname match is worth recording and is weak on its own; surnames are shared. The file should state the chain as far as it goes with each source named, state that the foundation is the terminus and why, list the specific questions that could not be answered, record that the customer was asked and what they said, and note the surname coincidence as an observation rather than a finding. Then escalate. What must not appear is a conclusion that no designated ownership was identified, because none was looked for successfully — the search did not fail to find something, it was unable to look.
