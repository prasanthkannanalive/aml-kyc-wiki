# What screening misses

This chapter completes the picture begun in Module 4. Screening detects designated names present in the data it is given. Each part of that sentence carries a limitation, and an accurate account of the control has to name all of them.


**5.6.1** Data the engine never sees Payment messages have structured fields for ordering customer, beneficiary and the institutions in the chain, and free-text fields for remittance information and instructions. Screening configurations frequently cover the structured fields and treat free text as low value. Names appear in free text constantly: an ultimate beneficiary described in the payment reference, an underlying contract counterparty, a vessel name, a project location. Where free text is not screened, a designated party named there passes through untouched. Screening free text is genuinely difficult — it is unstructured, multilingual, full of reference numbers that trigger spurious matches, and a considerable generator of alert volume. That difficulty is the reason many institutions restrict coverage, and it is a defensible decision only if it is a recorded decision rather than an unexamined default. The same question applies to attachments and documents in trade finance. A letter of credit names parties, vessels and ports across several documents, most of which never reach a screening engine.


**5.6.2** Data that has been removed Payment stripping is the deliberate removal or alteration of identifying information in a payment message so that intermediary institutions cannot see the underlying party. The technique was central to the largest sanctions enforcement cases. Originating institutions removed originator names and references from messages before sending them to US correspondents, or replaced them with the sending bank's own details, so that dollar clearing proceeded without the underlying Iranian, Sudanese or Cuban party being visible. For the correspondent doing the screening, the control worked exactly as designed and detected nothing, because the data was not there. This is the clearest illustration that screening quality is bounded by data quality and that no configuration compensates for information deliberately removed upstream. The indicators are structural rather than name-based: cover payments where the underlying detail is not visible, repeated messages from a particular institution with unusually sparse originator data, and formatting patterns suggesting fields have been overwritten. These are correspondent monitoring questions, not screening ones.


**5.6.3** Parties that are not named The ownership problem from Module 4 belongs here as well, stated from the screening side. An entity blocked by ownership carries a clean name. The engine has nothing to match. No threshold, algorithm or data source resolves this, because the entity is not on the list. The same applies to a designated person acting through an agent, a family member, a nominee or an employee. The person named in the transaction is not designated; the person benefiting is. The making-available prohibition from Module 3 catches it; name screening does not. And it applies to indirect benefit generally — payments for goods that will reach a designated party, services performed for their benefit, debts settled on their behalf. The screening engine sees the immediate counterparty and nothing beyond.


**5.6.4** Stating the control accurately The purpose of this chapter is not to undermine confidence in screening. It is a necessary and effective control against the risk it addresses. It is to make sure the institution's description of it is accurate. A sanctions risk assessment stating that the institution screens customers and payments against sanctions lists, without qualification, claims more than the control delivers — and the gap between claim and reality is what a regulator finds. An accurate description names four boundaries. Screening detects designated parties whose names appear in the fields screened. It does not detect entities blocked by ownership. It does not detect parties whose identifying data has been removed. And it does not detect indirect benefit where the beneficiary is not named. Each of those gaps is addressed by a different control — ownership analysis, correspondent monitoring, transaction monitoring, due diligence — and an institution that has named the gaps can point to what covers them. One that has not will be asked.


## What to take from this chapter
- Free-text fields carry names constantly. Not screening them is defensible only as a recorded decision, not as an unexamined default
- Trade finance documents name parties, vessels and ports that never reach a screening engine
- Payment stripping removes the data upstream. The control detects nothing because there is nothing there, and no configuration compensates
- Stripping indicators are structural — sparse originator data, cover payments, overwritten fields — and belong to correspondent monitoring
- Ownership-blocked entities, nominees and agents carry clean names. The making-available prohibition catches them; screening does not
- An accurate description of the control names four boundaries: fields screened, ownership, removed data, indirect benefit
