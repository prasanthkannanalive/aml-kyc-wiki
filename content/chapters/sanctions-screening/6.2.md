Discounting an individual Discounting means concluding that the party in your transaction is not the party on the list. This chapter sets out every basis on which that conclusion can properly be reached, what each is worth, and the combinations that do not work however they are added up. 6.2.1  What discounting is A discount is a positive finding that two parties are different people. It is not a finding that you could not establish they were the same, and the distinction is the whole of this chapter. The evidential position matters. You are not required to prove beyond doubt that the parties differ, and no regime demands certainty. What is required is a reasoned conclusion supported by identifying information, recorded so that another person can follow it. The failure mode is the null discount: an alert closed because nothing was found to confirm the match. That is not a discount. It is an unresolved alert recorded as a resolved one, and in a regulatory review it is the single most common finding in alert file testing. The test to apply to your own work is simple. Can you complete the sentence 'this is a different person because…' with a fact? If the honest completion is 'because nothing suggested otherwise', you have not discounted anything. 6.2.2  Strong discounting factors These can carry a discount on their own where the data is reliable on both sides. A differing unique document number. Passport, national identity card, or other government-issued identifier. Where the list carries a passport number and your record carries a different one for the same claimed identity, that is close to conclusive. Two caveats: people hold multiple passports, particularly dual nationals, so a different number in a different country of issue is weaker than a different number in the same country; and document numbers are transcribed and mistranscribed. A differing full date of birth. Where both sides carry a complete date and they differ, the parties are almost certainly different. The strength depends on the list entry being a single date rather than a range or a set of alternatives, and on the source of your own date being reliable — a date self-reported at onboarding is weaker than one captured from a passport. A differing place of birth, where both are specific. Place of birth does not change, unlike address, which makes it a genuinely strong identifier when both sides state a city. Confirmed different nationality, where the list entry states a single nationality and your record evidences another with documentation. Weaker where either side may hold several. A demonstrable impossibility. The listed individual is deceased and the date of death precedes the transaction; the listed individual is documented as being in custody in a stated jurisdiction. These are rare and they are conclusive when established from a reliable source. 6.2.3  Weak and unusable factors These are frequently relied on and none supports a discount on its own. 99 A differing address. Covered in Module 5: sanctions record addresses are historic and often incomplete, people move, and a designated person's location at designation is frequently not where they were before. Address differences are weak for discounting. A differing year of birth where the list carries only a range. A range of 1971 to 1974 is not contradicted by any date within it, and an analyst treating a 1973 date of birth as a discount against that range has misread the record. A different spelling of the name. This is the reason the alert exists. Fuzzy and phonetic matching are designed to surface exactly this, and treating the spelling variation as the discount inverts the entire control. A different occupation, employer or apparent standing. Occupation is rarely on a sanctions record, is not verified, and changes. Gender inferred from a name. Names that read as male or female in one culture do not in another, and the inference is unreliable across the range of names on sanctions lists. Age plausibility from context. Inferring that a company director must be older than the listed date of birth suggests is speculation, not evidence. 6.2.4  How factors combine Analysts frequently reason that several weak factors together amount to a strong one. Usually they do not, and understanding why matters. Weak factors are weak for a common reason: they concern attributes that change, are recorded imprecisely, or are inferred rather than evidenced. Combining three attributes that all change does not produce one that does not. A different address, a different apparent occupation and a different spelling are three weak observations about the same underlying uncertainty, not three independent tests. Independence is the property that makes combination valid. Two genuinely independent identifiers — a differing date of birth and a differing passport number, from separate sources — do strengthen one another, because it is unlikely that both are wrong in the same direction. The practical rule: one strong factor, properly evidenced and sourced, supports a discount. Several weak factors do not, and a rationale listing four weak factors is usually evidence that no strong one was available. Where only weak factors exist, the correct step is to seek a strong one — from the customer record, from the customer, or through a request for information — and to escalate if none can be obtained. Discounted as a different party.' Question.  Is the discount supportable? Answer.  No, and each of the four reasons fails separately. The address difference is weak — the listed party's Yemen address is where they were recorded, not where they are. The occupation difference is not evidence: occupation is not verified on a sanctions record, and a militia commander using a shipping agent as a commercial identity is precisely the pattern these regimes exist to catch. The spelling difference is the reason the alert fired and cannot also be the reason it closes. The eleven prior transactions are a fact about the payment history, not about identity, and may mean eleven prior breaches rather than eleven prior confirmations. What is missing is any identifier: no date of birth, no document number, no place of birth. The correct disposition is to seek one and, failing that, to escalate. Note that the rationale is fluent, specific and detailed — which is what makes it dangerous. A reviewer skimming the queue may read length as rigour.
