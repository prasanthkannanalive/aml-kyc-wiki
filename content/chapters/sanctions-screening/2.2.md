# The United States

The United States imposes more sanctions than any other jurisdiction and reaches furthest beyond its own borders. Four agencies matter, they administer different bodies of law, and confusing their remits is one of the most common and most consequential errors in a compliance function.


**2.2.1** OFAC The Office of Foreign Assets Control sits within the Department of the Treasury and is the body you will deal with most. It administers and enforces economic and trade sanctions, maintains the Specially Designated Nationals list and the other lists covered in the next chapter, issues general and specific licences, and brings civil enforcement actions. OFAC's authority derives principally from the International Emergency Economic Powers Act of 1977, under which the President declares a national emergency with respect to an unusual and extraordinary threat originating substantially outside the United States, and may then regulate or prohibit transactions relating to it. Each sanctions programme rests on an executive order issued under that authority, and the executive order sets the designation criteria. This matters practically because it explains something analysts find odd: the same person can be designated under several programmes at once, and the SDN entry will list multiple programme tags. Those tags are not decoration. They identify which authority applies, and therefore which general licences and which exemptions are available.


**2.2.2** BIS and export controls The Bureau of Industry and Security sits within the Department of Commerce and administers the Export Administration Regulations. It controls the export, re-export and in-country transfer of dual-use items — goods, software and technology with both civilian and military applications. BIS maintains three lists that appear in screening systems and are routinely misread. The Entity List identifies parties for whom a licence is required to export controlled items, with a stated licence review policy that is frequently presumption of denial. The Denied Persons List identifies parties whose export privileges have been denied outright. The Unverified List identifies parties BIS has been unable to verify through end-use checks. None of these is a sanctions list in the OFAC sense. An Entity List designation restricts the supply of controlled items to that party. It does not freeze assets, does not prohibit payments, and does not make the party's money untouchable. The single most common error in a screening function follows directly. An institution loads the Entity List alongside the SDN List, an alert fires on an Entity List name in a payment, and the analyst blocks. The customer is denied a service that was never prohibited, the institution has no lawful basis for the block, and if the customer complains there is nothing to point to.


**2.2.3** State and FinCEN The Department of State designates under several authorities of its own. The most significant for screening are terrorism designations — Foreign Terrorist Organizations under the Immigration and Nationality Act, and Specially Designated Global Terrorists — and proliferation designations under various non-proliferation statutes. State also administers the International Traffic in Arms Regulations, controlling defence articles and services, which sit outside the BIS regime entirely. State designations frequently result in a corresponding OFAC listing, so the party appears on the SDN List with a terrorism programme tag. But the legal effect flows from the specific authority, and some State designations carry consequences — immigration bars, prohibitions on material support — that have no OFAC equivalent. The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network is the US financial intelligence unit and administers the Bank Secrecy Act. It is an AML body, not a sanctions body, but it holds one power that behaves very like a sanction. Section 311 of the USA PATRIOT Act permits FinCEN to designate a foreign jurisdiction, institution, account type or transaction class as a primary money laundering concern and impose special measures — the most severe of which prohibits US financial institutions from maintaining correspondent accounts for the designated party. The practical effect of a fifth special measure is to cut a foreign bank off from the US dollar, which is close to terminal for an internationally active institution. But the party is not on the SDN List, its assets are not blocked, and the authority is AML rather than sanctions. An institution that screens only OFAC lists will not see it.


**2.2.4** Why the taxonomy matters operationally This is not an organisational chart to memorise. It determines three things an analyst has to get right. Licensing. A restriction administered by BIS cannot be licensed by OFAC and vice versa. An analyst who tells a customer to apply to OFAC for a licence covering an Entity List restriction has sent them to the wrong agency, and the delay may be commercially serious. Disposition. An SDN hit and an Entity List hit require different analysis and different outcomes. Flattening them into a single alert type produces a queue where the correct answer cannot be reached from the information presented. Escalation. A Section 311 special measure affecting a correspondent bank is a relationship-level issue for senior management, not a payment-level decision for an analyst. Recognising which category a restriction falls into determines who needs to know.


## Scenario — THE ENTITY LIST BLOCK

A US-incorporated distributor instructs a payment of $62,000 to a Chinese electronics manufacturer for a shipment of commercially available components. Screening alerts: the beneficiary appears on the BIS Entity List. The analyst blocks the payment and reports it to OFAC as a blocked transaction. **Question.** What has gone wrong, and what should have happened? **Answer.** Two things have gone wrong. The payment was not prohibited — an Entity List designation restricts the export of controlled items to that party, not the movement of funds — so a lawful transaction has been blocked and the customer denied service without basis. And the report to OFAC is a report of a blocking that should not have occurred, under an authority that does not apply.

What should have happened is a different question entirely: is the distributor exporting a controlled item to this party, and if so does it hold a licence? That is a trade compliance question, answered against the Export Administration Regulations and escalated to whoever owns export controls. The payment itself proceeds. Note the asymmetry — the institution's error was in the direction that feels safe, and it was still an error with a real cost to a customer.


## What to take from this chapter
- OFAC (Treasury) administers economic sanctions under IEEPA, maintains the SDN List, licences and enforces
- Programme tags on an SDN entry identify the authority and therefore which licences and exemptions apply
- BIS (Commerce) administers export controls. Entity List, Denied Persons List and Unverified List restrict exports, not payments
- Blocking a payment on an Entity List hit is unlawful denial of service and has no basis to point to if challenged
- State designates under terrorism and defence-trade authorities and administers ITAR separately from BIS
- FinCEN Section 311 special measures cut off correspondent access. They behave like sanctions, arise under AML law, and are not on the SDN List.
