# Reading an alert

Before you can dispose of an alert you have to understand what it is asserting. This chapter sets out the order in which to work, why the order matters, and what does not belong in the analysis at all.


**6.1.1** The four questions, in order Every alert is worked by answering four questions in sequence: what matched, what the list prohibits, whether the identifiers distinguish the parties, and whether the missing information can be obtained. What matched. Which list produced the hit, which field in your data it matched against, and which name on the list entry — the primary name or an alias, and if an alias, a strong or weak one. An alert that matched on a weak alias is materially different evidence from one that matched a primary name, and many systems do not make the distinction obvious. What the list prohibits. An SDN hit means blocking. An SSI hit means a specific directive and everything else remains lawful. An Entity List hit does not restrict a payment at all. Module 2 covered this; the point here is that it is the second question and not an afterthought, because the answer determines what a correct outcome even looks like. Whether the identifiers distinguish. Work down the hierarchy from the strongest identifier present on both sides. This is the substance of the next two chapters. Whether the missing data can be obtained. Where the identifiers are absent rather than different, the question is not what you conclude but what you go and find out.


**6.1.2** Why the sequence matters The order is not a matter of neatness. It exists because working the questions in a different order produces different answers from identical facts. The most damaging inversion is considering the transaction first. An analyst who begins by observing that the customer is long-standing, the amount is unremarkable and the payment resembles twenty others has formed a view before looking at the name. Every subsequent step is then read in the light of that view, and marginal identifier evidence is interpreted as supporting the conclusion already reached. This is not a criticism of analysts. It is how anybody assesses evidence, and it is why the identifier test comes before any consideration of the transaction rather than alongside it. The second common inversion is deciding the outcome and then finding the reason. A rationale written to justify a decision already made reads differently from one written to record how a decision was reached, and reviewers can usually tell. **6.1.3** What is not evidence A short list of considerations that appear constantly in real alert files and belong in none of them. That the transaction looks ordinary. Sanctions is an identity question, not a behavioural one. A designated party's payments look exactly like anybody else's, and the fact that a payment is unremarkable tells you nothing about who the counterparty is. That the customer is long-standing and well known. Your relationship is with your customer. The alert may concern a counterparty you have never dealt with. And even where it is the customer, a customer can be designated — length of relationship is not a fact about identity. That the amount is small. There is no de minimis in any sanctions regime. That similar alerts were discounted before. Unless the earlier discount was on the same parties with the same identifiers, it is not evidence. And if it was, the earlier decision may have been wrong. That the queue is large and the service level is breached. This is a real operational pressure and it is not an input to whether two people are the same person. That the name is common. This is the subtlest of the list, because it feels like analysis. A common name means the prior probability of coincidence is higher, which is a reason to look for identifiers — not a substitute for finding them. 'Common name, discounted' is not a rationale.


**6.1.4** Working method Three habits distinguish an analyst whose files hold up from one whose files do not. Record as you go rather than at the end. A rationale written after the decision reconstructs reasoning; one written alongside it records reasoning. The difference is visible. Name your sources. 'Date of birth differs' is weaker than 'date of birth on the customer record, captured at onboarding from passport, is 3 March 1984; the list entry shows 14 June 1971'. The second can be checked by somebody else. The first has to be taken on trust. State what you could not establish. A file that records only what was found reads as complete. A file that also records what was sought and not found is honest, and is far more useful to whoever reviews the same party next year. Figure 6.1 — The order in which an alert is worked. ## What to take from this chapter
- Four questions in order: what matched, what the list prohibits, whether identifiers distinguish, whether missing data can be obtained
- Whether the match was on a primary name or a weak alias is materially different evidence and is often not obvious in the alert
- Considering the transaction first contaminates everything after it. That is ordinary human reasoning, which is why the order is fixed
- A rationale written to justify a decision reads differently from one written to record how it was reached
- Ordinary-looking transaction, long-standing customer, small amount, large queue: none is evidence about identity
- A common name raises the prior probability of coincidence. That is a reason to look for identifiers, not a substitute for finding them
- Name your sources and record what you could not establish. Both are what make a file checkable.
