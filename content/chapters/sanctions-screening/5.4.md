Secondary identifiers A name alone rarely resolves an alert. What resolves it is everything else on the record, and how much of it the payment carries. This chapter sets out the hierarchy of identifiers, what each is worth, and why weak data on either side produces alerts nobody can close. 5.4.1  The hierarchy Identifiers differ enormously in discriminating power, and an analyst should hold the ranking clearly. At the top sit unique document numbers: passport, national identity card, tax identification, company registration, IMO number for a vessel. These are issued to be unique within their system. A mismatch on a document number is close to conclusive, and a match is very strong evidence. Below them, date of birth. Highly discriminating — the chance of two people sharing a name and an exact date of birth is small — but not unique, and frequently recorded imprecisely on sanctions records as a year or a range. Below that, place of birth and nationality. Useful, coarse, and sometimes plural on a record. Then address, which is weak for reasons covered below. Then position or title, weaker still. And at the bottom, the name itself, which in populations where a handful of names predominate is barely an identifier at all. The practical rule follows: work down the hierarchy from the strongest identifier available on both sides. An alert resolved on a passport mismatch is resolved. An alert resolved on a name being spelled slightly differently is not resolved at all. 5.4.2  Date of birth Date of birth is the workhorse of alert discounting and it repays understanding its failure modes. Sanctions records carry it in several forms: a full date, a year alone, a range of years, several alternative dates where sources disagree, or nothing. An entry showing 1971 to 1974 cannot be discounted by a payment date of birth in 1972. Payment messages rarely carry date of birth at all. Customer records usually do. This is why alerts on your own customers are far more resolvable than alerts on payment counterparties, and why the same institution can have a high discount rate in one screening context and a poor one in another. Where both sides carry a full date and they differ, that is strong evidence of different people — but not conclusive, because dates of birth on identity documents in some jurisdictions are approximate, and a first of January date is frequently a placeholder for an unknown day. An analyst should know that 1 January appears far more often in real data than chance allows. Where both sides carry a date and they match, that is strong evidence of the same person and should escalate rather than close. 5.4.3  Addresses Address is the identifier most often over-relied on, and the reasons it is weak are worth stating. 89 Sanctions records carry addresses that may be historic, may be one of several, and may be a last known location rather than a current one. A designated person's recorded address in Khartoum tells you where they were when the information was gathered. A different address in the payment is therefore poor evidence of a different person. People move, hold property in several countries, and are designated precisely at the point their circumstances are changing. Address is also the field most affected by formatting: abbreviations, transliterated street names, differing conventions for order, and free-text entry. It has one strong use. Where the payment address and the list address match, particularly at street level, that is significant corroboration and should escalate. Address is weak for discounting and strong for confirming, which is the opposite of how it is usually treated. 5.4.4  When there is nothing to work with The most common alert in most institutions is a moderate-to-strong name match with no secondary identifiers on either side. Figure 5.1 shows it. This cannot be resolved by analysis, because there is nothing to analyse. The name is similar, and no other fact distinguishes or connects the parties. An analyst who discounts it is guessing, and an analyst who blocks it is also guessing. The correct response is to obtain more information rather than to decide without it. For a customer, the CDD record may hold what the payment does not. For a counterparty, the customer can be asked, the correspondent can be asked through a request for information, and the underlying commercial documentation may name the party more fully. Where information cannot be obtained, the alert escalates. Escalation is not an admission of failure — it is the correct disposition when a decision cannot be supported. What is not acceptable is a rationale asserting a conclusion the file does not support, and the most common form of that is discounting on the basis that the transaction looks ordinary. Institutions should measure this. A high proportion of alerts closed with no secondary identifier recorded is a control finding, whichever way those alerts were closed. SCENARIO — NOTHING TO DISCOUNT ON Figure 5.1 is on your screen. A name score of 92 against an SDN entry. The list carries date of birth, place of birth, nationality and a passport number. The payment carries a name, an address in Dubai, and nothing else. The customer is a long-standing corporate importer and the payment is one of many similar ones. Question.  What is the disposition? Answer.  Not a discount, and not a block. Neither is supportable on what is in front of you. The list address is Khartoum and the payment address is Dubai, but address is weak for discounting — people move and hold property in several places — so that difference does not resolve it. The transaction looking ordinary is not evidence about identity and must not enter the rationale. The correct step is to seek the missing data: ask the customer for the beneficiary's full identifying details, or issue a request for information through the correspondent. If date of birth or a document number comes back and differs, the alert is resolved and the rationale records which identifier resolved it. If nothing comes back, the alert escalates with the position stated plainly — a strong name match, no secondary identifiers available on the payment side, information requested and not provided. That is a defensible record. 'Discounted, customer known to the bank' is not. 90 WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — The hierarchy: document numbers, then date of birth, then place of birth and nationality, then address, then title, then the name itself. — Work down from the strongest identifier present on both sides.
