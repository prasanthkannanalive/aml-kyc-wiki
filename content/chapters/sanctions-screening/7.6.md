Vessels in trade documents This closing chapter is about what a financial institution can actually do. Most of the analysis above requires maritime data a bank does not hold. What a bank does hold is documents, and there is more in them than is usually looked for. 7.6.1 Where the vessel appears In a documentary credit the vessel is usually named in the bill of lading and often in the credit itself where a specific carriage is required. Ports of loading and discharge are stated. The carrier is identified. In a charter party the vessel is the subject of the contract, with her particulars — name, IMO, flag, tonnage, class — stated in the preamble. In marine insurance documents she is named with her particulars and the voyage described. In a certificate of origin the goods rather than the vessel are described, but the routing implied by the document may be checkable against the vessel's track. The first practical step in most institutions is simply to extract the IMO number where it is present and screen it. That sounds trivial and frequently is not being done, because the number sits in a scanned document rather than in a structured field. 7.6.2 What to check in the documents themselves Six checks, none of which requires maritime data. Is the IMO number valid. The check digit rule from Chapter 7.2 takes seconds and catches fabricated numbers. Is the vessel type consistent with the cargo. A bulk carrier named on documents for a liquid cargo is a discrepancy visible on the paper alone. Is the voyage duration plausible for the route. A passage from the Baltic to South Asia does not take eleven days, and a bill of lading dated inconsistently with the arrival is a direct contradiction. Does the load port support the cargo. A refined product loading at a port with no refinery or terminal capacity is a question worth asking, and the answer is public. Is the freight rate consistent with the market. Chapter 7.5 explains why this matters beyond commercial curiosity. Does the intermediary chain have a commercial rationale. Two trading companies inserted between a producer and a buyer, incorporated recently and in the same jurisdiction, are performing a function somebody should be able to describe. 7.6.3 What to escalate and how Maritime findings escalate to trade compliance or to a specialist function, not to sanctions alert review, because the questions that follow require data and expertise a screening analyst does not have. What makes an escalation useful is the same as in Module 6: state what was observed, from which document, and what it is consistent with. 'Bill of lading names MV Sea Harmony, IMO 9271234, 124 matched to OFAC SDN entry dated 8 February 2026; load port stated as Fujairah; freight rate approximately three times market for the route' is a complete statement that another person can act on. Where the finding is an inconsistency rather than a match, say which two documents disagree and how. An inconsistency stated without its two sides cannot be verified by anybody else. And record what you did not check. A bank has no AIS data, no draught history and usually no ownership chain for a single-ship company in Panama. Saying so is not a weakness in the file; it tells the next person where the analysis stopped and why. 7.6.4 The honest limit This module has described a discipline that in its full form belongs to maritime specialists, commercial data providers and government analysts. A screening analyst in a bank will not perform most of it. What a bank can do is narrower and worth doing properly: screen the IMO number where it appears, validate it, check the internal consistency of the documents it holds, notice a freight rate that does not make sense, and escalate with a statement precise enough to be acted on. What a bank should not do is claim more. A trade file recorded as vessel screening completed, where the only step taken was a name search against a list, overstates the control in exactly the way Module 5 warned about — and vessel names, as Figure 7.1 shows, are the identifier least worth searching on. The accurate formulation is what was screened, against what, and what was not examined. That is a smaller claim than most institutions make and it is the one that holds up.
