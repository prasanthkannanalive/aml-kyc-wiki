# Trade measures, arms embargoes and dual-use goods

Financial institutions frequently treat trade measures as somebody else's problem. They are not, because trade finance instruments are payments with goods attached, and because dual-use controls are the point at which proliferation regimes reach ordinary commercial equipment.


**3.4.1** Import and export prohibitions Trade sanctions restrict the movement of goods rather than the identity of parties. They take three forms: prohibitions on export to a jurisdiction, prohibitions on import from one, and prohibitions on specified goods regardless of direction. Import prohibitions have become significant since 2022. Restrictions on Russian oil, coal, steel, gold, diamonds and a lengthening list of commodities operate on the origin of the goods, which is a documentary question rather than a party question. Origin is where evasion concentrates, because it is asserted on paper. Goods are transhipped through an intermediate country, repackaged, blended, or accompanied by falsified certificates of origin. Russian crude refined in a third country and exported as that country's product is the developed example, and it is lawful in some jurisdictions and prohibited in others depending on how each treats substantial transformation. For a bank financing trade, the relevant discipline is documentary. Does the certificate of origin come from a credible issuing body. Does the routing make commercial sense. Does the volume of a commodity exported by an intermediate country materially exceed its production capacity — a check that has repeatedly identified transhipment.


**3.4.2** Arms embargoes Arms embargoes are the oldest form of targeted trade measure and appear in almost every UN regime. They prohibit the supply, sale or transfer of arms and related materiel to a jurisdiction or party, and usually extend to technical assistance, training, financing and financial assistance related to military activities. The financing limb matters for banks. Providing finance or financial assistance connected to a prohibited arms transfer is itself prohibited, which means an institution can breach an arms embargo without any goods passing through its hands. Related materiel is construed broadly and reaches beyond weapons to spare parts, vehicles, communications equipment and in some regimes items with obvious military application even where not weapons themselves. The practical difficulty is that many controlled items look ordinary in a payment message. A description reading 'vehicle parts' or 'communications equipment' does not surface a problem, and the underlying commercial documentation is where the answer sits.


**3.4.3** Dual-use goods Dual-use items have both civilian and military applications, and they are the central concern of non-proliferation regimes. The category includes machine tools, certain valves and pumps, carbon fibre, specific semiconductors, high-precision measuring equipment, chemicals and certain software. Control is exercised through lists organised by technical parameter rather than by product name. An item is controlled if it meets stated thresholds — a machine tool above a specified positioning accuracy, a frequency converter above a specified output. Two apparently identical items may be controlled and uncontrolled depending on a specification the payment message does not mention. The multilateral export control regimes generate these lists: the Wassenaar Arrangement for conventional arms and dual-use items, the Nuclear Suppliers Group, the Australia Group for chemical and biological items, and the Missile Technology Control Regime. National control lists derive from them, which is why they broadly correspond across jurisdictions. Since 2022 a further category has become operationally important: common high-priority items. These are ordinary commercial goods — integrated circuits, electronic components, some machine tools — identified as being found in recovered Russian weapon systems, published as a priority list by the US, EU, UK and Japan jointly. They are not exotic. Several are consumer-grade components available from ordinary distributors, and the control problem is diversion through third countries rather than direct export.


**3.4.4** End use and end user Where an item is controlled, the licensing question turns on who will use it and for what. This produces the two checks that dominate export compliance and appear in trade finance review. The end-user check asks who will actually receive and use the goods, as distinct from who is buying them. Distributors, freight forwarders and trading companies are intermediaries, and an end-user statement naming the intermediary answers nothing. The end-use check asks what the item will be used for. A statement of civilian use is a representation, and its credibility depends on whether the stated use is consistent with the item, the quantity and the buyer's business. The red flags in this area are well documented and mostly turn on incongruity. A buyer with no track record in the relevant industry. A quantity or specification exceeding any plausible civilian requirement. A customer declining installation, training or servicing that would normally accompany the equipment. Unusual routing, or a delivery address that is a freight forwarder rather than a facility. Willingness to pay a premium or to pay in cash. And a reluctance to provide end-use information at all, which is the strongest signal of the set.


## Scenario — THE OSCILLOSCOPES

A letter of credit is presented for confirmation. The applicant is a trading company in a Central Asian country, the goods are high-specification oscilloscopes, and the quantity is 240 units. The company was incorporated eleven months ago. The stated end use is 'educational and laboratory purposes'. No party to the transaction appears on any list. **Question.** The screening is clean. What should concern the analyst? **Answer.** Almost everything except the names. The quantity is the first problem: 240 high-specification oscilloscopes is a volume no educational institution requires and is inconsistent with the stated use. The applicant is eleven months old, which gives no track record in a technical distribution business.

The jurisdiction has been repeatedly identified as a transhipment route for controlled items diverted to Russia, and high-specification test equipment appears on the common high-priority items list. The correct handling is not a sanctions disposition — no name is designated — but escalation to trade compliance with the specific question of whether the items are controlled at their stated specification and whether a licence exists. The wider lesson is that this transaction would pass every name-screening control an institution operates, which is precisely why trade measures cannot be managed by name screening alone.


## What to take from this chapter
- Trade measures restrict goods, not parties. Origin is a documentary question and origin documents are what evaders falsify
- Export volumes from an intermediate country exceeding its production capacity is a reliable transhipment indicator
- Arms embargoes extend to financing and financial assistance, so a bank can breach one without goods passing through it
- Dual-use items are controlled by technical parameter, not by product name. Two identical-looking items may differ in status
- Common high-priority items are ordinary commercial components found in recovered weapon systems. The problem is diversion, not exotic technology
- An end-user statement naming a distributor answers nothing. The question is who uses the item
- The strongest end-use red flags are incongruities: quantity, specification, buyer track record, declined servicing, and reluctance to answer.
