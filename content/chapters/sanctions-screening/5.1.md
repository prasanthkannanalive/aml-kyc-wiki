# Lists, sources and currency

Before matching there is data. This chapter covers where lists originate, what a record holds, how often it changes, and the gap between when a designation takes effect and when your system knows about it.


**5.1.1** Primary sources and consolidated lists Every list you screen has an authoritative publisher. OFAC publishes the SDN List and its companions. The EU publishes a consolidated list of persons subject to restrictive measures. The UK publishes the UK Sanctions List through OFSI and the FCDO. The UN publishes the Consolidated List maintained by its sanctions committees. Each is available in machine-readable formats — XML, CSV, JSON depending on the publisher — alongside human-readable versions. Most institutions do not consume these directly. They subscribe to a commercial data provider that aggregates the primary sources, normalises them into a common schema, adds enrichment such as alias expansion and ownership assessments, and delivers updates on a defined cycle. That aggregation is valuable and it introduces a dependency worth understanding. The provider decides how to normalise. It decides how to represent an entry that carries fourteen aliases, whether a date range for a date of birth becomes one record or several, and how to handle a designation whose source publishes it in a format the schema did not anticipate. Those decisions affect what your engine matches against. The practical consequence is that when an entry behaves oddly — a designation you expected to alert on and did not, an identifier that appears blank — the question is not only what the engine did but what arrived from the provider, and what the provider did to the source. Checking the primary publisher's own record is a legitimate and underused step.


**5.1.2** What a sanctions record contains A well-populated entry for an individual may carry: the primary name; a set of aliases distinguished as strong or weak; date of birth, sometimes as a range or a year alone; place of birth; nationality, sometimes several; passport and national identification numbers; addresses, current and historic; a title or position; and the programme or regime under which the designation was made. A poorly populated entry may carry a name and a country. Both exist on the same list, and the difference determines whether an alert can be discounted at all. Entity records carry a different set: registered name, trading names, registration number, registered address, and sometimes the names of connected individuals. Vessels carry IMO number, flag, tonnage, call sign and previous names. Aircraft carry registration and serial numbers. Cryptocurrency addresses appear as identifiers on some entries and behave unlike anything else on the list. The identifier that matters most is the one with the least ambiguity. A passport number is close to unique. A date of birth is highly discriminating. A name is barely discriminating at all in populations where a small number of names are extremely common. Chapter 5.4 works through the hierarchy.


**5.1.3** Update cycles and the currency gap A designation takes effect at a moment defined by the imposing authority. Your system knows about it at a later moment. The interval between the two is a period during which you are obliged to comply with something you cannot see, and every institution has one. The interval has several components: the publisher's release timing, the provider's ingestion and processing, the delivery schedule, and your own load and activation process. An institution loading files once daily overnight has an interval of up to twenty-four hours plus the provider's own lag. One consuming intra-day feeds has a shorter one. This is a risk to be managed and stated, not eliminated. What matters is that the institution knows its actual interval, that the interval is proportionate to the risk of its business, and that there is a process for expedited loading when a major designation event is announced — the large packages announced with advance notice are precisely when an overnight cycle is least adequate. The second currency question is what happens to transactions already in flight. A payment screened at 08:00 against a list loaded at 06:00 was screened against the list as it then stood. If a designation takes effect at 10:00, the payment was screened correctly and the party is now designated. Whether anything must be done depends on where the funds are and whether the institution still holds them, which is a different analysis from screening.


**5.1.4** Internal lists and why they exist Alongside published lists, most institutions maintain internal ones, and an analyst should know why a hit came from one. Watch lists carry parties the institution has decided to monitor or refuse for its own reasons — previous suspicious activity, adverse media, relationships exited. A hit is a policy matter, not a legal prohibition, and the disposition must say so. Good guy lists, or whitelists, carry parties previously investigated and discounted, so the same alert does not return every time they transact. They are a productivity control and they carry a specific danger: a party discounted in 2023 may have been designated in 2026, and a whitelist that suppresses alerts without re-testing against current lists will hide exactly the alert that matters. Any whitelist should be time-limited, re-validated, and cleared automatically on a list update affecting the entry. PEP lists are a different obligation entirely — a politically exposed person is subject to enhanced due diligence under AML rules, not to any sanctions prohibition. Presenting PEP hits in the same 81 queue as sanctions hits, without distinguishing them, produces the same category error as mixing the Entity List with the SDN List.


## What to take from this chapter
- Every list has an authoritative publisher; most institutions consume it through a provider that normalises and enriches it
- Normalisation decisions affect what your engine matches against. Checking the primary source is a legitimate step when an entry behaves oddly
- Records vary from fully populated to a name and a country, and that variation determines whether an alert can be discounted
- The interval between a designation taking effect and your system knowing about it is a real exposure. Know yours and have an expedited path
- Whitelists suppress alerts and must be time-limited and cleared on list updates, or they will hide the one that matters
- PEP status is an AML obligation, not a sanctions prohibition. Mixing the queues repeats the Entity List error.
