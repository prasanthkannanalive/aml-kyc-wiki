# Other jurisdictions and conflicts of law

Beyond the three largest regimes, a group of jurisdictions matters either because your institution operates there or because their divergences create genuine conflicts. This chapter closes with the situation nobody wants: two states requiring opposite things.


**2.6.1** Canada, Australia and Japan Canada operates under the Special Economic Measures Act for autonomous measures and the United Nations Act for UN-derived ones, with the Justice for Victims of Corrupt Foreign Officials Act serving as its Magnitsky equivalent. Global Affairs Canada administers designations. Canada has at times designated ahead of its allies and its list contains names found nowhere else, so it is not safely treated as a subset of the US or EU lists. Australia maintains both UN-derived and autonomous measures under the Autonomous Sanctions Act 2011, administered by the Australian Sanctions Office within the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade. It operates a permit system for activity that would otherwise be prohibited, and maintains a consolidated list combining both sources. Japan implements sanctions primarily through the Foreign Exchange and Foreign Trade Act, with the Ministry of Finance responsible for financial measures and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry for trade measures. Japan historically aligned closely with UN measures and was reluctant to adopt autonomous ones; that changed substantially from 2022, when it joined the Russia measures alongside the G7.


**2.6.2** Switzerland and Singapore These two are worth understanding properly because both are major financial centres whose policy positions are frequently misdescribed. Switzerland is not an EU member and has a constitutional tradition of neutrality. It decides regime by regime whether to align with EU measures, and the State Secretariat for Economic Affairs administers those it adopts. In practice it has aligned with the major EU packages including on Russia, but each alignment is a decision taken at the time rather than an automatic consequence of EU adoption. Given the concentration of cross-border wealth management in Switzerland, the divergences that do exist matter disproportionately to the assets affected. Singapore has historically implemented UN measures through the Monetary Authority of Singapore and related legislation, and has been reluctant to adopt autonomous sanctions on the view that multilateral measures are the appropriate basis. It departed from that position in 2022 with financial measures against Russia — a significant shift for a major Asian financial centre and one worth noting because it was explicitly framed as exceptional rather than as a change of general policy.


**2.6.3** Blocking statutes Occasionally two jurisdictions require opposite things: one prohibits a dealing, another prohibits refusing it. This is not theoretical and there is no clean answer. The principal instrument is Council Regulation 2271/96, the EU Blocking Statute, originally adopted in 1996 in response to US measures on Cuba, Iran and Libya. It does four things. It prohibits EU operators from complying, actively or by deliberate omission, with the listed 40 extraterritorial US measures. It requires them to inform the Commission where their interests are affected. It provides for recovery of damages caused by application of those measures. And it denies recognition and enforcement to foreign judgments giving effect to them. The conflict is real and unresolved. An EU bank asked to reject a payment because a US secondary sanction would otherwise expose it faces a prohibition under the Blocking Statute on complying with that very measure. The Commission may authorise compliance where non-compliance would seriously damage the operator's interests, and authorisations have been granted, but the process takes time and does not remove the underlying tension. Canada, the United Kingdom and Mexico maintain their own blocking provisions with narrower scope. China introduced blocking rules in 2021 through MOFCOM Order No. 1 and the Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law, which go further than the EU model: they additionally provide for counter-measures against parties that comply with foreign sanctions against Chinese entities, meaning compliance itself can attract retaliation.


**2.6.4** Bank Melli and what an analyst does The Court of Justice of the European Union decided Bank Melli Iran v Telekom Deutschland in December 2021, and it sharpened the position considerably. Telekom had terminated contracts with Bank Melli, an Iranian bank designated by the United States, giving no reason. Bank Melli argued the termination was compliance with US secondary sanctions and therefore prohibited by the Blocking Statute. The Court held three things that matter. First, the prohibition applies even where no US authority has instructed the EU operator to comply — voluntary anticipatory compliance is caught. Second, an EU operator terminating a contract must be able to establish that its reasons were unconnected to the blocked measures. Third, national courts should, in effect, treat an unexplained termination of a designated party's contract as evidence that the blocked measures were the reason. The practical effect is that an EU operator cannot quietly exit a relationship with an Iranian counterparty and decline to explain why. The commercial reason must exist and must be demonstrable. For an analyst, the operational rule is short. A conflict of this kind is never an analyst-level decision. It is a legal and senior-management matter, and the escalation path should exist before it is needed. What an analyst must do is recognise the shape of the problem: a hit arising from a foreign measure, on a counterparty in a jurisdiction with blocking provisions, where declining the business may itself be unlawful. Recognising it and escalating is the correct disposition. Deciding it is not.


## Scenario — THE INSTRUCTION YOU CANNOT LAWFULLY FOLLOW

A French bank holds an account for an Iranian trading company. The company is not designated by the EU. It is designated by the United States, and the bank's US correspondent has indicated informally that maintaining the relationship is creating difficulty. The relationship manager proposes closing the account, citing commercial reasons, and asks compliance to confirm. **Question.** What is the compliance function's answer? **Answer.** Not a confirmation. The proposal is to terminate a relationship with a US-designated Iranian party, prompted by pressure connected to US measures — which is precisely the anticipatory compliance Bank Melli holds to be prohibited under the Blocking Statute.

Citing commercial reasons does not help if the commercial reason is the US designation, and after Bank Melli the burden of demonstrating an unconnected reason falls on the bank. The correct handling is escalation to legal and senior management, with three questions on the table: whether a genuine and demonstrable commercial ground exists independent of the US measures, whether an authorisation from the Commission should be sought, and how the correspondent relationship is to be managed in the meantime. What the analyst records is the escalation and its grounds. What the analyst does not do is approve the closure, and does not record it as a routine de-risking decision.


## What to take from this chapter
- Canada, Australia and Japan each maintain autonomous regimes; Canada's list is not a subset of anyone else's
- Switzerland aligns with EU measures regime by regime, as a decision each time rather than automatically
- Singapore is UN-focused by policy and treated its 2022 Russia measures as an explicit exception
- EU Regulation 2271/96 prohibits EU operators from complying with specified extraterritorial US measures, including by deliberate omission
- China's 2021 rules go further than blocking: they provide for counter-measures against parties that comply
- Bank Melli (2021): the prohibition bites without any US instruction, and an unexplained termination is treated as evidence of prohibited compliance
