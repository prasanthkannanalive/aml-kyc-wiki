# Why sanctions are imposed

Regimes are not interchangeable. Each is built to serve a policy objective, and the objective determines who gets designated, how far the measures reach into trade, and what exemptions exist. An analyst who knows only that a name is on a list is missing the information that explains why the list is drawn as it is.


**1.3.1** International peace and security This is the founding objective and the legal basis for United Nations measures. Chapter VII of the UN Charter permits the Security Council to impose measures where it determines a threat to international peace and security exists, and every UN sanctions regime rests on that determination. Regimes of this type respond to armed conflict, coups, and territorial aggression. Designations concentrate on military and political leadership, armed groups, and the entities that finance them. An arms embargo is almost always present, and travel bans are common. For screening, these regimes tend to produce designations with relatively good identifying data — senior figures whose dates of birth, nationalities and passport numbers are documented — but also a long tail of armed-group figures and aliases with very little.


**1.3.2** Non-proliferation Non-proliferation regimes target the spread of nuclear, chemical and biological weapons and their delivery systems. The Iran and North Korea programmes are the largest and most developed examples. These regimes reach considerably further into trade than others, because the central concern is dual-use goods: items with entirely legitimate civilian applications that also contribute to a weapons programme. Centrifuge components, certain valves and pumps, carbon fibre, machine tools, specific electronics. The item is not inherently military, which is precisely what makes the control difficult. The practical consequence is that screening a proliferation regime involves goods and end-use questions alongside party identification. A trade finance analyst reviewing a letter of credit under a proliferation regime is looking at the commodity description, the end user, the stated end use and the routing, not only at the names. North Korea deserves specific mention because the regime is unusually broad. It restricts sectors of the economy, prohibits joint ventures, caps refined petroleum imports, bans the employment of North Korean workers abroad, and designates a large fleet of vessels. It is the closest thing remaining to a comprehensive regime and the one where maritime evasion is most developed.


**1.3.3** Counter-terrorism Counter-terrorism regimes are the oldest continuously operating targeted framework. UNSCR 1267, adopted in 1999, established the designation regime for Al-Qaida and the Taliban; it was expanded substantially after September 2001 and now covers ISIL as well. These regimes designate individuals and organisations rather than states, which makes them person-centred in a way that has direct screening consequences. They carry the heaviest 17 transliteration burden of any regime family: names originating in Arabic, Farsi, Urdu, Pashto and other non-Latin scripts, rendered into Latin characters by different conventions in different lists, with the same individual appearing as three or four distinct strings. They also tend to carry extensive alias data — noms de guerre, kunya, variant spellings — which improves recall and degrades precision. A counter-terrorism list will generate a disproportionate share of your false positives, and the reason is not a defect in the list.


**1.3.4** Human rights and corruption The newest major category. The framework originates in the US Magnitsky Act of 2012, adopted in response to the death in custody of Sergei Magnitsky, and was generalised by the Global Magnitsky Act of 2016 permitting designation for serious human rights abuse and significant corruption anywhere in the world. The European Union, the United Kingdom and Canada have adopted equivalent regimes. These regimes are person-centred by design and country-agnostic. That combination makes them harder to screen than they look. A country-based regime lets an institution focus its enhanced diligence on a defined geography; a Magnitsky-type regime designates a prison official in one country, a procurement officer in another and a militia commander in a third, none of whom is senior enough to be a public figure with a well-documented profile. The identifying data is often correspondingly thin, and the parties designated are frequently not the customers of major international banks but the beneficial owners of small companies that are.


**1.3.5** Counter-narcotics and cyber Counter-narcotics measures are dominated by the US Foreign Narcotics Kingpin Designation Act of 1999, which permits designation of significant foreign narcotics traffickers and, importantly, the businesses and family members through which they operate. Kingpin designations frequently cover extended commercial networks — restaurants, football clubs, pharmacies — which produces alerts on entities with no obvious connection to narcotics. Cyber regimes are the newest and technically the most distinct. They designate state-linked intrusion groups, ransomware operators and the infrastructure supporting them. The distinguishing feature for screening is that these regimes increasingly designate cryptocurrency addresses as identifiers rather than names. That matters more than it first appears. A wallet address does not behave like a name. It has no aliases, no transliterations and no fuzzy variants — it either matches exactly or it does not. But it also has none of the secondary identifiers that let you discount a false positive, and blockchain analysis rather than name matching is the relevant technique. Institutions that route crypto-address screening through a name-matching engine get the worst of both. ## What to take from this chapter
- Objective drives design. Proliferation regimes reach into goods; human rights regimes reach into individuals anywhere
- UN measures rest on Chapter VII of the Charter — a determination that peace and security are threatened
- Counter-terrorism regimes carry the heaviest transliteration burden and generate a disproportionate share of false positives
- Magnitsky-type regimes designate relatively junior officials with thin identifying data, often as beneficial owners
- Cyber regimes designate wallet addresses, which match exactly but offer nothing to discount on.
