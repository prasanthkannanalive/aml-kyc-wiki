The European Union test The EU test has two limbs where the US rule has one. This chapter covers the ownership limb, the control limb, and the guidance that has developed around both — including the parts that remain genuinely unsettled. 4.3.1  The ownership limb EU restrictive measures apply to funds and economic resources owned, held or controlled by designated persons, and the Council's guidance treats an entity as owned where a designated person holds more than 50 per cent of the proprietary rights, or has a majority interest in it. The threshold is more than 50 per cent, not fifty or more. A precise 50-50 joint venture between a designated person and a clean party is therefore not owned within the meaning of the EU limb — and is caught in the United States, where the rule reads fifty per cent or more. That is a real divergence and it is not a drafting accident on either side. Ownership may be direct or indirect, and the guidance addresses chains. The EU approach has generally been to look through, so that an entity majority-owned by an entity that is itself majority-owned by a designated person is caught. The drafting is less prescriptive than OFAC's cascade and the guidance leans more heavily on the control limb to reach structures the arithmetic does not. Aggregation across several designated persons is recognised. Practice has been less categorical than under the US rule, and the safer working position for a group operating in both jurisdictions is to aggregate: the US rule requires it, and applying two different arithmetics to the same shareholder register is how errors happen. 4.3.2  The control limb Control is not defined by a percentage, and this is where the EU test reaches furthest beyond the US position. The Council's guidance sets out criteria rather than a definition. They include: the right to appoint or remove a majority of the members of the administrative, management or supervisory body; having used one's voting rights to appoint such a majority; controlling alone a majority of shareholders' voting rights under an agreement with other shareholders; the right to exercise a dominant influence under an agreement or a provision of the entity's constitution; the power to exercise such dominant influence in fact; the right to use all or part of the assets of the entity; managing the business on a unified basis while presenting consolidated accounts; and sharing joint and several liability for the entity's financial obligations or guaranteeing them. That last pair is worth noticing because it is unlike the others. Guaranteeing an entity's obligations, or sharing liability for them, is evidence of control in the EU framework — which catches structures where a designated person holds nothing and stands behind everything. The guidance also makes clear that these criteria are indicative and that the presence of one may be enough, depending on the circumstances. It is not a scoring system and there is no threshold number of criteria. 66 4.3.3  Rebutting the presumption The guidance contemplates that a finding of control can be displaced. Where an entity meets one of the criteria, it may be possible to establish on the facts that the designated person does not in fact control it. This matters practically because it opens a route that the arithmetic rule does not. An entity that shares a guarantor with a designated person, or whose board was historically appointed by them, may be able to demonstrate genuine independence — a changed board, an unwound guarantee, an independent management structure. The evidential burden is real and it sits with whoever asserts independence. Assertions from the entity itself are the starting point and are not sufficient on their own. For an analyst, the relevance is in how an escalation is framed. Identifying a control indicator is not the same as concluding that control exists, and stating the indicator alongside anything that cuts the other way is a more useful escalation than one that presents only the case for control. 4.3.4  What remains unsettled Two areas are genuinely open and an analyst should know they are open rather than assume a settled answer exists. The first is aggregation of minority holdings under the ownership limb. Whether two designated persons each holding 30 per cent produce an owned entity is treated as settled under the US rule and has been handled less consistently in EU practice, with guidance developing over time. The second is how far indirect control reaches through a chain of entities none of which is majority-owned. The control criteria apply to a relationship between a designated person and an entity; applying them to a relationship mediated through three intermediate companies is a question the guidance addresses less clearly than the ownership cascade. Where an analyst encounters either, the correct handling is to say so. An escalation stating that the position is unsettled and identifying why is more useful than one that manufactures certainty in either direction. Its shares are held by two individuals with no designations, both of whom have run the business for fifteen years. A recently designated individual holds no shares, sits on no board and holds no office. He has, however, personally guaranteed the company's principal bank facility since 2019, and the guarantee remains in place. Question.  Is the company caught by the EU test? Answer.  Possibly, and on a limb most analysts do not have in mind. The ownership limb is not engaged — he owns nothing. But guaranteeing the entity's financial obligations is one of the Council's stated control indicators, and it is there precisely because standing behind a company financially is a form of control that leaves no trace in a shareholder register. The correct handling is to escalate, setting out the guarantee, its date and its scope, and noting the facts that cut the other way: the shareholders are independent and long-standing, the business predates the guarantee, and the guarantee was given years before the designation. That last point matters — a guarantee given in 2019 is not a post-designation arrangement and does not carry the circumvention indicators of one. Whether the indicator is displaced on those facts is a legal determination and not the analyst's. Recording both sides is.
