# What a sanction is

A sanction is easy to recognise and surprisingly hard to define precisely. This chapter sets out what one is in law, why the obligation to enforce it lands on private institutions rather than on governments, and how to tell a sanction apart from the three things it is most often confused with.


**1.1.1** The definition A sanction is a restriction imposed by a government or an international body on dealings with a named person, entity, vessel, sector or country. It is an instrument of foreign policy, delivered through the financial and trade system rather than through diplomacy or armed force. It sits between the two: stronger than a protest, weaker than a war. Three elements are always present. There is an imposing authority, which may be the United Nations Security Council, a national government, or a bloc such as the European Union. There is a target, which since the early 2000s is usually a named party rather than a country. And there is a prohibited act, which may be transferring funds, providing a service, exporting an item, or simply holding property without freezing it. What distinguishes a sanction from most other foreign-policy instruments is who has to carry it out. A tariff is collected by customs. A diplomatic expulsion is executed by a foreign ministry. A sanction, once imposed, is enforced overwhelmingly by banks, insurers, shipping companies, exporters and payment processors — private organisations with no role in deciding the policy and every obligation to implement it.


**1.1.2** Why the obligation lands on you This is worth dwelling on, because it explains the existence of your entire function. A government cannot freeze an asset it does not hold. If a designated person keeps a account at a commercial bank, no ministry can reach into that account. What the state can do is impose a legal obligation on the bank: freeze it, report it, and do not release it without a licence. The state then supervises and penalises the bank for failure, not the designated person. The same logic runs through the whole regime. The state cannot inspect every payment message, so it requires payment institutions to screen them. It cannot examine every shipment, so it requires exporters to check end users. It cannot know who owns a shell company in a third country, so it requires the institution onboarding that company to establish beneficial ownership. The consequence is a structural one that shapes your work every day. You are performing a public function inside a private organisation. The bank does not benefit commercially from screening — 10 it is a cost centre that generates friction with customers. It exists because the alternative is regulatory penalty, and in the largest cases, loss of the ability to clear US dollars. That framing matters when you are deciding how much effort an alert deserves. You are not protecting your employer's revenue. You are discharging an obligation your employer has been given, and the record you leave is the evidence that it was discharged.


**1.1.3** Sanctions and AML answer different questions The two functions usually sit in the same department, share some of the same data, and are frequently performed by the same people. They are not the same discipline and confusing them produces real errors. Anti-money laundering is concerned with the origin and purpose of funds. It asks whether money is the proceeds of crime, or is being moved to disguise its origin. The analysis is behavioural and probabilistic: does this pattern of activity make sense for this customer, and if not, is there an innocent explanation. The output is a judgement about suspicion, and the response is a report to a financial intelligence unit. Sanctions are concerned with the identity of a party. The question is not where the money came from but whether you are permitted to deal with this person at all. The analysis is binary and legal: either the counterparty is the designated party or they are not. There is no threshold of suspicion, no materiality, and no de minimis. The response is not a report but a block. The practical consequences of the distinction are sharp. A payment of a hundred dollars from a designated party's account is prohibited exactly as a payment of a hundred million would be. A perfectly clean commercial payment from a legitimate manufacturer is prohibited if that manufacturer is designated. And a highly suspicious payment from a party on no list at all is an AML matter that sanctions screening will correctly ignore. Analysts moving from AML into sanctions frequently import the wrong instinct: they look for a story that makes the activity make sense. In sanctions, the story is irrelevant. The only question is whether the party in front of you is the party on the list.


**1.1.4** Sanctions and export controls apply different tests Export controls restrict what goods, software and technology may be supplied and to whom, on the basis of what the item is and where it is going. Sanctions restrict who you may deal with, on the basis of who they are. The tests are separate and an institution can fail one while passing the other. In the United States the separation is institutional as well as legal. Sanctions are administered by the Office of Foreign Assets Control within the Treasury. Export controls are administered by the Bureau of Industry and Security within the Department of Commerce, under an entirely different body of regulation. BIS maintains its own lists — the Entity List, the Denied Persons List, the Unverified List — and a designation on those lists restricts the export of controlled items to that party. It does not freeze assets and it does not prohibit financial dealings generally. This produces one of the most common category errors in screening. An institution loads the BIS Entity List into its payment screening system alongside the OFAC SDN List, an alert fires on an Entity List name, and the analyst blocks a payment that was never prohibited. The customer is denied service unlawfully and the institution has no basis for the block if challenged. The reverse error is equally common. An exporter checks the SDN List, finds the customer clean, and ships a controlled item to a party on the Entity List. The party was not sanctioned; the shipment was still a violation. The remedy is not to stop screening both. It is to record which list produced the hit and to give the analyst a different disposition path for each. If your alert shows only that a name matched, without which list and what that list prohibits, no analyst can reach the right answer.


**1.1.5** De-risking is a choice; a sanction is not Institutions routinely decline business that is entirely lawful. They exit correspondent relationships in jurisdictions they consider high-risk, close accounts in customer categories that generate too many alerts, and refuse transactions connected to countries under partial measures even where the specific dealing is permitted. That is de-risking. It is a commercial and risk-appetite decision, made by the institution, reversible by the institution, and answerable to nobody but the institution's own governance. It is not a legal prohibition and it should never be described as one. A sanction is a legal prohibition. It is not subject to appetite, it cannot be waived internally, and declining to apply it is an offence. The confusion runs in both directions and both are damaging. Declining lawful business because it feels sanctioned denies service to customers entitled to it, and in humanitarian and remittance contexts it has measurable human cost. Processing prohibited business because nobody treated the prohibition as mandatory is a violation, and the fact that a commercial team considered it acceptable risk is not a defence. When you write a disposition, be precise about which you are applying. "Blocked under the EU asset freeze at Article 2 of Regulation 269/2014" is a legal statement. "Rejected in line with the firm's appetite for Belarus exposure" is a commercial one. Both can be correct. Recording one as the other is not.


## Scenario — THE CLEAN PAYMENT YOU MUST BLOCK

A long-standing corporate customer, a European engineering firm with an unblemished record, instructs a payment of €48,000 to a supplier for machine parts. The invoice is genuine, the goods exist, the price is at market, and the customer has made twenty similar payments over three years. Your screening system raises an alert on the beneficiary, which matches an entity designated under the EU Russia regime two weeks ago. **Question.** The transaction shows no indicator of money laundering whatsoever. Does that matter? **Answer.** No. The cleanliness of the funds is an AML question and this is not an AML alert. The only question is whether the beneficiary is the designated entity.

If it is, the payment is prohibited and any funds within reach must be frozen — not returned to the customer, which would itself be making funds available indirectly. The customer's good record is relevant to how you communicate, not to whether you block. Note also that the designation is two weeks old: the twenty prior payments were lawful when made, and their existence is not evidence that this one is.


## What to take from this chapter
- A sanction has three parts: an imposing authority, a named target, and a prohibited act
- The obligation is pushed from the state onto private institutions, which is why the function exists inside your bank rather than inside a ministry
- AML asks where money came from. Sanctions ask who you are dealing with. Clean money to a designated party is still prohibited
- Export controls test the item; sanctions test the party. An Entity List hit does not prohibit a payment
- De-risking is an appetite decision. A sanction is law. Never record one as the other.
