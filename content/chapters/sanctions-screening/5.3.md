Names that break matching Most false positives are not engine failures. They are the predictable consequence of applying a matching algorithm to names that were never written in the alphabet being matched. This chapter covers the naming conventions that cause the most difficulty and what an analyst should notice about each. 5.3.1  Transliteration Transliteration is the rendering of a name from one script into another, and there is rarely one correct answer. The Arabic name commonly written Mohammed appears as Mohamed, Muhammad, Muhammed, Mohammad, Mehmet, Mohamud and others, all representing the same underlying name. The Russian surname rendered Yeltsin in English is Eltsine in French and Jelzin in German. The Chinese surname written Zhang in Pinyin appears as Chang in Wade-Giles. Different lists transliterate differently, and the same list may contain several transliterations of the same name as separate aliases. Payment data reflects whatever convention the originating institution used, which may follow the customer's passport, their own national convention, or nothing in particular. The consequence is that a single individual can legitimately be represented by six or more distinct strings, none of which is wrong. Phonetic matching exists to bridge this and does so imperfectly, and the residue is a large volume of alerts on names that sound alike because they are alike — belonging to different people. 5.3.2  Arabic naming conventions Arabic names carry structural features that Western-designed matching handles poorly, and counter-terrorism lists are dense with them. The name is frequently a chain rather than a forename and surname: a given name, then bin or ibn meaning son of, then the father's name, then the grandfather's, sometimes continuing. Abd al- constructions — Abdullah, Abdulrahman, Abd al-Aziz — are compound and may be written as one word, two words or hyphenated. The definite article al- appears attached, separated, capitalised or omitted. A kunya — Abu followed by a name, meaning father of — functions as a form of address and appears extensively as an alias on terrorism lists. A nisba indicates origin, tribe or place: al-Masri meaning the Egyptian, al-Baghdadi meaning from Baghdad. These are not surnames and are shared by very large numbers of unrelated people. Two practical points follow. A match on al-Masri or al-Baghdadi alone is close to meaningless, because it is a description rather than an identifier. And the number of tokens in the payment name and the list entry may differ substantially without indicating a different person, because how much of the chain is recorded varies by document and by institution. 5.3.3  Other conventions worth knowing Russian and other Slavic names carry patronymics — Ivanovich, Petrovna — which sit between given name and surname and are often omitted in international payments, changing the token 87 count. Surnames also inflect by gender: Ivanov and Ivanova are the same family name for a man and a woman, and an engine treating them as different strings will miss the connection. Chinese names place the family name first, which reverses when rendered in Western order, and the same character may transliterate differently by system and by dialect. A short two-syllable name offers very little for fuzzy matching to work with, so scores are volatile. Spanish and Portuguese naming uses two surnames — paternal then maternal in Spanish, often the reverse in Portuguese. International systems frequently truncate to one, and which one survives varies. Particles such as de, del, da and dos may be included, dropped or attached. Corporate names have their own set: legal form suffixes that may be present, absent or abbreviated; ampersands against the word and; the words holding, group, international and trading appearing in enormous numbers of unrelated entities; and transliterated corporate names carrying every problem individual names do. 5.3.4  What an analyst should notice The point of knowing all this is not to second-guess the engine. It is to read an alert correctly. Where the matched token is a nisba, a common patronymic, or a corporate filler word, the score overstates the evidence. Three tokens matching where two of them are al- and Mohammed is much weaker than the number suggests. Where the token count differs, ask whether the convention explains it before treating it as a discrepancy. A payment name with three tokens against a list entry with six may be the same person with a shorter chain recorded. Where a surname inflects by gender, or a family name and given name may have been reversed, a low score may understate the evidence — and low scores are not reviewed, which is why below-the-line testing exists. None of this is a licence to overrule the engine on instinct. It is the reasoning that has to appear in a rationale when you discount or escalate, and a rationale that engages with why the name matched is a materially better record than one that does not.
