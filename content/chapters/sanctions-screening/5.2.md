# How matching works

A matching engine decides whether two strings refer to the same party. This chapter covers the techniques it uses, what each is good at, and why the output is a probability rather than an answer.


**5.2.1** Exact matching and why it is not enough The simplest approach compares strings character by character and reports a match only where they are identical. It is fast, produces no false positives on the string itself, and misses almost everything. Real payment data is not clean. Names arrive abbreviated, truncated by field length, with initials instead of forenames, with titles included, in different orders, with and without middle names, in upper case or mixed, with diacritics stripped, and with genuine typographical errors introduced by whoever keyed them. An engine relying on exact matching would miss MOHAMED where the list says MOHAMMED. Since a single character defeats it, and single-character variation is the normal condition of transliterated names, exact matching alone is not a viable control. It retains one use. For identifiers that are genuinely exact — passport numbers, IMO numbers, cryptocurrency addresses, registration numbers — exact matching is the correct technique, and applying fuzzy logic to them produces noise for no benefit.


**5.2.2** Fuzzy matching Fuzzy matching measures how different two strings are and treats sufficiently similar strings as candidate matches. The usual foundation is edit distance, most commonly Levenshtein distance: the minimum number of single-character insertions, deletions or substitutions needed to turn one string into the other. MOHAMED to MOHAMMED is one insertion, so a distance of one. HASSAN to HASAN is one deletion. Damerau-Levenshtein adds transposition — the swapping of two adjacent characters — as a single operation, which matters because transposition is one of the most common keying errors. Raw distance is not directly usable because its significance depends on length. A distance of two in a four-character name is enormous; the same distance in a twenty-character name is trivial. Engines therefore normalise distance against length to produce a similarity score, usually expressed as a percentage. Other measures appear alongside it. Jaro-Winkler weights agreement at the start of a string more heavily, which suits names because forenames and surnames tend to differ at their ends rather than their beginnings. N-gram comparison breaks both strings into overlapping fragments and measures overlap, which handles insertions and reorderings robustly.


**5.2.3** Phonetic matching Phonetic algorithms reduce a name to a code representing how it sounds, so that differently spelled names that sound alike collide. Soundex is the oldest and crudest: it keeps the first letter and encodes the rest as digits by consonant group, discarding vowels. Metaphone and Double Metaphone are substantially better, 83 handling more spelling-to-sound rules and producing a primary and alternate code for names that could be pronounced two ways. NYSIIS and Cologne phonetic serve particular language families. Phonetic matching is powerful for transliterated names, which is why it appears in every sanctions engine. It is also a considerable source of false positives, because reducing a name to its sound discards information, and different names frequently sound similar. The critical limitation is language dependence. These algorithms were built around English or German pronunciation rules. Applied to names transliterated from Arabic, Chinese or Russian, they behave less predictably, sometimes collapsing genuinely distinct names into one code and sometimes failing to collapse variants of the same name. An engine using phonetic matching alone on a counter-terrorism list will produce results that surprise you in both directions.


**5.2.4** Tokens, order and scoring Names are not single strings but sequences of parts, and engines usually break them into tokens and compare token to token. This is what allows an engine to match ALI HASSAN MOHAMED against MOHAMED ALI HASSAN. All three tokens are present; only the order differs, and in many naming conventions order is not fixed. Token-based comparison also handles missing middle names, since a two-token payment name can still match three of three tokens partially. It creates its own problem. If order is disregarded entirely, JOHN THOMAS matches THOMAS JOHN, and those may be different people. Engines therefore usually apply a penalty for order mismatch rather than ignoring it, and the size of that penalty is a configuration choice with real consequences. The final score is a weighted combination: token coverage, per-token similarity from the fuzzy measures, phonetic agreement, order penalty, and frequently a weighting that treats rare tokens as more significant than common ones. A match on an unusual surname is stronger evidence than a match on a name shared by millions. The number that reaches you is the output of all of that. It is not a probability that the parties are the same person, and it does not mean what its scale suggests — a score of 92 does not mean ninety-two per cent likely. ## What to take from this chapter
- Exact matching misses almost everything, because single-character variation is the normal condition of transliterated names
- It remains correct for genuinely exact identifiers — passport, IMO, wallet address — where fuzzy logic adds only noise
- Levenshtein counts insertions, deletions and substitutions; Damerau adds transposition, the most common keying error
- Raw distance must be normalised against length: two characters in a four-letter name is enormous, in a twenty-letter name trivial
- Phonetic algorithms were built around English and German pronunciation and behave unpredictably on Arabic, Chinese and Russian names
- Token comparison handles reordering, but ignoring order entirely matches JOHN THOMAS to THOMAS JOHN
- A score of 92 does not mean ninety-two per cent likely. It is a weighted similarity on the engine's own scale.
