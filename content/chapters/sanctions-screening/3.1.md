# Asset freezes and the making-available prohibition

The asset freeze is the core financial sanction and the one you will apply most. It has two limbs, and the second catches more transactions than the first. Most institutions understand freezing; the making-available limb is where the errors are.


**3.1.1** What freezing actually means An asset freeze requires that funds and economic resources belonging to, owned, held or controlled by a designated person are frozen. Frozen means immobilised: no movement, transfer, alteration, use, access or dealing that would change volume, amount, location, ownership, possession, character or destination. Note what freezing is not. It is not confiscation — title remains with the designated person and the institution is a custodian, not an owner. It is not closure of the account. And critically it is not return of the funds to the sender. That last point causes more failures than any other. An institution receives an inbound payment for a designated beneficiary, recognises the problem, and returns the money to the originating bank. It feels like the safe and neutral act. It is a breach: the funds were within reach and should have been immobilised, and returning them made funds available to whoever sent them, who may themselves be restricted. It also destroys the evidence trail and denies the authority the ability to make a decision about the money. Economic resources are assets of every kind other than funds — property, vehicles, vessels, artwork, commodities, intellectual property — which may be used to obtain funds, goods or services. The scope is deliberately wide, and it is why a designated person's warehouse stock is caught as surely as their bank balance.


**3.1.2** The making-available prohibition The second limb prohibits making funds or economic resources available, directly or indirectly, to or for the benefit of a designated person. This is broader than the freeze and it operates independently of it. You can breach the making-available prohibition without ever touching a designated person's account, because the test is not whose account the money sits in but who benefits. Directly is straightforward: paying a designated person. Indirectly is where the work is. Paying a non-designated intermediary who will pass funds on. Paying a supplier for goods that will be 43 delivered to a designated person. Settling a designated person's debt to a third party. Providing services — legal, accounting, insurance, transport — that benefit them. The benefit limb reaches further still. A payment to a designated person's spouse for household expenses is for their benefit. A payment to a company they do not own but which exists to serve their interests may be. The analysis is factual rather than formal, which is precisely why it cannot be automated and why an analyst is needed. One common misreading is worth naming. Making available does not require that the designated person obtains legal title. Giving them the use of an asset — letting them occupy a property, drive a vehicle, draw on a facility — is making an economic resource available even if ownership never moves.


**3.1.3** Ownership, control and the extension of the freeze An asset freeze on a person extends to entities they own or control. This is the point at which a two-thousand-name list becomes an unbounded screening problem, because the entities are not themselves listed. The tests differ by jurisdiction and Module 4 covers them in detail. In outline: the EU and UK apply a fifty per cent ownership threshold together with a separate control test; the United States applies the Fifty Percent Rule, under which an entity owned fifty per cent or more, directly or indirectly, individually or in the aggregate, by one or more blocked persons is itself blocked automatically and without being listed. The word automatically is the operative one and it is frequently missed. An OFAC-blocked subsidiary is blocked by operation of the rule the moment the ownership threshold is met. It does not appear on the SDN List, OFAC does not announce it, and there is no publication event to trigger a list update. An institution screening only against published lists will not see it at all. This is why customer due diligence and sanctions screening cannot be separate disciplines. The screening system sees the name in front of it; only the ownership record tells you whether that name is caught.


**3.1.4** Reporting and holding Freezing is not the end of the obligation. Every major regime requires the institution to report frozen assets and, usually, attempted transactions. In the United States, blocked property must be reported to OFAC within ten business days of blocking, and an annual report of all blocked property held as of 30 June is due by 30 September. Rejected transactions — those the institution declined rather than blocked, because the property never came within its possession — must also be reported within ten business days. In the United Kingdom, relevant firms must report to OFSI as soon as practicable, and must report frozen assets held. The reporting obligation is a criminal offence to breach in its own right, independent of any underlying dealing. In the EU, reporting obligations sit in each regulation and are discharged to the national competent authority of the member state concerned. The distinction between blocking and rejecting matters for reporting as well as for the money. Blocking means the funds are in your hands and must be held. Rejecting means the transaction 44 was refused before the funds came into your possession — for example an instruction you declined to execute. Both are reportable in the US; they are not the same event and recording one as the other misstates what happened.


## Scenario — THE PAYMENT YOU RETURNED

An inbound wire of $240,000 arrives for a corporate customer. Screening identifies the ordering customer as an SDN. The payment has been credited to a suspense account pending investigation. The operations team, wanting to resolve it quickly and avoid customer complaint, instructs the funds returned to the sending correspondent with the message 'unable to process'. **Question.** What has the institution done? **Answer.** Breached the blocking requirement, and probably compounded it. The funds came within the institution's possession, which means they were required to be blocked: placed in a blocked account, reported to OFAC within ten business days, and held until licensed.

Returning them moved property in which a designated person had an interest, which is a prohibited dealing, and made funds available to the sending institution and ultimately back to the SDN. The message 'unable to process' also fails to tell the correspondent what happened, which in a chain of banks means the next institution may repeat the attempt. The correct handling was to block, report and notify internally — and the customer complaint, which was the thing the team was trying to avoid, is not a consideration that bears on the obligation at all.


## What to take from this chapter
- Freezing immobilises. It is not confiscation, not account closure, and emphatically not returning the funds to the sender
- Returning a blocked payment is itself a prohibited dealing and destroys the evidence trail
- The making-available limb is broader than the freeze and is breached without touching a designated person's account
- Benefit is a factual test — a spouse's household expenses, a settled debt, a service provided. It cannot be automated
- Giving use of an asset is making it available even where title never moves
- US Fifty Percent Rule subsidiaries are blocked automatically and never appear on the SDN List. No list update will reveal them
- Blocked property is reported within ten business days in the US; rejected transactions are reported separately and are not the same event.
