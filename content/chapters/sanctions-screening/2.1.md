The United Nations UN measures are the only sanctions with genuinely global legal force. They are also the shortest lists you will screen against, and understanding why they are short tells you a great deal about what national regimes are for. 2.1.1  Chapter VII and the legal basis United Nations sanctions are adopted by the Security Council under Chapter VII of the UN Charter, which permits the Council to decide what measures are to be employed where it has determined that a threat to international peace and security exists. Every UN regime rests on that determination. The legal significance is that Chapter VII decisions bind all 193 member states. Article 25 of the Charter obliges members to accept and carry out the Council's decisions, and Article 103 provides that Charter obligations prevail over conflicting obligations under any other international agreement. No other sanctioning body has that reach. In practice the obligation is discharged by domestic implementation. A UN designation does not itself freeze an asset in London or Singapore; it obliges the United Kingdom and Singapore to make law that does. That implementation step is why a UN designation appears simultaneously on the OFAC SDN List, the EU consolidated list, the UK Sanctions List and every other national list — and why the implementation dates differ by days or occasionally weeks between jurisdictions. 2.1.2  Sanctions committees and how a listing happens The Council does not administer regimes directly. Each is run by a sanctions committee composed of all fifteen Council members, which maintains the list, considers exemption requests and handles delisting. Committees are named for the resolution that created them: the 1267 Committee for ISIL and Al-Qaida, the 1718 Committee for North Korea, the 1591 Committee for Sudan. A designation begins with a proposal from a member state, supported by a statement of case setting out the grounds. The proposal circulates to committee members, who have a defined window — usually five working days — to object or place a hold. If nobody objects, the designation is adopted. Most committees are supported by a panel or group of experts: a small standing team that investigates violations, tracks evasion networks and reports publicly to the Council, usually annually. These reports are among the most valuable open-source material available to a 25 sanctions analyst. The North Korea Panel of Experts reports in particular document ship-to-ship transfer techniques, front company structures and financial evasion methods in operational detail you will not find in commercial data. 2.1.3  Why consensus makes UN lists short Adoption requires that no committee member objects, and every one of the fifteen can block. The five permanent members can block anything, and regularly do where a proposed designation touches an ally, a client state or a commercial interest. The consequence is that UN lists are dramatically shorter than national ones and lag behind geopolitical events. There is, for example, no UN sanctions regime concerning Russia's actions in Ukraine, and there cannot be one while Russia holds a veto. The measures you screen against for that conflict are entirely national and regional: US, EU, UK, Canadian, Australian, Japanese, Swiss. This produces the single most important operational point about UN measures. The UN Consolidated List is a floor, not a ceiling. Every jurisdiction implements it, so anything on it will appear on the national lists you screen. But the great majority of the names you screen exist only in national regimes, and an institution screening solely against the UN list would miss most of its exposure while believing itself compliant. 2.1.4  Delisting and the Ombudsperson Being designated by the Security Council was for many years close to unchallengeable. A listed individual had no forum in which to contest the evidence, because the Council is a political body and its committees deliberate confidentially. That position was challenged successfully in the courts. The Kadi litigation, decided by the European Court of Justice in 2008 and again in 2013, concerned a Saudi businessman listed under the 1267 regime. The Court held that EU measures implementing a UN designation had to respect fundamental rights, including the right to be heard and to effective judicial review, and annulled the EU regulation as it applied to him. The judgment did not strike down the UN listing — the Court had no power to do that — but it made the EU's implementation of it legally vulnerable, which was politically almost as significant. The Council's response was Resolution 1904 in 2009, establishing an Office of the Ombudsperson to receive delisting petitions, gather information and make a recommendation to the committee. The mechanism has real teeth: a recommendation to delist takes effect unless the committee decides by consensus to maintain the listing, which reverses the usual burden. Two limitations matter. The Ombudsperson's mandate covers only the ISIL and Al-Qaida regime; petitioners under other UN regimes go to a Focal Point with far weaker powers. And no equivalent mechanism exists for most national designations, which are instead challenged in domestic courts. 26 SCENARIO — SCREENING THE UN LIST AND NOTHING ELSE A small payments institution licensed in a jurisdiction with limited autonomous sanctions decides that, since UN measures are binding on all states and national measures are not binding on it, screening the UN Consolidated List is sufficient. It processes euro and dollar payments for corporate customers. Question.  Is that position defensible? Answer.  No, for two separate reasons. The first is legal: the institution's own jurisdiction will have implemented UN measures through domestic law, and that domestic law is what binds it — but it will almost certainly also carry national provisions the institution has not considered. The second is the one that ends the business. Its dollar payments clear through a US correspondent, which brings the transaction within US jurisdiction at that point, and its euro payments touch EU institutions. Neither list is optional in practice. An institution screening only the UN list would have missed every Russia-related designation since 2014, because no UN Russia regime exists or can exist while Russia holds a veto.
