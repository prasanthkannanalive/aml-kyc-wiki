Writing the rationale The rationale is the only part of your work that survives. In two years the alert will be a row in a sample and the rationale will be all there is. This chapter covers what it is for, what it must contain, and the language that makes reviewers uneasy. 6.5.1  What a rationale is for Three audiences, and they want different things. The reviewer, next week, checking a sample of closures. They want to see that a method was followed and that the conclusion follows from the facts recorded. The next analyst, next year, working a new alert on the same party. They want to know what was established, from what source, and what was left open — so the work is not repeated and, more importantly, so a gap is not mistaken for a clearance. The regulator or auditor, in three years, examining a period. They are testing whether the institution's controls operated. A rationale that asserts conclusions without recording how they were reached fails that test even where the conclusion was right. Module 2 noted that OFSI applies strict civil liability for penalties — a process failure can be penalised without knowledge and without any designated party being paid. The written record is the evidence that the process did not fail. This is the concrete reason rationales matter more in sanctions than in most compliance work. 6.5.2  The elements A complete rationale contains six things and can be written in four or five sentences. What matched and against what: the list, the entry, the field, primary name or alias. What the applicable restriction is, where it is not simply blocking. The identifiers compared, with values on both sides. The source and date of your own side's data. The conclusion, stated as what was found rather than as what was not. What remains unassessed, where anything does — the ownership point from Chapter 6.3 being the usual case. Anything shorter than this is likely to be missing an element rather than efficiently written. Anything much longer is usually padding a weak conclusion, and Chapter 6.2's scenario shows how fluent that padding can be. 6.5.3  Language that makes reviewers uneasy Certain formulations recur in weak files often enough to function as signals. 'No adverse information found.' This describes a search, not a finding, and is the standard phrasing of a null discount. 'Customer is well known to the bank.' A statement about the relationship, offered in place of a statement about identity. 107 'Appears to be a false positive.' Appears to whom, on what basis. The hedge concedes that the conclusion is not supported. 'Payment is consistent with expected activity.' True and irrelevant — this is AML reasoning applied to an identity question. 'Discounted in line with previous alerts.' Unless the previous alert is identified and its basis restated, this is deferring to a decision nobody is now examining. 'Low risk.' Sanctions prohibitions are not risk-weighted. There is no threshold below which a designated party may be paid. None of these is forbidden phrasing, and each can appear in a good rationale alongside actual findings. What makes them signals is when they appear instead of findings. 6.5.4  Two rationales, same alert Take the alert in Figure 5.1: a 92 score against an SDN entry, no secondary identifiers in the payment, a long-standing corporate customer. A poor rationale: 'Name match to SDN list. Customer is a long-standing importer with regular payments to this region. Beneficiary address in Dubai does not match list address in Sudan. No adverse information found. Discounted as false positive.' Everything in it is true. It contains no identifier, uses address as though it were strong, offers relationship length as evidence, and closes with the standard phrasing of an unresolved alert. It would pass a queue review and fail a sample test. A better rationale: 'Score 92 against SDN entry 24817 (SDGT), matched on primary name in field :50K. Entry carries DOB 14 Jun 1971, POB Port Sudan, Sudanese nationality and passport P0428817. Payment carries name and address only — no DOB, nationality or document number. Address differs (Dubai / Khartoum) but is not relied on. RFI issued to correspondent 14 Aug requesting beneficiary DOB and identification. Escalated pending response: strong name match with no identifier available on either side to distinguish.' The second reaches no conclusion, which is the point. It is a complete, checkable record of an alert that could not be decided on the information available, and it is a better piece of work than a confident discount would have been.
