The oil price cap The price cap introduced in December 2022 works by permission rather than prohibition, runs on attestations, and has produced an entire parallel shipping market. It is the newest maritime measure and the one most likely to appear in a trade file. 7.5.1  How the cap works The G7, the EU, Australia and others prohibited the provision of maritime services — shipping, insurance, reinsurance, brokering, flagging and financing — in connection with the seaborne transport of Russian-origin crude oil and refined products, unless the cargo was purchased at or below a stated price. The design is deliberate and unusual. A total ban on the trade would have removed a large volume of oil from the market and driven prices sharply higher, harming importing economies more than the exporter. The cap instead keeps the oil flowing while capping the revenue reaching the seller, using the West's dominance of shipping, insurance and finance as the lever. Separate caps apply to crude and to refined products, with different levels for products trading at a premium and a discount to crude, and the levels are subject to periodic review. The measure is therefore a permission, not a prohibition. Below the cap, the services may lawfully be provided. Above it, they may not. Establishing which side of the line a cargo falls on is the entire compliance question. 7.5.2  Attestations Nobody in the shipping chain except the buyer and seller sees the price. The compliance mechanism therefore runs on attestations passed along the chain. The model is tiered by access to price information. Parties with direct access to the price — traders and commodity brokers — provide attestations based on what they know. Parties without it — shipowners, insurers, flag registries, banks — receive and rely on attestations from those who do, and record them. The reliance is what makes the model workable and what makes it vulnerable. A service provider acting in good faith on an attestation from a party with price visibility is generally within the safe harbour; one who ignores information contradicting the attestation is not. The practical questions for a bank are three. Does an attestation exist for this cargo. Is it from a party in the appropriate tier. And is there anything else in the file inconsistent with it — a freight rate far above market, an unusually complex intermediary chain, or a counterparty whose commercial rationale is unclear. That last question is the one that matters. The safe harbour protects good faith reliance; it does not protect reliance in the face of contrary evidence sitting in the same file. 7.5.3  The shadow fleet The predictable response to a measure operating through Western services was the assembly of a shipping capability that uses none of them. 122 Several hundred ageing tankers have been acquired, reflagged to registries with limited oversight, insured outside the International Group by providers whose capacity to meet a major pollution claim is untested, classed outside IACS or unclassed, and operated through opaque single-ship companies. The estimates of fleet size vary by source and by definition, and all of them are large. Two consequences follow. The first is a genuine safety and environmental exposure: old tankers, uncertain maintenance, unverified insurance and crews of unknown competence, operating in congested waters including the Baltic and the Danish straits. Several incidents have already occurred. The second is a screening consequence. A vessel in this fleet has, by construction, no relationship with any Western service provider — which means the withdrawal signals from Chapter 7.4 are absent not because nothing was withdrawn but because nothing was ever provided. An unclassed vessel with non-IG insurance and a small-registry flag is not showing the pattern of a ship that lost its cover; it is showing the pattern of one built to operate without it. SCENARIO — THE ATTESTATION AND THE FREIGHT RATE A trade finance file concerns a cargo of refined product. An attestation is present, provided by the trader, confirming purchase at or below the applicable cap. The vessel is twenty-one years old, flagged to a small registry, and insured by a provider outside the International Group. The freight rate is roughly three times the market rate for the route. The intermediary chain runs through two trading companies incorporated in the same month, both in the same jurisdiction. Question.  The attestation exists and is from the right tier. Is that the end of it? Answer.  No, and the freight rate is the reason. The safe harbour protects good faith reliance on an attestation; it does not protect reliance where other information in the same file contradicts it. A freight rate at three times market is the recognised mechanism for moving value outside the capped oil price — the cargo is nominally sold at or below the cap and the seller is compensated through inflated shipping costs, so the attestation can be literally true and the measure entirely defeated. Combined with an over-age vessel, non-IG insurance, a small-registry flag and two intermediaries incorporated in the same month, the file describes the shadow fleet pattern in full. The correct disposition is escalation setting out each element, with the freight rate stated as the specific inconsistency. What is not defensible is recording that a valid attestation was held and treating that as the whole analysis, because the file shows the analyst had the contradicting information in front of them.
