# The European Union and the United Kingdom

Two regimes that were one until 2020 and have diverged since. Screening the EU list alone now leaves UK exposure uncovered, and the differences are not only in who is designated but in how ownership is tested and what must be reported.


**2.5.1** How EU measures are made EU sanctions follow a two-stage process, and the split explains several features that otherwise look inconsistent. The Council first adopts a decision under the Common Foreign and Security Policy. This requires unanimity among all member states and sets the policy. Where the measures fall within EU competence — asset freezes, trade restrictions, financial services — a Council regulation is then adopted, usually by qualified majority on a joint proposal. It is the regulation that has direct legal effect on persons and businesses across the Union, without any national implementing legislation being needed. The unanimity requirement at the first stage is the defining constraint. Any single member state can block a designation or the renewal of a regime, and renewals are periodic — most regimes require renewal every six or twelve months — which creates recurring leverage for any state that wishes to use it. Hungary's position on successive Russia packages is the most visible recent example. It also shapes the rhythm. EU measures tend to arrive in large negotiated packages rather than incrementally, which means an institution faces a substantial list update at intervals rather than a steady trickle.


**2.5.2** Uniform law, national enforcement This is the feature most often misunderstood, and it produces real operational divergence. The regulation applies uniformly across the Union and does not need transposition. But investigation, prosecution, penalties and licensing are matters for each member state's own competent authority. There are twenty-seven of them, with different resources, different penalty regimes and different licensing practices. The consequence is that the same conduct may be treated quite differently depending on where it is examined. Penalty levels have historically ranged from administrative fines in some states to criminal liability in others. Licensing decisions on materially similar facts have gone different ways in different capitals, which is a genuine problem for a group operating across several member states. The Union has moved to address this. A 2024 directive on the definition of criminal offences and penalties for the violation of restrictive measures sets minimum standards, requiring member states to criminalise specified conduct and to provide penalties of a defined severity. Convergence will take time and divergence remains a live consideration. Scope of application is broader than many assume. EU measures apply within EU territory, to EU nationals wherever located, to entities incorporated under member state law including their foreign branches, on board EU aircraft and vessels, and to any business done in whole or in part 37 within the Union. That last limb catches a good deal of activity with no other European connection.


**2.5.3** The United Kingdom after Brexit Until the end of the transition period the UK implemented EU sanctions. It now operates an autonomous regime and the two have diverged materially — in designations, in the ownership test, and in enforcement approach. The framework is the Sanctions and Anti-Money Laundering Act 2018, which empowers ministers to make sanctions regulations for stated purposes and establishes the architecture for designation, review and challenge. Individual regimes are made as statutory instruments under that Act. Three bodies matter. The Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office makes designation decisions and maintains the UK Sanctions List. The Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation, part of HM Treasury, administers financial sanctions: it issues licences, receives reports, investigates and imposes monetary penalties. Trade sanctions are administered by the Department for Business and Trade, with enforcement of trade offences sitting with HM Revenue and Customs. Designations diverge in both directions. The UK has designated parties the EU has not, and the EU has designated parties the UK has not. An institution operating in both must screen both lists; neither is a superset.


**2.5.4** Reporting and strict liability Two features of the UK regime deserve particular attention because they change what an institution has to do rather than only whom it must avoid. The first is the reporting obligation. A relevant firm must inform OFSI as soon as practicable if it knows or has reasonable cause to suspect that a person is a designated person, or has committed an offence under the regulations, where that knowledge or suspicion came to it in the course of its business. It must also report frozen assets it holds. Failure to report is itself a criminal offence, distinct from any underlying breach. The threshold — reasonable cause to suspect — is lower than certainty and lower than the balance of probabilities. An analyst who forms a genuine suspicion and does not escalate has created a reporting failure regardless of whether the underlying party turns out to be designated. The second is the standard for monetary penalties. Since 2022 OFSI has operated a strict civil liability standard: it may impose a penalty without needing to establish that the firm knew or had reasonable cause to suspect that its conduct breached the prohibition. Knowledge is relevant to the amount, not to liability. The combined effect is that a process failure — a screening gap, a missed list update, an alert closed without proper analysis — can attract a penalty even where nobody intended anything and no designated party was actually paid. This is why UK institutions place unusual weight on demonstrable controls and on the quality of the written record, and it is the clearest single reason your rationales matter.


## Scenario — DESIGNATED IN LONDON, CLEAN IN BRUSSELS

A group operates banks in Frankfurt and London. A corporate customer of both instructs a payment to a counterparty that appears on the UK Sanctions List but not on the EU consolidated list. The Frankfurt entity finds no hit. The London entity alerts. **Question.** How should each entity proceed, and what does the group need to consider? **Answer.** The London entity must treat the counterparty as designated: freeze any funds within reach, decline the payment, and report to OFSI.

The Frankfurt entity, screening a payment with no UK nexus, faces no EU prohibition arising from a UK designation and may proceed on that basis — but should first establish that the transaction genuinely has no UK nexus, since a UK branch, a UK-incorporated group entity in the chain or sterling clearing would change the answer. The group-level consideration is the one that causes most difficulty: if the Frankfurt entity processes what London has just frozen, the group has facilitated the very transaction one of its regulated entities blocked. Most groups handle this with a group-wide screening standard applying the union of the lists, which is a policy decision rather than a legal requirement — and one worth recognising as such when a customer asks why a lawful payment was declined.


## What to take from this chapter
- EU measures need a unanimous CFSP decision, then a regulation with direct effect. Any member state can block a designation or a renewal
- Regimes require periodic renewal, which gives recurring leverage to any state wishing to use it
- The regulation is uniform; enforcement is national across twenty-seven authorities, producing real divergence in penalties and licensing
- EU scope covers EU territory, EU nationals anywhere, EU-incorporated entities and any business done partly in the Union
- The UK operates autonomously under SAMLA 2018. FCDO designates, OFSI administers financial sanctions, DBT handles trade
- UK reporting is mandatory at the threshold of reasonable cause to suspect, and failure to report is a separate offence
- OFSI applies strict civil liability for penalties. A process failure can be penalised with no knowledge and no designated party paid.
