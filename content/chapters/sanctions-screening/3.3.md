Sectoral and financial measures Sectoral measures restrict specified activities with specified entities, leaving everything else lawful. They are the hardest category to apply correctly because the answer depends on the nature of the transaction rather than the identity of the party, and a name match alone cannot resolve them. 3.3.1  The sectoral principle A sectoral measure identifies entities operating in a defined sector of a target economy and prohibits particular dealings with them. The party is not blocked; a specified class of transaction is. The design objective is to impose cost on a strategically important sector — typically finance, energy or defence — without freezing an economy or cutting off the population. It also allows escalation: the maturity limit on new debt can be tightened, a further sector added, or the restriction extended from debt to equity, each a discrete increment. For a screening function this creates a category of alert that cannot be dispositioned from the alert alone. An SDN hit is answerable from the identity of the party. A sectoral hit requires knowing what the transaction is — its instrument, its tenor, its purpose — and that information frequently sits outside the payment message. 3.3.2  Debt and equity restrictions The most common sectoral form restricts dealing in new debt or new equity of designated entities beyond a stated maturity. Three words carry the weight. New means originated after the effective date; pre-existing instruments are generally grandfathered, which is why the effective date of the directive matters as much as the designation date. Debt is construed broadly and reaches beyond bonds and loans to include deposits, drawn and undrawn facilities, extended payment terms on trade, and in some readings any extension of credit. And the maturity limit — fourteen days, thirty days, sixty days depending on the directive — is measured from origination. The breadth of debt is the practical trap. A supplier offering a designated energy company ninety-day payment terms has extended credit beyond a sixty-day limit. Nobody involved thinks of it as debt. A bank confirming a letter of credit with deferred payment beyond the limit is in the same position. Equity restrictions are narrower in scope but absolute in effect: transacting in new equity of a designated entity is prohibited regardless of size. Secondary market trading in existing equity may or may not be caught depending on the measure, which is a question for the specific directive rather than a general rule. 3.3.3  Service and technology restrictions The second sectoral form prohibits the provision of specified goods, services or technology in support of defined activities. The Russian energy measures are the developed example. Directive 4 in the US framework, and its EU and UK analogues, prohibit providing goods, services and technology in support of deepwater, Arctic offshore and shale projects that have the potential to produce oil. The 49 restriction is defined by the project type, not by the entity alone — which means the same designated company may be dealt with lawfully in relation to a conventional onshore field and unlawfully in relation to an Arctic offshore one. Professional and business services restrictions have expanded considerably since 2022. The EU and UK now prohibit providing accounting, auditing, management consulting, public relations, architectural, engineering, legal advisory, IT consultancy and market research services to persons in Russia, subject to exceptions. These bite on professional firms and on banks providing advisory services rather than on payments. Insurance and reinsurance restrictions operate similarly and matter disproportionately in shipping, where the withdrawal of protection and indemnity cover is often the effective restriction rather than any prohibition on the voyage itself. 3.3.4  Price caps The oil price cap introduced by the G7 and allies in December 2022 is a distinct mechanism and worth understanding because it works by permission rather than prohibition. The design is unusual. Rather than banning the trade in Russian seaborne crude and refined products outright, it prohibits the provision of services — shipping, insurance, brokering, financing — where the cargo is sold above a stated price. Below the cap, those services may lawfully be provided. The intent is to keep oil flowing to global markets, avoiding a price shock, while capping the revenue reaching the exporter. Compliance runs on attestations. Parties in the chain provide declarations, tiered according to how much price information they have access to, confirming the cargo was purchased at or below the cap. A service provider without direct sight of the price relies on the attestation from the party that does. That creates the obvious vulnerability, and it has been exploited: false attestations, opaque intermediaries, and the growth of a shadow fleet operating outside Western insurance and service markets entirely. Chapter 7.13 deals with the maritime dimension. For a financing or payment function, the operational question is whether an attestation exists, whether it is from a party with the requisite price visibility, and whether anything else in the file contradicts it. The supplier's standard commercial terms are payment ninety days after delivery. The supplier's bank is asked to discount the resulting receivable. Nobody in the chain has treated the arrangement as a financing. Question.  Is there a sectoral issue? Answer.  Yes, and it exists before the bank is involved. Extended payment terms are an extension of credit and are treated as debt under these measures. Ninety-day terms to an entity subject to a sixty-day limit create new debt beyond the permitted maturity, which is prohibited for persons subject to the measure. The discounting compounds it rather than causing it. What makes this scenario worth studying is that everyone involved would describe the arrangement as ordinary commercial terms — no loan agreement exists, no interest is charged, and no one considers themselves a lender. The analyst's contribution is recognising that the legal test is the extension of credit and its tenor, not what the parties call it. The correct disposition escalates, and the correct commercial answer is to restructure to terms within the limit. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — Sectoral measures restrict transaction types, not parties. The identity of the counterparty does not answer the question. — New means originated after the effective date, so the directive date matters as much as the designation date. — Debt is construed broadly: deposits, facilities, extended payment terms and deferred letters of credit are all capable of being caught. — Extended commercial payment terms are the most commonly missed form of prohibited new debt. — Service restrictions attach to project types, so the same entity may be lawful on one project and prohibited on another. — Professional services restrictions bite on advisory work rather than payments. — The price cap permits services below the cap and prohibits them above it.
