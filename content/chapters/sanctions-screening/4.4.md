# The United Kingdom test

The UK inherited the EU framework and has since developed a distinct body of law around it, largely through litigation. This chapter covers the statutory test, the case that unsettled it, the legislative response, and what the sequence tells you about relying on any control test.


**4.4.1** The statutory test UK sanctions regulations made under SAMLA 2018 treat a person as owning or controlling an entity where any of several conditions is met, and the drafting is consistent across regimes. The ownership condition is that the person holds, directly or indirectly, more than 50 per cent of the shares or voting rights in the entity, or holds the right to appoint or remove a majority of the board. The control condition — and this is the one that matters — is that it is reasonable, having regard to all the circumstances, to expect that the person would be able, in most cases or in significant respects, by whatever means and whether directly or indirectly, to achieve the result that the affairs of the entity are conducted in accordance with their wishes. Read that condition carefully, because its breadth is the subject of everything that follows. It contains no percentage, no instrument, and no requirement of any formal relationship. It asks a question about practical capability: could this person, by whatever means, get the entity to do what they want.


**4.4.2** Mints and what it exposed Mints and others v PJSC National Bank Trust and another reached the Court of Appeal in 2023. The underlying dispute was commercial — proceedings brought by Russian banks against former shareholders — and the sanctions question arose collaterally: whether the claimant banks were controlled by designated individuals, including the President of the Russian Federation, in a way that engaged the regulations. The Court's observations on the control test were obiter, meaning they were not necessary to the decision, and they were nonetheless taken extremely seriously by practitioners. The reasoning ran that on a natural reading of the condition, a designated senior political figure might be said to be able to achieve the result that the affairs of a very large number of entities in that state were conducted in accordance with their wishes. The implication, if correct, was extraordinary. It would mean that every entity within the effective reach of a designated head of state or senior minister was caught by the control test, and therefore that dealing with any of them was prohibited. Applied literally, it would have captured a substantial part of an entire national economy, including entities the UK government had deliberately chosen not to designate. The practical consequence for compliance functions in the weeks that followed was significant uncertainty about counterparties nobody had previously regarded as caught.


**4.4.3** The response The UK government issued a statement in the autumn of 2023 setting out its position: that it does not consider that a designated person is to be regarded as controlling an entity merely by virtue 69 of holding a political office, and that the government did not intend the control test to operate in that way. A ministerial statement is not law and cannot overrule a court's construction of a statutory instrument, so the position remained uncomfortable. Legislation followed, amending the regulations to disapply the ownership and control provisions in relation to entities where the only basis for the connection is a designated person's political office. The sequence is now settled in outcome. What it should teach is not the specific rule — which is narrow and may well change again — but the general lesson: a control test drafted in terms of practical capability is inherently uncertain at its edges, and its edges move. For an analyst, the working conclusion is that political office alone does not establish control, and that anything beyond office alone remains a live question requiring escalation rather than a settled answer.


**4.4.4** How the UK and EU tests differ They are close and they are not identical, and a group operating in both should know where they part. The UK ownership limb, like the EU's, uses more than 50 per cent. Both diverge from the US position at exactly fifty. The UK control condition is drafted as a single broad question about practical capability. The EU sets out a list of indicative criteria. In practice the EU criteria are more predictable to apply and the UK condition is broader in principle — which is precisely why Mints arose in the UK and not elsewhere. The UK regulations expressly include the right to appoint or remove a majority of the board within the ownership limb, where the EU treats board appointment as a control criterion. The classification differs; the outcome usually does not. Designations themselves diverge in both directions, as Module 2 noted. A party designated in London and not in Brussels is caught by the UK test and by no EU test at all, and the ownership analysis follows the designation.


## Scenario — OFFICE, AND SOMETHING MORE

A UK institution holds a relationship with a company in a third country. The company's majority shareholder is a businessman who has not been designated. A designated individual is a minister in that state's government, and the company's principal contracts are all with ministries within his portfolio. The company's chief executive is his former chief of staff. The company was awarded its principal contract without competitive tender four months after his appointment. **Question.** Does the Mints correction settle this? **Answer.** No, and the distinction is the point of the chapter. The legislative response disapplies control where the ONLY basis for the connection is the designated person's political office.

Here there is more: the chief executive is his former chief of staff, the contracts sit within his portfolio, and the principal award followed his appointment without tender. Those facts go to practical capability — whether it is reasonable to expect that he could, in significant respects, have the company's affairs conducted in accordance with his wishes. The correct disposition is escalation setting out each fact and stating explicitly that the connection does not rest on office alone, since that is the question the amendment turns on. What would be wrong is to release on the basis that Mints was corrected, which reads the amendment as broader than it is.


## What to take from this chapter
- The UK ownership limb is more than 50 per cent of shares or voting rights, or the right to appoint or remove a board majority
- The control condition asks whether the person could, by whatever means, achieve that the entity's affairs are conducted in accordance with their wishes
- Mints (2023) raised, obiter, that a senior political figure might on that reading control a large part of a national economy
- The government stated that office alone is not control, and legislation followed to disapply the provisions on that basis
- The lesson is general: a test drafted as practical capability is uncertain at its edges, and its edges move
- The amendment covers connections resting on office ALONE. Anything more remains live
- EU criteria are more predictable; the UK condition is broader in principle, which is why the litigation arose there.
