# The United States: the Fifty Percent Rule

The US test is arithmetic and it is stricter than it first appears. This chapter works through direct ownership, indirect ownership, aggregation across multiple designated persons, and the cases where the rule produces results that surprise people.


**4.2.1** The basic rule OFAC's rule is that any entity owned in the aggregate, directly or indirectly, fifty per cent or more by one or more blocked persons is itself considered blocked. Four elements deserve attention. Fifty per cent or more, so exactly fifty is caught. In the aggregate, so holdings by several designated persons are added together. Directly or indirectly, so ownership through intermediate entities counts. And blocked persons, which includes entities blocked by the rule itself — producing the cascading effect covered below. The rule concerns ownership, not control. An entity controlled but not owned by a designated person is not blocked under this rule. OFAC has issued guidance strongly cautioning against dealing with such entities, on the basis that the designated person may derive benefit and that OFAC may designate the entity in due course, but caution is not prohibition. This is a genuine divergence from the EU and UK positions and one of the few places where the US test is narrower. There is no de minimis and no good faith exception in the rule itself. An institution that did not know and could not reasonably have known is still dealing with blocked property; knowledge goes to penalty rather than to whether the prohibition applied.


**4.2.2** Indirect ownership and the cascade Indirect ownership is where the arithmetic becomes counter-intuitive, and the counter-intuitive result is the correct one. Suppose a designated person owns 50 per cent of Company A. Company A is blocked. Company A owns 50 per cent of Company B. Because Company A is itself a blocked person, its 50 per cent holding in Company B is a holding by a blocked person — so Company B is blocked too. Note what has happened. The designated individual's economic interest in Company B is 25 per cent, well below the threshold. Company B is nonetheless blocked in full, because the test at each level asks whether blocked persons hold fifty per cent or more, and Company A qualifies as a blocked person in its own right. The chain continues indefinitely. Company B owning 50 per cent of Company C blocks Company C, at which point the original individual's economic interest is 12.5 per cent. There is no point at which the cascade exhausts itself through dilution. This is why ownership analysis in sanctions cannot be done by calculating an ultimate beneficial ownership percentage. UBO analysis multiplies down the chain and asks what proportion the individual ultimately owns; the Fifty Percent Rule tests each level independently. The two methods give different answers and only one of them is the legal test.


**4.2.3** Aggregation Holdings by different blocked persons are added together, and this catches structures that no single designation would. Three designated individuals holding 20, 20 and 15 per cent of a company hold 55 per cent in aggregate. The company is blocked, although no one of them approaches the threshold and none may have any connection to the others. Aggregation applies across the ownership chain as well as at a single level. Blocked Company A holding 30 per cent and blocked individual X holding 25 per cent of the same entity aggregates to 55 per cent. For an analyst this means that identifying one designated shareholder and finding they hold less than half is not the end of the analysis. The question is what proportion is held by blocked persons in total, which requires the whole shareholder register rather than a check on the one name that alerted. It also means a company can become blocked without any change in its own ownership. If a second shareholder is designated tomorrow, and their holding takes the blocked aggregate over fifty per cent, the company is blocked from that moment with nothing about the company having changed.


**4.2.4** Aggregation across a chain Aggregation and the cascade combine, and the combination is where analysts most often lose the thread. Two worked chains show why. First chain. Designated individual X holds 30 per cent of Company A. Designated individual Y, unconnected to X, holds 25 per cent of Company A. Together they hold 55 per cent, so Company A is blocked. Company A then holds 40 per cent of Company B, and X separately holds 15 per cent of Company B directly. Blocked persons therefore hold 55 per cent of Company B — Company A's 40 as a blocked person, plus X's 15 — and Company B is blocked. Notice what happened. Neither X nor Y approaches the threshold at either level individually. X's ultimate economic interest in Company B is roughly 27 per cent. Company B is blocked in full. Second chain, and this one runs the other way. Designated individual Z holds 45 per cent of Company C. Company C is not blocked — 45 is below the threshold and no other blocked person holds an interest. Company C holds 100 per cent of Company D. Company D is not blocked either, because Company C is not a blocked person and Z's holding in Company D is indirect and, at 45 per cent, still below the threshold. So a 45 per cent holding blocks nothing however long the chain, and a 50 per cent holding blocks everything below it however long the chain. The rule has a cliff edge at exactly fifty and behaves entirely differently on each side of it. The practical instruction that follows: at every level, list all shareholders, mark those who are blocked persons — including entities blocked by the rule at a higher level — and sum only those. Do not compute a percentage down the chain. That is UBO arithmetic and it gives the wrong answer.


**4.2.5** What the rule does not do Three limits are worth knowing precisely, because analysts frequently over-apply the rule as well as under-apply it. It does not aggregate ownership with control. A designated person owning 30 per cent and exercising board control does not trigger the arithmetic rule, whatever the practical reality. OFAC's guidance urges caution; it does not make the entity blocked. It does not run upwards. A designated subsidiary does not block its parent. The parent owns the subsidiary, not the reverse, and the rule tests ownership by blocked persons. A parent with a designated subsidiary has a serious problem to manage but is not itself blocked by that fact. It does not reach sister companies laterally. Two subsidiaries of the same clean parent, one designated, does not block the other — unless the designated one holds an interest in it. Getting these limits right matters as much as getting the rule right. Blocking a parent because its subsidiary is designated is an unlawful denial of service, and it is a mistake made by institutions attempting to be careful.


**4.2.6** What OFAC says about control The rule is about ownership and stops there, but OFAC has published guidance on entities that a blocked person controls without owning, and it repays reading precisely because it is not a prohibition. The guidance states that US persons are advised to act with caution when considering a transaction with a non-blocked entity in which a blocked person has a significant ownership interest that is less than fifty per cent, or which a blocked person may control by means other than a majority ownership interest. It notes that such entities may be the subject of future designation or enforcement. Three things follow. It is advice, not law: an entity in that position is not blocked and dealing with it is not prohibited. It is a warning about the future: OFAC is saying it may designate the entity, and if it does, the relationship you have built becomes a problem overnight. And it puts the institution on notice, which matters if enforcement ever follows — an institution that dealt with a 49-per-cent-owned entity having considered the guidance is in a different position from one that never looked. The practical handling is a risk decision rather than a compliance determination. An entity with a 49 per cent designated shareholder is lawful to deal with and is a materially different proposition from one with no designated shareholder at all. ## Scenario — TWENTY-FIVE PER CENT, BLOCKED IN FULL

Your customer is a Cyprus-registered trading company. Its shareholder register shows a single corporate shareholder, a Netherlands holding company, holding 100 per cent. The Netherlands company is 50 per cent owned by an individual designated last month and 50 per cent by an unconnected pension fund. A relationship manager argues the designated individual's ultimate economic interest in your customer is 50 per cent of 100, and that as this is not more than fifty per cent, and the pension fund holds the other half, the position is finely balanced but acceptable. **Question.** Is it? **Answer.** No, and the analysis contains two errors. First, 50 per cent is caught — the rule reads fifty per cent or more, so the Netherlands company is blocked outright, not finely balanced.

Second, once the Netherlands company is blocked it is a blocked person, and it holds 100 per cent of your customer. Your customer is blocked in full. The pension fund's stake is irrelevant: it does not dilute the designated interest for these purposes, because the test at each level asks what proportion blocked persons hold, and at the level of your customer that proportion is 100 per cent. The pension fund's position is genuinely unfortunate — it holds an interest in a blocked entity through no fault of its own — but that is a matter for a licence application, not for the analysis of whether the prohibition applies.


## What to take from this chapter
- Fifty per cent or more, in aggregate, directly or indirectly, by one or more blocked persons
- Exactly fifty is caught. Forty-nine is not, whatever the practical control
- The rule tests ownership, not control. OFAC urges caution on controlled entities but caution is not prohibition
- Each level is tested independently, so a blocked company's 50 per cent blocks the next entity — with no dilution as the chain lengthens
- UBO analysis multiplies down the chain; the Fifty Percent Rule does not. They give different answers and only one is the legal test
- Aggregation means one designated shareholder holding under half does not end the analysis. You need the whole register
- The rule does not run upwards to parents and does not reach sister companies laterally. Blocking those is unlawful denial of service.
