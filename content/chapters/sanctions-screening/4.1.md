# Why ownership extends the freeze

If an asset freeze applied only to the named person, it would be trivial to defeat. This chapter explains the extension, why it produces entities that are blocked without being listed, and why that fact changes what screening can and cannot achieve.


**4.1.1** The problem the rule solves Freeze the assets of a named individual and they will hold assets through a company instead. Freeze that company and they will hold it through another, incorporated somewhere with weaker disclosure. The designation would restrict nothing but the person's own current account. Every major regime therefore extends the freeze to entities owned or controlled by designated persons. The property of such an entity is treated as property in which the designated person has an interest, and is caught. The extension is not an anti-avoidance provision bolted on afterwards. It is the mechanism that makes an asset freeze meaningful at all, because holding wealth through corporate structures is the ordinary condition of the people these regimes target — not an evasive response to designation. The consequence for you is that a designation of one individual may capture dozens of operating companies across several jurisdictions, none of which will appear on the list you screen against.


**4.1.2** How much exposure sits here It is worth putting a number on this, because the ownership extension is usually described as an edge case and it is not. Take a designated individual with a commercial network — an oligarch, a procurement facilitator, a senior official with business interests. The designation is one entry on a list. The entities caught by it may number in the dozens or the hundreds: operating companies, holding vehicles, property companies, single-ship owners, trading arms, and their subsidiaries in turn. OFAC has periodically published lists of entities it assesses as blocked by ownership, and commercial data providers maintain far larger ones. The consistent finding is that the derived population substantially exceeds the designated population. For heavily networked individuals the ratio has been observed at ten to one and higher. Now consider the direction of visibility. The designated individual appears on your list and will be matched by any competent screening engine. The hundred entities they own appear nowhere and will be matched by nothing. That is the shape of the exposure: the part of the prohibition that a screening system detects is the smaller part, and it is the part least likely to appear in your transactions, because a designated individual does not usually make payments in their own name after being designated.


**4.1.3** Blocked without being listed This is the single most important operational fact in the module, and it is the one most often misunderstood by people who have worked in sanctions for years. Under the US Fifty Percent Rule, an entity owned fifty per cent or more by one or more blocked persons is itself blocked. It is blocked by operation of the rule. OFAC does not designate it, does not announce it, and does not add it to the SDN List. There is no publication event, no list update, and no notification. The rule is stated in OFAC's own guidance in terms that leave no room for doubt: such entities are blocked regardless of whether they appear on the SDN List, and the property and interests in property of such entities are blocked. Follow the consequence through. An institution whose entire sanctions control is screening customer and payment data against published lists cannot detect a Fifty Percent Rule entity. The screening system is working exactly as designed and will return no hit, because there is no name to match. The exposure is invisible to the control. The EU and UK positions differ in the detail of the test but produce the same structural result: entities are caught without being separately designated. What follows is that ownership analysis is not an enhancement to screening. It is a different control, running on different data, and an institution that has one without the other has a gap that no amount of tuning will close.


**4.1.4** Where the answer actually comes from If the list does not tell you, something else must. In practice there are four sources and each has a limitation worth knowing. Customer due diligence records are the primary source. Beneficial ownership information collected at onboarding and refreshed periodically is what allows you to know that your customer is owned by a designated person. Its limitation is currency: ownership recorded three years ago may have changed, and a designation made yesterday must be tested against ownership as it stands today. Corporate registries are the public source and vary enormously in quality. Some jurisdictions publish full shareholder and beneficial ownership data. Others publish nominee directors and nothing more. Several publish nothing accessible at all. The registry that would answer your question is frequently in the jurisdiction that does not have one. Commercial data providers assemble ownership graphs from registries, filings, news and litigation records, and several publish derived lists of entities they assess as caught by ownership rules. These are useful and they are assessments, not designations. A provider's view that an 60 entity is fifty-per-cent-owned is evidence, not authority, and the institution remains responsible for its own conclusion. The customer themselves is the fourth source, and an underused one. Asking a corporate customer to confirm its ownership chain, in writing, at a point when a designation has made the question live is legitimate, quick and creates a record. The answer may be false, but a false written representation is a materially different position from no enquiry at all.


## Scenario — THE CLEAN NAME THAT WAS BLOCKED

A logistics company incorporated in a European jurisdiction has held an account with your bank for six years. It appears on no sanctions list, screening has never alerted, and its payment activity is consistent and unremarkable. A designation is announced against an individual with no obvious connection to the company. Two weeks later a journalist publishes an ownership chain showing the designated individual holds 62 per cent of the logistics company through two intermediate holding companies. **Question.** What was the position during those two weeks, and what is it now? **Answer.** The position did not change when the journalist published.

The company became blocked at the moment the individual was designated, because the ownership already existed — the publication revealed the position rather than creating it. During those two weeks the bank was dealing with a blocked entity and every transaction it processed was a prohibited dealing, notwithstanding that no control the bank operated could have detected it. That is uncomfortable and it is the correct legal analysis. What it argues for is not better screening — screening cannot solve this — but an ownership refresh triggered by designation events, checking new designations against held beneficial ownership records rather than only against names in payments. The remedial steps now are to freeze, report, review the two weeks of activity, and disclose.

Voluntary disclosure of a breach discovered and reported by the institution is treated very differently from one found by a regulator.


## What to take from this chapter
- The ownership extension is what makes an asset freeze meaningful; holding wealth through companies is ordinary, not evasive
- US Fifty Percent Rule entities are blocked by operation of the rule, with no listing, no announcement and no list update
- A screening system cannot detect them. There is no name to match and the control is working as designed
- Ownership analysis is a separate control on separate data, not a refinement of screening
- CDU records answer the question but go stale; registries vary from complete to non-existent
- Commercial providers' ownership assessments are evidence, not authority. The conclusion remains yours
- Asking the customer in writing is legitimate and creates a record even if the answer is false.
