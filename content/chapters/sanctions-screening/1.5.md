# The cost of sanctions

Targeting solved much of the humanitarian problem but not all of it, and the residue is something you will meet directly in alert handling. This chapter covers de-risking, over-compliance, and the humanitarian exemptions that have been built to address both.


**1.5.1** De-risking The largest residual harm is not caused by sanctions directly. It is caused by institutions withdrawing from markets, correspondent relationships and customer categories that sanctions make expensive to serve. The calculation is straightforward and, from the institution's perspective, rational. Serving a small bank in a jurisdiction adjacent to a sanctioned country generates screening cost, alert volume, enhanced diligence obligations, regulatory scrutiny and the tail risk of a penalty running to hundreds of millions. The revenue from that relationship does not come close. The relationship is exited. The consequence falls on parties that are not sanctioned and have done nothing wrong. Money transfer operators serving remittance corridors lose bank accounts. Humanitarian organisations working in conflict zones cannot move funds to their own field offices. Small banks in the Caribbean, the Pacific and parts of Africa lose correspondent relationships and with them access to the dollar system. This is a documented systemic issue, not a complaint. The Financial Action Task Force, the Financial Stability Board, the World Bank and successive UN reports have all identified de-risking as a problem requiring policy attention, and regulators have issued explicit statements discouraging wholesale exit on the basis of customer category rather than assessed risk.


**1.5.2** Over-compliance The institutional analogue of de-risking is over-blocking: treating anything that resembles a designated party as prohibited, on the reasoning that a false block is safer than a false release. That reasoning is wrong, and increasingly regulators say so directly. A false block is not a neutral act. It denies a service to someone entitled to receive it, and in humanitarian, medical and remittance contexts it has consequences measured in delayed treatment and unpaid families. It also degrades the institution's own control environment, because an alert queue full of unnecessary blocks obscures the alerts that matter. OFAC has said in guidance that over-compliance is a concern; the EU has issued clarifications specifically to counter over-implementation of its measures; and OFSI's published enforcement material treats disproportionate action as a failure of the control rather than as caution. The professional standard is precision. Block exactly what must be blocked. Release exactly what may be released. Both halves carry equal weight, and an analyst who is only ever wrong in one direction is not being careful — they are being imprecise in a way that happens to feel safe.


**1.5.3** Humanitarian exemptions The response to both problems has been to build explicit humanitarian carve-outs into the regimes themselves rather than leaving relief to case-by-case licensing. The most significant development is UN Security Council Resolution 2664, adopted in December 2022. It established a standing humanitarian exemption across UN asset-freeze regimes, permitting the provision of funds and services necessary to ensure the timely delivery of humanitarian assistance, by the UN, by NGOs and by other specified actors, without requiring prior approval. This was a substantial change: previously each regime handled humanitarian relief differently and largely through individual licences. Equivalent provisions exist at national level. OFAC maintains general licences authorising humanitarian activity across most of its programmes, covering food, medicine, medical devices and NGO activity. The EU has introduced humanitarian derogations across its regimes. The UK operates general licences on a similar basis. The operational point for an analyst is that a payment connected to a sanctioned jurisdiction is not automatically prohibited, and a humanitarian purpose is not a mitigating factor to be weighed — it may be an authorisation that makes the transaction lawful outright. Establishing whether a general licence applies is part of the analysis, not an appeal to sympathy after the analysis has concluded.


**1.5.4** What this means for how you work This chapter belongs at the start of the study guide rather than in an appendix because it should shape how you think about the whole function. The purpose of sanctions screening is not to block as much as possible. It is to identify precisely what is prohibited and to let everything else through without delay. An institution that blocks a hundred payments to catch one is not ten times safer than one that blocks two; it is imposing ninety-eight unlawful denials of service and burying the one real hit in noise. The measure of a good screening function is not how much it stops. It is how accurately it distinguishes. Everything in the remaining modules — matching thresholds, secondary identifiers, ownership analysis, discounting logic, vessel identity — exists to serve that distinction.


## Scenario — THE AID PAYMENT

An international medical charity instructs a payment of $180,000 from its London account to a supplier in a country under comprehensive UN and national measures. The funds are for medical equipment destined for a field hospital. Your system alerts on the destination country and on the beneficiary bank, which is designated. **Question.** The purpose is plainly humanitarian. Is that a reason to release the payment? **Answer.** Not on its own — sympathy is not an authorisation. But it is a reason to establish whether an authorisation exists, which is a different thing.

The analysis should identify whether the charity falls within the scope of UNSCR 2664 or an equivalent national general licence, whether the specific goods fall within the licensed categories, and whether the designated beneficiary bank is itself covered or excluded by the terms of that licence. A general licence that authorises humanitarian activity does not necessarily authorise routing it through a designated financial institution. The correct disposition is either a release under a named licence, with the licence identified in the rationale, or an escalation to establish whether a specific licence is required. What it is not is a release because the cause is good, or a block because the country is sanctioned.


## What to take from this chapter
- De-risking withdraws service from entire categories and is treated by FATF, the FSB and the World Bank as a systemic harm
- Over-blocking is a compliance failure in its own right. Regulators do not treat it as a safe harbour
- UNSCR 2664 (2022) created a standing humanitarian exemption across UN asset-freeze regimes
- A humanitarian purpose may be an authorisation, not a mitigating factor. Identify the licence by name or escalate
