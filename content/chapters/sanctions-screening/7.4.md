Evasion techniques Maritime sanctions evasion is the most developed concealment architecture in the field, and it has grown substantially since 2022. This chapter covers the principal techniques and what each looks like in the documents a bank sees. 7.4.1  Ship-to-ship transfers Cargo is transferred between vessels at sea rather than at a terminal. The technique is entirely legitimate in ordinary trade — it is used for lightering large tankers that cannot enter shallow ports, and for parcelling cargoes — and it is also the central evasion method. Used evasively, it breaks the chain between origin and destination. Oil loaded at a sanctioned terminal is transferred at sea to a second vessel, which then arrives at a discharge port having loaded, on her documents, from a location that raises no question. The cargo's true origin is concealed by the fact that no single vessel's voyage connects the two ends. The known transfer areas are well documented and recur: waters off Malaysia and Singapore, the Gulf of Oman near Fujairah, the Kerch Strait and the eastern Mediterranean off Kalamata and Ceuta. In documents the technique surfaces as inconsistencies rather than as an admission: a bill of lading naming a load port the vessel's track does not support, a voyage duration inconsistent with the stated route, or a cargo quantity that does not correspond to a single loading. 7.4.2  Flag hopping and open registries A vessel is registered in a state, and reflagging is a routine commercial decision driven by cost, crewing rules and regulatory burden. Open registries — Panama, Liberia, the Marshall Islands and others — register vessels with no national connection to the state, and this is entirely normal. Used evasively, reflagging serves two purposes. It breaks continuity: a vessel that changes flag changes MMSI and call sign, and any control keyed to those loses her. And it moves her to a registry that does not screen, does not respond to enquiries and does not deregister on designation. The pattern to notice is frequency and direction. One reflagging in a decade is ordinary. Three in four years, each move toward a registry with weaker oversight, is a trajectory. Several small registries have been publicly identified as having registered large numbers of vessels involved in sanctioned trades, and some have had registrations revoked wholesale after investigation. A related technique is false registration: a vessel flying a flag she is not entitled to, or registered simultaneously in two states. Flag states have on several occasions issued public notices disowning vessels claiming their registration. 7.4.3  Name changes and identity laundering Renaming is ordinary. Renaming repeatedly, in combination with reflagging and a change of registered owner, is identity laundering: the deliberate construction of a vessel with no visible history. 119 The pattern in Figure 7.1 is the recognisable form — a new name, a new flag, a new single-ship owning company and a new MMSI, all within a short period, with the IMO number as the only thread connecting them. The most aggressive version is IMO number falsification, where documents state a number belonging to a different vessel, or a number that does not exist. The check digit rule from Chapter 7.2 catches the invented ones. Catching a borrowed one requires comparing the stated IMO against the vessel's other stated characteristics — a number belonging to a bulk carrier appearing on documents for a tanker is a discrepancy anybody can see. A further variant is the use of a vessel that has been scrapped: her identity continues to appear in documents after the hull has been broken up, on the reasoning that the identity has a clean history and no one will check whether the ship still exists. 7.4.4  Insurance, class and the withdrawal signal Two commercial facts about a vessel carry substantial screening value, because they represent decisions taken by parties who did diligence. Protection and indemnity insurance covers third-party liabilities including pollution, and the thirteen clubs of the International Group cover the overwhelming majority of world tonnage. They screen before writing cover and withdraw it where a vessel becomes designated or engages in trades they will not support. A vessel outside International Group cover is either small, or in a trade the mainstream market will not touch. Classification confirms a vessel is built and maintained to a recognised standard, and is a precondition of insurance and of port entry in many jurisdictions. The major societies of the International Association of Classification Societies screen similarly. A vessel classed by a society outside IACS, or unclassed, is in the same position. Withdrawal is the signal. A vessel that was covered by an International Group club and is no longer, or was classed by an IACS member and is no longer, has had a decision taken about her by an organisation with better information than you have. That is worth more than most of the data you can gather directly.
