# Why targeting replaced embargoes

Three arguments drove the shift, and each one still shapes how regimes are built today. This chapter sets them out, and then addresses honestly the question analysts are least often given a straight answer to: whether sanctions actually work.


**1.4.1** The moral argument If the purpose of a sanction is to change the behaviour of a government, imposing its cost on a population that has no means of changing that government is difficult to justify. The population is not the wrongdoer, has no vote in an authoritarian state, and cannot negotiate. The Iraq experience made this argument practically unanswerable, whatever the underlying epidemiological data showed. Once the political perception had formed that comprehensive sanctions killed children while leaving the leadership comfortable, the instrument could not be used again at that scale. The moral argument also produced the humanitarian exemption, which is now standard. Chapter 5 of this module covers where that has got to.


**1.4.2** The practical argument Comprehensive embargoes leak, and the leaks are structurally advantageous to the people the sanctions target. A total prohibition on trade with a country does not stop trade with that country. It moves it into channels controlled by whoever can operate outside the law — which, in a state under sanction, means the regime and those it favours. Scarcity creates margin, and the margin accrues to whoever controls the smuggling route. Elites in comprehensively sanctioned states frequently emerged from the experience wealthier and more entrenched than they entered it, having captured the black market the sanctions created. Meanwhile the ordinary economy — which operates on thin margins and legal channels — collapsed. The second practical problem is narrative. External economic hostility is an extremely convenient explanation for internal hardship, and a government under comprehensive sanction is handed that explanation for free.


**1.4.3** The diplomatic argument A comprehensive embargo is close to an all-or-nothing instrument. It is difficult to escalate, because it is already near-total. It is difficult to calibrate, because it applies to an entire economy. And it is politically hard to lift, because lifting it looks like capitulation. Targeted measures behave differently. They can be applied in tranches, expanded in response to specific conduct, and unwound selectively as part of a negotiated settlement. That makes them usable as a bargaining instrument rather than only as a punishment. The Iran nuclear negotiations are the clearest illustration. Measures were escalated over several years in response to specific programme milestones, then partially suspended under the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action in 2015, then reimposed after the US withdrawal in 2018. Whatever one thinks of the outcome, the instrument was being used as a dial rather than a switch — which a comprehensive embargo cannot be.


**1.4.4** Do sanctions work? An honest answer You will encounter both overstatement and dismissal on this question, and neither is useful. The empirical literature is genuinely contested, and the honest position is more qualified than either camp allows. What is reasonably well established: targeted sanctions impose real and substantial costs on designated individuals and entities, and they do restrict access to the international financial system in ways that are hard to work around at scale. Designated parties lose correspondent banking, insurance, and access to major currencies. That is not nothing. What is not established: that sanctions on their own produce the policy reversal they were imposed to achieve. The frequently cited estimate that sanctions succeed in roughly a third of cases comes from work by Hufbauer and colleagues at the Peterson Institute, and even that figure has been contested downward by other scholars using stricter definitions of success. The most defensible summary is that sanctions are one instrument among several; they work best when the objective is specific and achievable rather than regime change, when they are multilateral rather than unilateral, and when they are combined with a credible diplomatic path to relief. Sanctions with no exit ramp tend to become permanent features of the landscape rather than instruments of change. For your purposes the important point is a professional one. Whether sanctions work is a question for policymakers. Your obligation does not vary with the answer, and an analyst who lets scepticism about the policy affect the rigour of a disposition has substituted their own judgement for the law's. Equally, an analyst who believes sanctions are unambiguously effective may over-block. Neither belief belongs in the rationale.


## What to take from this chapter
- Three arguments drove the shift: the moral cost to populations, the practical leakiness of embargoes, and the need for a calibrated diplomatic instrument
- Comprehensive regimes often enriched the elites they targeted by creating black markets they controlled
- Targeted measures can be escalated and unwound in tranches, which is what makes them usable in negotiation
- Effectiveness is genuinely contested. Targeted measures impose real costs but rarely produce policy reversal alone
- Your obligation does not vary with the answer. Neither scepticism nor enthusiasm belongs in a disposition.
