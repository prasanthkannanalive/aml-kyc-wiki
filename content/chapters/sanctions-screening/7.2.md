# Vessel identity

A ship has more identifiers than a person and all but one of them can be changed. This chapter covers each, which are reliable, and why the interests in a vessel matter as much as the vessel itself.


**7.2.1** The IMO number The IMO ship identification number is a seven-digit number assigned when the hull is built and it does not change for the life of the vessel. It survives a change of name, a change of flag, a change of owner, a change of class and a change of trade. It is the only identifier with that property. It is also structured: the seventh digit is a check digit derived from the first six, so a transcription error usually produces an invalid number rather than a different valid vessel. An analyst who knows the check digit rule can validate an IMO number in a few seconds without any reference data — multiply the first six digits by seven, six, five, four, three and two respectively, sum them, and the last digit of the total is the check digit. Every mandatory element of vessel identity in the sanctions context therefore reduces to one question: does the IMO number appear on a list. Name matching on vessels is a supplementary control, and a weak one. Two limitations. IMO numbers are not universal — smaller vessels, fishing vessels and some coastal craft are outside the scheme. And they are occasionally falsified in documents, which is a different problem from being changed: the number on the paper is wrong, the number on the hull is not.


**7.2.2** Everything that changes The name changes and changes often. A vessel may carry four or five names in a decade, some for entirely ordinary commercial reasons — sale, rebranding, transfer within a fleet — and some to break a screening trail. The flag changes. A vessel is registered in a state and flies its flag; reflagging is a normal commercial act and also the core of one evasion technique. Chapter 7.4 covers flag hopping. The MMSI number changes with the flag, because it is issued by the flag state and its first three digits encode the country. The call sign changes with the flag for the same reason. The owner changes, the manager changes, the charterer changes voyage by voyage. A screening control keyed to the name would have missed her three times.


**7.2.3** Interests in a vessel Several parties hold different relationships to a ship, and sanctions may attach to any of them. An analyst should know the vocabulary because documents use it precisely. The registered owner holds legal title, and is very often a single-ship company incorporated in an offshore jurisdiction for liability reasons. That is ordinary industry practice, not in itself an indicator. The beneficial owner is whoever ultimately controls the single-ship company, and is frequently not determinable from any registry. This is the ownership problem from Module 4 in its most acute form. The technical manager operates the ship: crewing, maintenance, compliance with maritime regulation. The commercial operator or disponent owner controls where she trades and what she carries. The charterer hires her, under a time charter for a period or a voyage charter for a passage. Any of these can be designated independently. A clean vessel with a designated commercial operator is a problem; so is a designated vessel with a clean charterer, though a different one. The disposition must say which interest was designated, because the prohibition follows the interest.


**7.2.4** Which lists carry vessels OFAC designates vessels on the SDN List with IMO numbers, and publishes them in the same file as individuals and entities. The EU, UK, UN and other regimes designate vessels in their own lists. Beyond designations there are advisories, and the distinction matters. OFAC, the UK, the EU and others publish maritime advisories identifying vessels of concern, deceptive shipping practices, and in some cases named ships that are not designated. An advisory is guidance, not a prohibition: dealing with a vessel named in an advisory is not unlawful by that fact alone. There are also third-party and industry lists — vessels of interest published by commercial data providers, by industry bodies, and by non-governmental researchers tracking sanctioned trades. These are useful intelligence and carry no legal effect. The screening discipline is the same as in Module 2: record which source produced the hit, and let the disposition follow from what that source actually does. Blocking on an advisory listing is the maritime equivalent of blocking on an Entity List hit. Figure 7.1 — One vessel, four names, one IMO number. ## What to take from this chapter
- The IMO number is assigned at build and never changes. It is the only stable vessel identifier
- It carries a check digit, so a transcription error usually produces an invalid number rather than a different valid ship
- Name, flag, MMSI and call sign all change, and MMSI and call sign change with the flag because the flag state issues them
- Name matching on vessels is a supplementary control and a weak one
- Registered owner, beneficial owner, technical manager, commercial operator and charterer are distinct interests, any of which may be designated
- Single-ship companies in offshore jurisdictions are ordinary industry practice, not an indicator in themselves
- An advisory naming a vessel is guidance, not a prohibition. Blocking on one repeats the Entity List error.
