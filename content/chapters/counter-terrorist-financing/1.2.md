Where the money comes from Terrorist financing draws on sources that range from an ordinary wage to organised crime to the tax revenue of territory under armed control. This chapter covers the range, because the source determines both the scale of the problem and what an institution can realistically see. 1.2.1  Legitimate sources A substantial proportion of terrorist financing originates in money that is entirely lawful, and this is the single hardest fact for an AML-trained analyst to internalise. Employment income. Salaries and wages, saved or diverted. Nothing in the earning of them is unlawful and nothing in the account behaviour marks them out. Small business revenue. Restaurants, car washes, mobile phone shops, import-export trading. The business may be entirely genuine and its takings entirely declared, with a portion diverted. Personal savings and family wealth. Money accumulated over years, withdrawn or transferred at a moment that has nothing to do with how it was acquired. Loans and credit. Personal loans and credit cards taken out with no intention of repayment, sometimes across several institutions in a short period — one of the few genuinely detectable patterns in this category, and covered in Module 4. Charitable donations. Given in good faith by donors who have no idea where the money ends up, and diverted at some point in the chain between collection and delivery. This is a serious problem and also a serious source of over-reaction, which Chapter 2.4 of Module 2 addresses directly. 1.2.2  Criminal sources Terrorist organisations also fund themselves through crime, and where they do, the AML and TF pictures overlap — which is the one circumstance in which conventional laundering controls will help you. Narcotics trafficking is the largest by value. Several proscribed organisations have financed themselves substantially through the drug trade, either directly or by taxing production and transit in territory they influence. Kidnap for ransom has been a major and well-documented income stream, particularly in the Sahel and parts of the Middle East. Ransoms are typically paid in cash and frequently move through informal channels. Extortion and protection payments extracted from businesses and residents in areas of operation. These generate a steady, local, cash-based income and rarely touch the formal financial system at the point of collection. Fraud of many kinds — benefit fraud, credit card fraud, VAT and excise fraud, counterfeit goods. Low-risk, low-visibility, and well-suited to funding cells operating in Western jurisdictions. The looting and sale of antiquities, and the trade in cultural property from conflict zones. 15 1.2.3  State sponsorship and territorial control Two sources sit outside the ordinary categories because they operate at a scale individual compliance functions cannot address. State sponsorship. Financial, material or logistical support provided by a government to a proscribed organisation. It rarely appears as an identifiable transfer in the commercial banking system; where it surfaces at all, it does so through front companies, procurement networks and sanctioned financial institutions — which is where CFT meets sanctions screening, the subject of Module 5. Territorial control. An organisation that holds territory can tax it. At its peak, ISIL derived very substantial revenue from oil sales, taxation, extortion and the seizure of bank branches within the area it controlled — an income model closer to a state's than to a criminal group's. For an analyst, both matter mainly as context. You will not detect state sponsorship from a payment message. But knowing that a proscribed organisation has a large, self-generated revenue base tells you something useful about what you might see: an organisation that funds itself locally has less need to move money internationally, and the transfers you do observe are more likely to be operational — paying a facilitator, moving a fighter, buying a specific item — than to be bulk funding. 1.2.4  Why the mix matters The distribution of sources is not academic. It determines what proportion of terrorist financing is visible to a financial institution at all. Money originating in crime and passing through the formal system has laundering characteristics, and conventional AML monitoring has a genuine chance of surfacing it. Money originating in lawful income has none of those characteristics. It will be surfaced, if at all, by the identity of the counterparty, the pattern of the activity, or intelligence supplied from outside. And money that never enters the formal system — cash extortion, ransom payments in currency, hawala settlement outside banking channels — is invisible to a financial institution by definition, whatever it does. An honest description of a CFT control therefore starts by saying what it cannot reach. That is not a counsel of despair; it is what stops an institution believing its monitoring covers a risk it does not.
