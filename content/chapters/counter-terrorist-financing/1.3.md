The scale problem Terrorist financing involves sums so small that the machinery built to detect financial crime does not register them. This chapter puts numbers to that, and explains why value-based controls fail against this risk specifically. 1.3.1  What attacks actually cost The most useful single fact in counter-terrorist financing is how little money is involved. Studies of completed attacks in Europe, including work by the Norwegian Defence Research Establishment covering attacks over more than two decades, have consistently found that the large majority cost under a few thousand euros, and a substantial share cost under a few hundred. Vehicle and bladed-weapon attacks in particular can be mounted for the cost of a rental deposit or a kitchen knife. Even the most expensive attacks in modern memory are modest by financial crime standards. The 2004 Madrid train bombings have been estimated at around ten thousand euros. The 2005 London bombings at a similar or lower figure. The 11 September 2001 attacks, the outlier, are estimated at four to five hundred thousand dollars — which is a rounding error against a single significant laundering case. Set that against an AML environment where a reporting threshold of ten thousand units of currency is standard and monitoring rules are commonly tuned around sums an order of magnitude higher. Almost every attack in the past twenty years could have been funded entirely beneath the threshold at which anything would have been reviewed. 1.3.2  Why thresholds fail here Threshold-based monitoring rests on an assumption that holds for laundering and fails for financing: that criminal value is proportionate to criminal harm. In laundering, that assumption is broadly sound. A large fraud produces large proceeds and those proceeds must move, so the money is roughly proportionate to the offence, and a threshold set to catch significant sums catches significant crime. In terrorist financing the relationship breaks entirely. The harm is measured in lives; the money is measured in hundreds. There is no value at which a threshold would catch financing without also catching an unmanageable share of ordinary retail activity — because ordinary retail activity is exactly what it resembles. This is why an institution cannot address TF by lowering thresholds. Halving them doubles the alert volume and still misses the four-hundred-euro transfer. The answer is not a different number; it is a different kind of control, which is what Module 4 is about. 1.3.3  Structuring is not the signal here In AML, a series of payments just beneath a reporting threshold is a classic indicator: someone knows where the line is and is deliberately staying under it. That inference is much weaker in TF, and the reason is worth understanding. A terrorist financier moving four hundred euros is not structuring. They are moving four hundred euros, because that is the amount required. The transaction sits below the threshold incidentally, not by design. 18 So the absence of structuring tells you nothing, and its presence tells you less than it would in a laundering case. Amounts that look like deliberate avoidance in an AML context look like ordinary remittance in a TF context — and frequently are ordinary remittance, which is the false positive problem Module 4 addresses at length. The practical implication: a monitoring rule written to catch structuring will not catch financing, and if it fires on a TF case at all it will be by coincidence. 1.3.4  Low signal, high consequence The combination that defines this discipline is a very weak financial signal attached to a very severe outcome. In most financial crime, the strength of the signal is roughly proportionate to the seriousness of the case: a large fraud is loud, a small one is quiet. Terrorist financing inverts that too. A transfer that would fund a mass-casualty attack is quieter than a fraudulent invoice for a used car. This has an uncomfortable consequence for how institutions think about materiality. There is no de minimis. A payment of two hundred euros deserves the same treatment as a payment of two million if the indicators are present, and an analyst who deprioritises small alerts on a TF-flagged pattern is applying an AML instinct to a risk where it does not hold. It also means the meaningful work happens outside the transaction. Since the payment itself carries almost no information, everything depends on who the parties are, what the pattern looks like over time, and what is known about the counterparty from elsewhere. The transaction is the smallest part of the case. SCENARIO — BELOW EVERY THRESHOLD Over eleven weeks a customer makes cash withdrawals of €200, €150, €300, €200 and €250 from ATMs in three cities, buys a €180 prepaid card, and pays €340 for a short-term vehicle hire. Total activity: €1,620. No single transaction approaches any reporting threshold. The account is otherwise ordinary and funded entirely by salary. Question.  What would a conventional AML monitoring system make of this? Answer.  Almost certainly nothing. Every amount is unremarkable, the funding is clean, there is no structuring in the AML sense because nothing is near a threshold to be structured beneath, and the total over eleven weeks is consistent with ordinary living expenses. A system tuned for laundering has no rule that fires here. What is present, if you are looking for it, is a pattern rather than a transaction: cash extraction across multiple locations, conversion into an anonymous stored-value instrument, and a vehicle hire — a sequence that means nothing individually and something specific in combination. Whether that combination is worth acting on is Module 4's subject, and the honest answer is that on this evidence alone it is not.
