# Hawala and informal value transfer

Informal value transfer systems move enormous sums worldwide, overwhelmingly for entirely legitimate purposes. This chapter explains how they work, why they are used, where the terrorist financing risk genuinely sits, and why treating the system itself as the risk is both wrong and counterproductive.


**3.2.1** How hawala works Hawala is a system for transferring value without transferring money. It predates modern banking by centuries and remains in wide use across South Asia, the Middle East, the Horn of Africa and among diaspora communities everywhere. The mechanism is simple. A sender gives cash to a broker — a hawaladar — in one city. That broker contacts a counterpart in the destination city, who pays the equivalent to the recipient, usually within hours and often against nothing more than a code word. No money crosses a border. What crosses is an instruction and an obligation. The two brokers now hold a balance between them, and settle it later — by netting against transfers running the other way, through trade in goods, or occasionally by a conventional bank transfer. Settlement is periodic and aggregated, which is why the individual transfers are invisible even where the settlement is not. The system runs on trust and reputation rather than contract. A hawaladar who fails to pay loses the network, and the network is the business. It is not inherently illegal. In many jurisdictions hawala operators are licensed or registered money service businesses, which is precisely what Recommendation 14 requires. In others the activity is unlicensed and therefore unlawful — but unlawful as unlicensed money transmission, which is a regulatory offence, not as terrorist financing.


**3.2.2** Why people use it An analyst who does not understand why hawala is used will misread every case involving it. It is cheaper. Remittance fees through formal channels are high, particularly on small transfers to developing countries, and the World Bank has tracked average costs that make a fifty-dollar remittance materially expensive. Hawala routinely undercuts them. It is faster. Delivery in hours against days. It reaches places banks do not. In conflict zones, rural areas and states with limited banking infrastructure, hawala may be the only functioning channel. Humanitarian organisations use it for precisely this reason. It requires no documentation the sender may not have. A migrant worker without a residence permit or a local bank account can still send money home. And it is trusted, in a way formal institutions frequently are not, by communities with reason for that distrust. Every one of those reasons is legitimate. The overwhelming majority of hawala volume is migrant remittance — people sending money to families. Any analytical framework that treats hawala use 38 as suspicious in itself will produce false positives at enormous scale and will fall disproportionately on exactly the people the system exists to serve.


**3.2.3** Where the terrorist financing risk sits None of that means the risk is absent. It means the risk is specific rather than general, and an analyst should know where it concentrates. The absence of a transaction record is the core issue. There is no payment message, no originator field, no beneficiary field, nothing to screen. Recommendation 16 cannot apply to a transfer that leaves no message. Settlement is aggregated and netted, so an individual transfer of a few hundred units disappears entirely into a periodic balance between two brokers. Even where the settlement passes through a bank, what the bank sees is a wholesale movement between two businesses. Reach into ungoverned space. The same property that makes hawala valuable to humanitarian organisations — it works where banks do not — makes it valuable to anyone else operating there. And the operator matters more than the system. A hawaladar knowingly moving funds for a proscribed organisation is the risk; the mechanism is the same one used by ten thousand legitimate customers that week. So the analytical target is the operator and the specific corridor, not the practice. That is the same shape as Recommendation 8 and the charitable sector in Module 2, and for the same reason.


**3.2.4** What an institution can actually do A bank rarely sees hawala directly. What it sees is a money service business account, and the practical questions are about that business. Is it registered or licensed, and with whom. This is checkable and is the single most useful fact. Does the account behaviour fit a remittance business — many small inbound cash deposits or transfers, aggregated outbound settlement to a small number of correspondents. That is the expected shape, and it is worth knowing what expected looks like so that departure from it is visible. Are the settlement counterparties known, and do any appear on a designation. Does the corridor match the customer base the business claims to serve. And what is not an indicator: that the business serves a particular community, that it operates in a corridor with a large diaspora, or that its customers send small amounts frequently. Those describe the remittance industry. Module 4 makes the general argument; this is the case where it bites hardest, because de-risking money service businesses has been documented by FATF, the Financial Stability Board and the World Bank as pushing remittance flows further from oversight rather than reducing them.


## Scenario — THE REMITTANCE BUSINESS

A registered money service business banks with you. Its account receives many small cash deposits and makes weekly consolidated transfers to four correspondents — two in South Asia, one in the Gulf, one in East Africa. Monthly volume is consistent. An analyst reviewing it notes that the business serves a predominantly one-nationality customer base, that the East African corridor is high-risk, and recommends exit. **Question.** Is that recommendation supportable? **Answer.** No, and both stated grounds fail. A remittance business serving one community is describing the remittance industry — corridors are ethnic and linguistic by nature, and a Somali-serving MSB serving Somalis is the business working, not a finding.

The corridor's risk rating is a reason for enhanced diligence, not for exit; if high-risk corridors were disqualifying, no remittance business would bank anywhere, which is the documented de-risking harm rather than a hypothetical one. What would justify concern is different in kind: unregistered status, settlement counterparties who appear on designations, a corridor that does not match the claimed customer base, or volumes inconsistent with the declared business. The correct disposition is to establish those, record what was found, and keep the account if they come back clean — because the alternative moves the same flows to somewhere nobody is looking.


## What to take from this chapter
- Hawala transfers value without moving money: two brokers, an instruction, and a balance settled later by netting or trade
- It is not inherently illegal — many operators are licensed money service businesses, which Recommendation 14 requires
- It is used because it is cheaper, faster, reaches where banks do not, needs no documentation, and is trusted
- The overwhelming majority of volume is migrant remittance, so treating hawala use as suspicious produces false positives at scale
- The risk sits in the absence of a transaction record, aggregated settlement, reach into ungoverned space, and the operator
- The analytical target is the operator and the corridor, never the practice
- De-risking money service businesses pushes remittance further from oversight — a documented harm, not a hypothetical one.
