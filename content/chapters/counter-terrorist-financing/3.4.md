Trade, virtual assets and crowdfunding Three newer channels, each with a different risk profile and each frequently described in terms that overstate the threat. This chapter covers what is actually documented, and separates it from what is assumed. 3.4.1  Trade-based movement Value can be moved by misrepresenting trade. Over-invoicing, under-invoicing, multiple invoicing for one shipment, and shipments that do not correspond to the goods described all transfer value between parties while appearing to be commerce. The technique is far more common in money laundering than in terrorist financing, and the reason is scale. Trade misinvoicing is efficient for large sums and cumbersome for small ones — setting up a shipment to move four hundred euros makes no sense. So where it appears in a TF context it is usually at the organisational level rather than the operational one: an organisation with substantial revenue moving it, rather than a cell funding an attack. It also appears as hawala settlement, which is the more relevant connection for most analysts. Where two brokers settle a balance through trade rather than a bank transfer, the trade is genuine in form and its pricing carries the value. For an institution, the visibility is in trade finance documentation rather than in payments — the same ground a sanctions analyst covers, and largely the same documentary checks. 3.4.2  Virtual assets Cryptocurrency in terrorist financing is the subject of more commentary than evidence, and an analyst should hold both halves of the picture. What is documented: proscribed organisations have solicited donations in cryptocurrency, publicly and by address, and some have raised meaningful sums. Addresses have been designated by sanctions authorities. Seizures have occurred. It is a real channel. What is also documented: the volumes are small relative to other methods, and public blockchains are a poor choice for concealment. A transparent, permanent, publicly queryable ledger is close to the worst possible place to hide a payment, and blockchain analysis has repeatedly traced flows to their conclusion. The elements that create genuine risk are specific rather than general: exchanges with weak or absent identity verification, privacy coins designed to break traceability, mixing and tumbling services, and peer-to-peer transfer that touches no regulated intermediary. One thing that makes crypto distinctive for screening: designated addresses are exact-match identifiers. There are no aliases, no transliterations and no fuzzy variants — an address matches or it does not. That makes detection trivial where the address is known and impossible where it is not, with nothing in between. 3.4.3  Crowdfunding and online solicitation Online fundraising is the channel that has grown fastest, and it is the one where legitimate and illegitimate use are hardest to separate. 43 The mechanism is straightforward: a campaign is created on a crowdfunding platform, or an appeal is posted on social media, and small donations arrive from many people. Some campaigns are outright fronts. More often the campaign is genuine — a humanitarian appeal, a medical fund, a community project — and diversion happens after collection, which is the Recommendation 8 pattern from Module 2 in a modern wrapper. Two features make it effective for financing. Small individual amounts, well below any threshold and consistent with ordinary giving. And a very large number of donors, most acting in complete good faith, which means most of the transactions are exactly what they appear to be. The direction of enquiry that works is therefore the same as with charities: not who gave, but where the money went. Donors are not the risk and treating them as one produces an enormous number of wrong answers about generous people. For an institution the observable points are the receiving account, the platform's own payment flows, and the onward distribution — which is where the question belongs. 3.4.4  Keeping the channels in proportion It is worth ending this module with an honest ranking, because attention tends to follow novelty rather than volume. The bulk of terrorist financing moves through cash and informal value transfer — channels that are old, unglamorous and largely invisible to a financial institution. A meaningful share moves through ordinary banking, looking like ordinary banking, catchable mainly through screening. Trade, virtual assets and crowdfunding are real and are growing, and they are not where most of the money is. An institution that builds a cryptocurrency monitoring capability and has no view of its money service business relationships has optimised for the interesting problem rather than the large one. The analyst's version of the same error is treating a crypto reference in a file as significant while passing over a pattern of cash withdrawals — and Module 4 is about which patterns actually carry information.
