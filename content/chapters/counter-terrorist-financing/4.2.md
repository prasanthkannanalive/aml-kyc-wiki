Account and transaction indicators This chapter sets out the indicators that genuinely carry information, with an honest weight against each. Most are weak alone. A few are strong. Knowing which is which is the difference between an analyst who escalates usefully and one who escalates constantly. 4.2.1  Funding indicators How money arrives in an account, and whether it fits. Multiple credit facilities in a short period with no repayment. Several loans or credit cards from different institutions inside weeks, drawn down and not serviced. This is one of the few genuinely strong indicators available on lawful-source financing, and Module 1's closing scenario turned on it. It is also largely invisible to any single institution, which is why credit bureau data and information-sharing arrangements matter more here than elsewhere. Income inconsistent with the account. Credits substantially exceeding declared income, or a pattern of third-party deposits into a personal account. Moderate strength, and it is a shared AML indicator. Sudden liquidation of savings or investments with no apparent purpose. Weak alone — people liquidate savings for many reasons — but meaningful as an element in a sequence. Benefit or student income being transferred abroad in amounts inconsistent with the recipient's own needs. Weak, and easily misread: supporting family abroad on a low income is extremely common and entirely ordinary. 4.2.2  Movement indicators What happens to the money once it is there. Cash withdrawal followed by conversion to stored value. Cash out, then a prepaid card, gift cards or similar. The sequence removes the funds from observation twice, and the second step has no ordinary explanation that the first does not already serve. Moderate to strong as a sequence; each element alone is nothing. Repeated moderate withdrawals across multiple locations over weeks. Moderate — and only as a pattern. A customer who uses cash is a customer who uses cash. Transfers to multiple individuals in the same jurisdiction, with no evident relationship between them. Moderate, and stronger where the amounts are similar and regular. Rapid pass-through: funds arriving and leaving within a short period, leaving no balance. Shared with AML, where it is a mule-account indicator, and worth the same weight in both. Account activity resuming after a long dormancy, particularly where the resumption is a series of transfers rather than ordinary use. Weak alone. 4.2.3  Contextual indicators Things around the account rather than in it. 48 A counterparty appearing on a terrorism designation. This is not an indicator; it is a match, and it is the strongest thing in this module by a distance. Everything else here is inference; this is a fact. Travel or transactions in a location adjacent to a conflict zone, particularly transit through a border region rather than to a destination. Moderate, and heavily dependent on other elements. Purchases with dual-use potential — certain chemicals, specific equipment, quantities inconsistent with domestic use. Strong where the incongruity is genuine, and worth remembering that most such purchases are exactly what they appear to be. Adverse media or law enforcement contact regarding the customer or a counterparty. Strong, and the mechanism by which most external intelligence actually arrives. Refusal to explain a transaction, or an explanation that changes. Moderate, and one of the few things an analyst can generate rather than merely observe — asking is a control. 4.2.4  How the indicators combine The same arithmetic as anywhere else in compliance, and it is worth being explicit because this is where escalations go wrong. One strong indicator supports escalation on its own. A designation match, a genuine dual-use incongruity, or credible adverse media each stands alone. Weak indicators do not add up to a strong one unless they are genuinely independent. Four observations that are all versions of 'this customer sends money to a poor country' are one observation stated four times, and combining them produces confidence without evidence. Independence is the test. Cash withdrawal, prepaid conversion and a designated counterparty are three different kinds of fact and reinforce one another. Corridor, community, remittance frequency and small amounts are four descriptions of the same demographic reality and reinforce nothing. And the honest conclusion when only weak indicators are present is that you have not established anything — which is a legitimate outcome, recorded as such, rather than a reason to keep listing observations until the file looks substantial. Customer is from that jurisdiction. Transfers are frequent and small. The corridor is assessed as high risk for terrorist financing. Escalated for review.' Question.  What has this escalation established? Answer.  Nothing, and it is worth being precise about why. All four statements are true and all four are the same statement: this customer sends remittances home. Nationality, corridor, frequency and amount are not four independent observations — they are one demographic fact described four times, and the escalation reads as substantial only because it lists things. Nothing here distinguishes this customer from the several million people doing exactly this today. What would make it an escalation is a fact of a different kind: a recipient appearing on a designation, an unexplained funding source, a sequence involving cash conversion, or an answer to a question that does not hold up. The correct disposition on this evidence is no escalation and a recorded rationale saying so — and an institution whose escalation volume is made of files like this one has a discrimination problem and an intelligence problem at the same time, which is Chapter 4.4.
