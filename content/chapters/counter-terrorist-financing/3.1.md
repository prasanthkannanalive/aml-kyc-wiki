# Formal banking channels

Most terrorist financing that a compliance function will ever see passes through ordinary banking. This chapter covers what that looks like, why it is unremarkable, and the one control that does most of the work.


**3.1.1** Why the banking system is used at all It is worth asking why anyone financing terrorism would use a system built to monitor them. The answer is that they use it for the same reasons everyone does: it is fast, cheap, reliable and reaches everywhere. And, crucially, they are not usually hiding the money. Module 1 established that terrorist financing frequently uses lawful funds. A person transferring a lawful salary to a lawful account has no reason to avoid a bank, because there is nothing about the money to conceal. What must remain hidden is the purpose, and purpose does not appear in a payment message. This produces the central fact of the chapter: terrorist financing through banking channels usually looks exactly like ordinary banking. There is no distinctive technique to learn, because there is no technique. The transfer is a transfer. The exceptions are the small number of cases where funds do need concealing — proceeds of fraud, narcotics or extortion — and those behave like laundering and are caught, or missed, by laundering controls.


**3.1.2** Wire transfers and the travel rule A cross-border wire transfer is the most common formal channel, and Recommendation 16 exists to make it screenable. The travel rule requires that originator and beneficiary information accompanies the transfer through the chain: name, account number, and address or an equivalent identifier for the originator; name and account number for the beneficiary. Without it, an intermediary institution sees a payment between two banks and cannot screen the people involved. The rule matters more in CFT than in AML for the reason Module 1 gave. In laundering, the value and pattern of a payment carry information. In terrorist financing they carry almost none, so identity is the whole of the signal — and a payment message stripped of identity carries nothing at all. Two gaps are worth knowing. Implementation is uneven: not every jurisdiction enforces the rule to the same standard, and information degrades as a payment crosses institutions that apply it differently. And domestic transfers are frequently subject to lighter requirements than cross-border ones, which matters because a cell operating within a country moves money domestically.


**3.1.3** The accounts themselves Beyond the transfer, the account structures used are ordinary, and that is the point. Personal current accounts, held in the person's own name, opened legitimately with genuine identification. Nothing about the onboarding is fraudulent because nothing needs to be. Student accounts and accounts of young people, which carry lower expected balances and attract less scrutiny — and which have featured in cases involving young recruits. Business accounts of small legitimate businesses, where a portion of genuine turnover is diverted. The business is real, the trading is real, and the account behaviour is consistent with the declared activity. Third-party accounts used with the holder's knowledge — a relative or associate receiving and forwarding funds. This is closer to a laundering behaviour and is one of the few places conventional monitoring may help. What is comparatively rare, and worth noting because films suggest otherwise, is elaborate corporate structuring. Layering through shell companies is a laundering technique that costs money and attracts attention. Terrorist financing rarely needs it.


**3.1.4** What actually catches it Given all of the above, an honest account of what works in the formal channel comes down to three things. Sanctions screening, first and by a distance. Designated individuals and entities appear on lists, payments name their counterparties, and a match is a match. This is why Recommendation 6 and Module 2's designation regimes matter so much operationally, and why Module 5 treats screening as the primary CFT control rather than a supporting one. Pattern monitoring built for the right shape — regular modest transfers to a jurisdiction of concern, credit taken across institutions without repayment, cash extraction followed by conversion to stored value. Module 4 is about this, and about how weak these indicators are on their own. External intelligence. A law enforcement request, a production order, or information shared through a public-private partnership frequently arrives before anything in the institution's own monitoring has fired. That is not a criticism of the monitoring; it reflects that the decisive fact is usually who the counterparty is, and that fact lives outside the bank.


## What to take from this chapter
- Terrorist financing uses banking for the same reasons everyone does, and usually has nothing about the money to conceal
- Through formal channels it looks like ordinary banking, because there is no distinctive technique to learn
- The travel rule matters more in CFT than AML: identity is the whole signal, so a stripped message carries nothing
- Implementation of the travel rule is uneven, and domestic transfers are often subject to lighter requirements
- The accounts used are ordinary — personal, student, small business, or a knowing third party's
- Elaborate shell structuring is rare. It is a laundering technique that costs money and terrorist financing rarely needs it
- Screening does most of the work; pattern monitoring is weak alone; external intelligence frequently arrives first.
