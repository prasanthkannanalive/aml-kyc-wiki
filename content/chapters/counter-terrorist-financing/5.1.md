# Where CFT meets sanctions screening

Screening is the strongest control in counter-terrorist financing, for a reason Module 1 established: identity does the work that value does in AML. This chapter covers what screening catches here, what it cannot reach, and how a terrorism hit differs from an ordinary sanctions hit.


**5.1.1** Why screening is the primary control Every other control in this guide produces inference. Screening produces a fact. A transaction pattern suggests something. A cash withdrawal sequence suggests something. A counterparty appearing on a terrorism designation is not a suggestion — it is a match against a list a state has published, and it carries a legal obligation attached to it under Recommendation 6 and Module 2's freezing rules. That is why an institution serious about CFT invests in screening before it invests in anything else. It is also why Module 4's indicator table puts a designation match in a category of its own rather than at the top of the list: it is a different kind of thing. The corollary is that screening quality is CFT quality. An institution with weak list coverage, slow updates or badly tuned matching has a weak CFT function whatever its analysts know, because the one control that produces facts is impaired.


**5.1.2** What must be screened, and against what Module 2 established that most terrorism designations are national rather than UN, which determines the list set. The UN Consolidated List, giving 1267 designations. Necessary and nowhere near sufficient. Every national terrorism list of every jurisdiction whose law reaches you. For most institutions that means at least the domestic list, the US SDGT designations, the EU terrorism list and the UK list — and the union is materially larger than the UN list alone. As to what is screened: customers at onboarding and on an ongoing basis, payment counterparties on both sides, and beneficial owners. Beneficial ownership matters here for the same reason it matters in sanctions — a designated person can hold an entity that is not itself listed, and the ownership rules make that entity caught. One thing specific to terrorism lists is worth knowing. They carry heavier alias data than most other list types — multiple transliterations, kunyas, noms de guerre, variant spellings — because the underlying names are frequently rendered from another script and the individuals use 56 working names. That improves recall and degrades precision, which is to say it produces more hits and a higher proportion of them are false. That is a deliberate trade in the list design, not a defect in your engine.


**5.1.3** How a terrorism hit differs The mechanics of clearing a hit are the same as any sanctions alert: compare identifiers, establish whether this is the listed person, record what you found. Three things differ. The urgency is higher. Module 2's without delay obligation is stated in hours, and a terrorism designation is one of the categories where an institution should be able to act inside a working day rather than within a review queue's ordinary service level. The consequence of a false negative is different in kind. A missed sanctions hit is a serious compliance failure. A missed terrorism hit may be a serious compliance failure and something else. And discounting requires more care. Terrorism list entries frequently carry sparse identifiers — a name, an approximate year of birth, a nationality, sometimes nothing more. A hit that would be discounted on a full sanctions entry, because the date of birth differs, cannot always be discounted on an entry that has no date of birth to differ from. Where the entry is thin, the honest disposition is frequently escalation rather than discount, and the rationale should say which it was and why. What does not differ is the discipline: identifiers compared, sources named, conclusion stated, and no reliance on how long the customer has banked with you.


**5.1.4** What screening cannot reach Stating this precisely is what stops an institution believing its screening covers the risk. Four limits, and each was established earlier in this guide. It cannot see undesignated parties. The large majority of terrorist financing involves people nobody has listed, and no amount of screening will surface them. It cannot see purpose. A payment between two entirely unlisted parties for an entirely terrorist purpose passes screening cleanly, because there is nothing on any list to match. It cannot see indirect benefit. Module 2's making-available prohibition catches payments that benefit a designated person through an intermediary, and an engine comparing the immediate counterparty to a list will not detect it. That obligation depends on an analyst noticing a relationship. And it cannot reach what never enters the system. Module 3's cash, hawala and locally-spent revenue are outside screening by definition. So the honest position is this: screening is the strongest control available and it addresses a minority of the risk. Both halves of that sentence are true, and an institution that states only the first has misdescribed its own coverage.


## What to take from this chapter
- Screening produces a fact where every other control produces inference — which is why it is the primary CFT control
- Screening quality is CFT quality: weak coverage or slow updates impairs the one control that produces facts
- The UN list gives you 1267 only. The national lists that reach you are a materially larger set
- Terrorism entries carry heavy alias data, which raises recall and lowers precision by design
- Terrorism hits are more urgent, and a thin list entry frequently cannot be discounted the way a full one could
- Screening cannot see undesignated parties, purpose, indirect benefit, or anything outside the system
- It is the strongest control available and it addresses a minority of the risk. Both halves are true.
