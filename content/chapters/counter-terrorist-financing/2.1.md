# FATF and the TF recommendations

The Financial Action Task Force sets the standard the world is measured against. Most of its forty recommendations concern money laundering; a handful deal specifically with terrorist financing, and those are the ones that shape a CFT function.


**2.1.1** What FATF is, and what it is not The Financial Action Task Force is an intergovernmental body that sets standards for combating money laundering, terrorist financing and proliferation financing. It was established in 1989 and extended its mandate to terrorist financing shortly after September 2001. It is not a treaty organisation and it has no power to make law. Its output is a set of forty recommendations, and their force comes from mutual evaluation: countries assess one another against the standard, publish the results, and a poor assessment carries consequences — reputational, and eventually commercial, as correspondent banks reprice or withdraw from jurisdictions on its lists. That mechanism matters to an analyst for one reason. The obligations you actually work under are national law, and national law is written to satisfy FATF. So the recommendations explain why your rules say what they say, and reading them tells you the intent behind a domestic provision that may be stated tersely. The two lists are worth knowing by name. The blacklist — formally the call for action list — and the greylist, jurisdictions under increased monitoring. Neither is a sanctions list and neither prohibits anything. An institution treating a greylisted country as prohibited is over-applying, and Chapter 2.4 explains why that pattern is a recurring problem in this field.


**2.1.2** Recommendation 5 — the offence Recommendation 5 requires countries to criminalise terrorist financing, on the basis of the 1999 International Convention for the Suppression of the Financing of Terrorism. Three features of the required offence matter operationally, and each was introduced in Module 1. It extends to any funds, from any source, lawful or unlawful. A country whose offence covers only criminal proceeds has not implemented the recommendation, and would miss most terrorist financing. It does not require the funds to be linked to a specific terrorist act. Financing an organisation or an individual terrorist is sufficient, whether or not any attack is contemplated. And it does not require the funds to have been used. Collection and provision complete the offence; nothing need have been spent. The recommendation also requires the offence to be a predicate for money laundering — so laundering the proceeds of terrorist financing is itself an offence, which closes a loop that would otherwise leave the movement of already-collected funds unaddressed.


**2.1.3** Recommendation 6 — targeted financial sanctions Recommendation 6 requires countries to implement targeted financial sanctions to comply with United Nations Security Council resolutions on terrorism and terrorist financing. This is the recommendation that connects counter-terrorist financing to sanctions screening, and for most institutions it is where the two disciplines meet in practice. It requires that funds and other assets of designated persons and entities are frozen without delay, and that no funds or assets are made available to them, directly or indirectly. It also requires a country to be able to designate at national level, not merely to implement UN designations — which is why national terrorism lists exist alongside the UN Consolidated List, and why screening the UN list alone is insufficient. Two obligations here are frequently confused and should not be. Freezing concerns assets you already hold. The making-available prohibition concerns anything you might provide. An institution can comply perfectly with the first and breach the second on the same day, by paying a designated party's supplier.


**2.1.4** Recommendations 8, 14, 16 and 32 Four further recommendations shape the operational environment, and an analyst should recognise each by what it produces. Recommendation 8 concerns non-profit organisations, and Chapter 2.4 is devoted to it. Recommendation 14 concerns money or value transfer services — the licensing or registration of remittance businesses, and the requirement that they apply the same obligations as banks. This is the recommendation behind the regulation of hawala and similar systems, covered in Module 3. Recommendation 16, commonly called the travel rule, requires that originator and beneficiary information accompanies a wire transfer along the chain. Without it an intermediary institution cannot screen the parties, and the Module 1 point that identity does the work value does in AML becomes impossible to act on. Recommendation 32 concerns cash couriers — declaration or disclosure systems for currency crossing borders, and the power to stop and restrain. It exists because a substantial share of terrorist financing never enters the banking system at all.


## What to take from this chapter
- FATF sets standards and cannot make law. Its force comes from mutual evaluation and the commercial consequences of a poor assessment
- The obligations you work under are national law written to satisfy FATF, so the recommendations explain the intent behind a terse domestic rule
- The blacklist and greylist are not sanctions lists and prohibit nothing. Treating a greylisted country as prohibited is over-application
- Recommendation 5: the offence covers funds from any source, needs no link to a specific act, and is complete on collection or provision
- Recommendation 6 requires freezing without delay and prohibits making assets available — two distinct obligations, frequently confused
- Countries must be able to designate nationally, so screening the UN list alone is insufficient
- Recommendations 14, 16 and 32 govern remittance businesses, the travel rule and cash couriers respectively.
