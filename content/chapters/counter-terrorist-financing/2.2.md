# UNSCR 1267 and 1373

Two Security Council resolutions govern terrorism designations and they work on opposite principles. One produces a list; the other produces an obligation to make your own. Confusing them is the most common framework error in this field. 2.2.1 1267 — a list maintained centrally Resolution 1267, adopted in 1999 and substantially expanded after 2001, established a designation regime originally directed at the Taliban and Al-Qaida and now covering ISIL as well. The mechanism is centralised. A Security Council committee — the 1267 Committee, composed of all fifteen Council members — maintains a list. A member state proposes a designation with a statement of case, the committee considers it, and if no member objects within the review window the party is added.

The result is the ISIL and Al-Qaida Sanctions List, published by the United Nations and implemented identically in every member state. It carries names, aliases, dates and places of birth, nationalities, addresses and passport numbers, in the same form and with the same variability Module 5 of any screening course would recognise. For a compliance function this is the straightforward regime. There is a list; you screen against it; a match is a match everywhere. The obligation is to implement, not to decide. The Office of the Ombudsperson, established by Resolution 1904 in 2009, handles delisting petitions for this regime. It is worth knowing that it exists and that its mandate covers only 1267 — petitioners under other UN regimes go to a weaker Focal Point mechanism.

2.2.2 1373 — an obligation to designate Resolution 1373, adopted three weeks after the September 2001 attacks, works entirely differently, and this is the part analysts most often get wrong. It produces no list. It obliges every member state to criminalise terrorist financing, freeze the assets of persons who commit or facilitate terrorist acts, and prohibit making funds available to them — but it leaves each state to determine for itself who those persons are. So 1373 is the legal basis for national terrorism lists. The UK's list under its terrorist asset-freezing legislation, the EU's autonomous terrorism list, the US SDGT designations under Executive Order 13224 — all are 1373 implementations, and none of them is a UN list. The consequence is that these lists differ.

A party designated in one jurisdiction may be undesignated in another, and there is no central authority reconciling them. That is not a failure of the system; it is the system working as designed, because 1373 deliberately devolved the judgement. For an institution operating across borders this is the operative fact: you must screen the national lists of every jurisdiction whose law reaches you, and the union of those lists is materially larger than the UN list alone.


**2.2.3** Why the difference matters in practice Three practical consequences, and they explain most of the confusion this topic causes. Coverage. Screening only the UN Consolidated List gives you the 1267 designations and nothing else. Every 1373 designation — which is to say the large majority of terrorism designations worldwide — is national, and appears only on national lists. Divergence is normal. When an analyst finds a party listed by one authority and not another, the instinct is to assume a data error. Under 1373 it is frequently correct: two states genuinely reached different conclusions. Delisting routes differ completely. A 1267 designation is challenged through the Ombudsperson. A 1373 designation is challenged in the domestic courts of the designating state, under that state's administrative law. There is no common procedure and no common standard of evidence. One further point worth holding: 1373 obligations are binding on all member states under Chapter VII, exactly as 1267 obligations are. The absence of a central list does not make the obligation weaker — it makes it wider. ## Scenario — LISTED IN ONE PLACE, CLEAN IN ANOTHER

A payment involves a counterparty who appears on the US SDGT list under Executive Order 13224. He does not appear on the UN Consolidated List, the EU terrorism list, or the UK Sanctions List. Your institution is European, the payment is in euro between two European parties, and an analyst records: not a UN designation, therefore not a terrorism concern, released. **Question.** What is wrong with that reasoning? **Answer.** The conclusion may be right and the reasoning is not. SDGT is a 1373 designation — a national judgement by the United States — and its absence from the UN list means only that the 1267 Committee has not designated him, which tells you nothing about whether he is a terrorism concern. The correct analysis asks two different questions.

First, does US law reach this payment: if any part of it touches a US person, US-origin element or dollar clearing, the SDGT designation binds directly. Second, and separately, does the fact that another state has assessed this party as involved in terrorism warrant enquiry under your own institution's risk appetite, even where no law compels action. The rationale should record which of those was asked. 'Not a UN designation' answers neither.


## What to take from this chapter
- 1267 produces a centrally maintained UN list, implemented identically everywhere. Screen it and a match is a match
- 1373 produces no list at all — it obliges each state to designate for itself, and is the basis for every national terrorism list
- Most terrorism designations worldwide are 1373 designations, so the UN list alone covers a minority of them
- Divergence between national lists is the system working as designed, not a data error
- 1267 delisting goes to the Ombudsperson; 1373 delisting goes to the domestic courts of the designating state
- Both are binding under Chapter VII — the absence of a central list makes the obligation wider, not weaker
- 'Not on the UN list' is not a finding. It answers one question out of several.
