2 Counter-Terrorist Financing — Study Guide Prepared as the course material for the Certified Counter-Terrorist Financing Analyst credential. Accredited by the accreditor. First edition, 2026. This guide is educational material. It is not legal advice, and it does not state the law of any jurisdiction as it applies to any particular institution or transaction. Terrorism designations are made and revoked continually, national lists diverge from one another by design, and the obligations described here are given effect by domestic legislation that differs in detail from country to country — nothing here should be relied on in place of the current text of the applicable measure, your institution’s own procedures, or professional advice. All names, organisations, charities, remittance businesses, transactions and corridors used in examples and scenarios are invented. Where an actual event is referred to — an attack, an estimate of what it cost, a published evaluation or a regulatory statement — it is a matter of public record and is identified as such. 3 Who this guide is for This guide is written for the person who will have to decide whether something in front of them is terrorist financing. It assumes no prior knowledge of the subject, no legal training, and no experience of financial services beyond a general familiarity with what a bank does. That covers a wider group than the job title suggests, because very few institutions employ anybody whose only work is counter-terrorist financing. If you already work in anti-money laundering You will know the infrastructure — customer due diligence, monitoring, alerts, reporting — and you will find that the infrastructure is largely the same. What is different is nearly everything you have learned to ask of it. Laundering makes dirty money look clean; terrorist financing sends clean money to a dirty purpose. The direction is reversed, and with it the value of almost every instinct that makes a good AML analyst. A clean source of funds, a small amount and a simple transaction are not reassuring here; they are entirely consistent with the thing you are looking for. Module 1 sets out why an AML function working exactly as designed will pass a terrorist financing case, and it is the chapter that matters most for readers arriving from this direction. If you work in sanctions screening Screening is the most productive counter-terrorist financing control an institution has, and Module 5 explains why: it is the only control in the set that produces a fact rather than an inference. If you work alerts, you are already doing CFT work whether or not it is described that way. What this guide adds is what the hit means and what surrounds it — why a terrorism entry carries more alias data than an ordinary sanctions entry and what that does to your precision, why the UN list on its own covers a minority of designations worldwide, and what the control cannot see at all. If you are moving into compliance Counter-terrorist financing is a durable specialism inside a growing discipline, and it does not require a prior qualification. It is also unusually demanding for an entry-level subject, because the reasoning matters more than the procedure — there is no checklist that produces the right answer, and several of the right answers are counter-intuitive. If that is your position, read the guide in order and do not skip Module 1. The temptation is to go straight to Module 4 because detection is the visible part of the job; the reason Module 4 makes sense is that Modules 1 to 3 explain what you are trying to detect and why it is so faint. What you need before you start Nothing formal. No prior certification, no degree requirement, no minimum experience. English at a professional reading level, and the willingness to work the scenarios rather than skim them. 4 If you have never worked in a bank, two pieces of vocabulary will help throughout. A payment has an originator who sends it and a beneficiary who receives it, and between them sit one or more correspondent banks that move the money. And a designation is an official decision naming a person or organisation, which brings legal consequences with it — freezing, and a prohibition on making anything available to them. Almost every example in this guide is a variation on one or the other. 5 The work this prepares you for It is worth being concrete about the work, because counter-terrorist financing is usually described either as intelligence work, which it is not, or as a subset of anti-money laundering, which it is not either. What an analyst actually does Very little of this work arrives labelled. In most institutions there is no queue called terrorist financing. What there is instead is a sanctions screening alert against a terrorism list, a monitoring alert raised for some other reason, a request for information from another institution, an approach from law enforcement, or a colleague who has noticed something and does not know what to do with it. The analyst establishes what is actually known, what the pattern is across time and accounts rather than in a single transaction, whether the indicators present are genuinely independent of one another or the same observation restated, and what should be reported. The outcome is one of four: report, escalate, seek more information, or record that nothing has been established. That last one is a real outcome and this guide treats it as such. The volumes are not the point here, and that is the first difference from AML. Terrorist financing is rare. An analyst may work an entire career without seeing a confirmed case, which makes the discipline of the work matter more than its throughput — because the value of what you produce is not visible to you at the time you produce it. Why it is harder than it sounds Because the transaction tells you almost nothing. A four-hundred-euro transfer from a salaried person to a relative abroad is indistinguishable from a four-hundred-euro transfer from a salaried person to a relative abroad. There is no technique to spot, no structuring pattern, no unexplained wealth. The case is in the counterparty, the sequence and what is known elsewhere. Because the base rate is brutal. Terrorist financing is so rare that even a genuinely good indicator produces overwhelmingly false positives, and Module 4 works through why that is arithmetic rather than a defect in the indicator. Because the obvious response is frequently the wrong one. Excluding a category of customer, declining a corridor, exiting the money service business sector — each feels like the careful choice, and each moves the activity somewhere nobody can see it while harming people who have done nothing. Module 5 sets out what the documented consequences have actually been. And because you are working with a fragment. Your institution sees its own customers, their financial connections and a retention window. It does not see the other banks, the cash, or the intent. What you produce goes to somebody holding the rest, which is why the quality of what you write is the whole of your contribution. Where it leads Counter-terrorist financing sits alongside sanctions and financial crime investigations, and analysts move between them readily — the reasoning transfers in a way the procedures do not. From there the routes run into financial crime advisory work, into the financial intelligence units 6 of regulators and law enforcement, into policy roles, and into the risk assessment and programme design work that decides what the controls are in the first place. The subject also travels further than banking. Money service businesses, payment firms, crypto-asset service providers, charities and non-profits, and the supervisors of all of them face the same questions — and several of them face those questions with considerably less support than a bank has. 7 Foreword Counter-terrorist financing is the part of financial crime compliance where the ordinary instincts of the discipline are least reliable, and where the consequences of applying them anyway fall hardest on people who have done nothing wrong. That is an uncomfortable way to open a study guide, and it is the reason this one is written the way it is. There is no shortage of training material that lists indicators and leaves the reader to apply them. Applied without the reasoning underneath, several of those indicators are demographic descriptions wearing analytical clothing, and using them produces exactly two results: people are harmed, and detection gets worse. This guide spends a good deal of its length on why that is so. Three themes run through it and are worth stating at the outset. The first is that anti-money laundering instincts do not transfer. Laundering conceals the origin of money that is already dirty; terrorist financing directs money that is frequently clean towards a purpose that is not. Every question AML monitoring is built to ask — where did this come from, why is it structured this way, why is the value disproportionate — returns a reassuring answer in a terrorist financing case. A system that passes such a case may be working exactly as specified. That is a design limit, and knowing it is part of operating the control. The second is that over-application is a failure, not a form of caution. FATF has said so plainly about the charitable sector, and the same reasoning applies to remittance corridors, money service businesses and whole communities. Wholesale exit does not reduce terrorist financing; it relocates the activity to channels with no oversight and destroys the institution’s ability to see any of it. The careful response and the fair response are the same response, and an institution that treats them as competing loses both. The third is that you are supplying a fragment. No institution sees a terrorist financing case whole. What you write goes to somebody assembling it from many sources, and it is useful only in proportion to how clearly it separates what you observed from what you inferred, and how completely it carries the identifiers that let your fragment be matched to theirs. In this field the quality of a report matters more than the volume of reports, and Module 5 is specific about what makes the difference. Where something is genuinely contested — the effectiveness of the measures, the reach of Recommendation 8, whether particular indicators earn their place — the guide says so rather than presenting one side as settled. Work through the modules in order. Each assumes the one before it. 8 How to use this guide The structure is consistent throughout, and knowing what each element is for will make the material easier to work with. Modules, chapters and topics There are five modules. Each module holds four chapters, and each chapter holds numbered topics. A module is a subject; a chapter is something you sit down to read in one go; a topic is a single idea within it. Cross-references use the full number. A reference to Chapter 4.2 means Module 4, Chapter 2. A reference to 4.2.3 means the third topic within it. Chapters begin on a fresh page, so you can stop at the end of one without losing your place. Scenarios Most chapters close with a scenario: a situation, a question, and an answer. They are set on a shaded ground so you can find them. Read the situation and the question, and decide what you would do before reading the answer. There are thirteen of them. They are built around the mistakes analysts actually make rather than around textbook cases, and several have answers that are not what the setup suggests — which is the point of including them. At least two are designed to be failed on first reading. What to take from this chapter Each chapter ends with a summary panel. It is a revision aid and it is not a substitute for the chapter: several points in it will not make sense without the reasoning that produced them, and an assessment question can be answered from the reasoning but not from the summary alone.
