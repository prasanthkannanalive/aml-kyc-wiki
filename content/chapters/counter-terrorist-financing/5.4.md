# De-risking, and the cost of getting this wrong

The last chapter of this guide is about the response that feels safest and does the most harm. It closes the argument Module 2 opened with charities and Module 4 made with indicators, and it is the thing a holder of this credential should carry out of it. 5.4.1 What de-risking is De-risking is the wholesale exit from a category of customer or a class of business, rather than the management of individual relationships according to their individual risk. The categories affected are consistent worldwide and have been documented for a decade: money service businesses and remittance providers, charities and humanitarian organisations, correspondent banking relationships with institutions in developing countries, and customers connected to particular nationalities, regions or communities.

The reasoning inside an institution is always the same and always sounds prudent. The category carries elevated risk; the revenue is modest; the cost of getting one case wrong is enormous; therefore exit is the rational choice. Each step of that is defensible in isolation and the conclusion is wrong, for reasons that are now well evidenced rather than contested. FATF has stated that de-risking is not consistent with a risk-based approach and that wholesale exit is a failure of implementation rather than an application of it. The Financial Stability Board, the World Bank and successive United Nations bodies have documented the same conclusion from different directions.

5.4.2 Why it fails on its own terms Set aside the harm to customers for a moment and take the institution's own objective — reducing exposure to terrorist financing. De-risking does not achieve it. The activity continues. A remittance corridor does not close because a bank withdraws; it moves to a provider with weaker controls, to an unregistered operator, or to cash carried by hand. Visibility is lost. The account you closed was producing screened, monitored, recorded transactions. What replaces it produces none. The institution has not reduced terrorist financing; it has stopped being able to see a portion of it. Concentration increases. As institutions exit, the remaining providers absorb the displaced volume, and risk concentrates in fewer and frequently less capable hands.

And the institution's own understanding degrades. An institution with no charitable customers and no money service business relationships has no data about those sectors, no analysts experienced in them, and no basis for the risk assessment it is required to maintain. So the trade is not safety for revenue. It is the appearance of safety for the loss of both visibility and capability. 5.4.3 The human cost It should be stated directly, because a guide that treated this only as a technical problem would be dishonest about what is at stake. Humanitarian organisations have been unable to pay staff in conflict zones, delayed medical procurement, and suspended programmes for want of a payment channel. These are documented outcomes reported to FATF and to United Nations bodies, not hypotheticals.

Individuals have lost bank accounts with no explanation and no appeal, on grounds that describe their community rather than their conduct. For a person supporting family abroad, the practical effect is being unable to send money to a parent. Small businesses in affected sectors have been unable to obtain banking at all. And whole countries have lost correspondent relationships, cutting their financial systems off from the international one — which raises the cost of every remittance into them and pushes more volume into channels nobody observes. Every one of those outcomes was produced by people who believed they were being careful. That is what makes de-risking worth a chapter: it is not a failure of intention. 5.4.4 What to do instead The alternative is not to ignore risk.

It is to manage it at the level of the relationship rather than the category. Assess individually. A registered money service business with known correspondents and consistent volumes is a different proposition from an unregistered operator with opaque settlement, and treating them alike is the error. Apply enhanced diligence rather than exit. More questions, more frequent review, more documentation, tighter monitoring — all of which keep the activity visible while addressing the risk. Require what you actually need. Registration status, settlement counterparties, corridor consistency, screening of the parties. Not evidence that the customer's community is unlike its reputation. Record the decision either way.

A file showing what was asked and what was found supports keeping the relationship and evidences the risk-based approach the regulator expects. And treat category-level exit as a decision requiring justification, not a default. If your institution is exiting a class of customer, the question 'what happens to this activity after we exit' should have an answer, and the answer should not be 'it becomes invisible'. That is the whole of the argument this guide has been making, in one sentence: the careful response and the fair one are the same response, and the institution that separates them loses both.


## Scenario — THE PRUDENT DECISION

A bank reviews its exposure and identifies eleven money service business customers and four registered charities operating in conflict-affected regions. Combined revenue is modest. Compliance costs are material. A paper recommends exiting all fifteen relationships within ninety days, noting that peer institutions have done the same and that the residual risk after exit will be nil. **Question.** Is the residual risk after exit nil? **Answer.** No, and that sentence is the flaw in the paper. The institution's exposure through those accounts becomes nil; the terrorist financing risk does not change at all, because the flows continue through whoever absorbs them.

What the bank has actually purchased is the loss of visibility into fifteen relationships it was monitoring, the loss of any data about two sectors it is required to risk-assess, and a contribution to the concentration that makes those sectors harder for anyone to serve. It has also, in the charities' case, potentially interrupted humanitarian delivery — an outcome the paper does not cost because it falls outside the institution. The observation that peers have done the same is not support; it is a description of how a systemic harm accumulates from individually rational decisions. The defensible version of this paper assesses each of the fifteen, exits any that fail on their own facts, and states plainly what the institution will no longer be able to see for each one it closes.
