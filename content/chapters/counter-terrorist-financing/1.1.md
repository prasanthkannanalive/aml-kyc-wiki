Why TF is not money laundering The two are named together so often that they are assumed to be one subject. They are not, and an analyst who applies laundering logic to a terrorist financing case will look for the wrong things in the wrong order. This chapter sets out the differences that matter operationally. 1.1.1  The direction of travel Money laundering takes criminal proceeds and makes them appear legitimate. The money is dirty at the start and clean at the end, and every step in between exists to break the link between the funds and the crime that produced them. Terrorist financing does the reverse. It takes funds — often entirely lawful ones — and moves them to an unlawful purpose. The money may be clean at the start and remains, in a forensic sense, unremarkable throughout. Nothing about a salary, a donation or a small business's takings changes when the recipient intends to buy explosives with them. This inversion is the source of almost every practical difficulty in the field. Laundering leaves a trail because the launderer must do something to the money: layer it, move it through structures, convert it, bring it back. Each of those acts is a deviation from ordinary financial behaviour and each is potentially detectable. A terrorist financier frequently does none of that. They may simply transfer a modest sum to a relative abroad, or withdraw cash over several weeks, or buy a prepaid card. There is no layering to detect because there is nothing to hide about the money's origin — only about its destination, and destination is not something a transaction record states. 1.1.2  Predicate offence and the problem it creates Money laundering requires a predicate offence. Something criminal generated the funds — fraud, trafficking, corruption, tax evasion — and the laundering is a second offence layered on the first. An investigator working backwards from suspicious funds is looking for a crime that has already happened. Terrorist financing has no predicate requirement. The offence is complete when funds are provided or collected with the intention or knowledge that they will be used for terrorism. Nothing need have happened yet. Often nothing has: the whole point of financing is that it precedes the act. 12 That single legal difference reshapes the analyst's work. In an AML case you are reconstructing a past event from its financial residue. In a TF case you may be looking at the financial footprint of an event that has not occurred, committed by people who have not yet done anything else unlawful. It also explains why TF intelligence is so heavily dependent on information the financial institution does not hold. Whether a transfer is terrorist financing frequently turns on who the recipient is and what they intend — facts that live with law enforcement and intelligence services, not in a payment message. 1.1.3  What the two offences actually prohibit The international definition of terrorist financing derives from the 1999 International Convention for the Suppression of the Financing of Terrorism and is carried into FATF Recommendation 5. It covers the wilful provision or collection of funds, by any means, directly or indirectly, with the unlawful intention that they should be used, or in the knowledge that they are to be used, to carry out a terrorist act, or by a terrorist organisation, or by an individual terrorist. Read that carefully, because three phrases in it do real work. By any means, directly or indirectly. The offence is not limited to bank transfers. Cash, goods, cryptocurrency, prepaid value, in-kind support and services all count. An analyst who thinks only in payment records has already narrowed the field. Or by a terrorist organisation, or by an individual terrorist. Funds need not be tied to any specific plot. Providing a designated organisation with money for its ordinary running costs — salaries, rent, vehicles — is terrorist financing even where nothing violent is contemplated by the transaction itself. Wilful, with intention or knowledge. This is the mental element, and it is why financial institutions cannot determine terrorist financing on their own. You can see a payment. You cannot see intent. What a compliance function does is identify indicators that warrant reporting, and the determination happens elsewhere. 1.1.4  Why the distinction changes what you do Four practical consequences follow, and they are the reason this chapter opens the guide. You cannot rely on the source of funds. In AML, an unexplained source is the beginning of suspicion. In TF, a fully explained and entirely lawful source proves nothing at all. You cannot rely on value. Laundering is proportionate to the crime that generated the money, so significant laundering involves significant sums. Terrorist financing is proportionate to the operation being funded, and operations can be very cheap. Chapter 1.3 covers this. You cannot rely on complexity. Layering is a laundering behaviour. Its absence is not reassurance in a TF context; the simplest possible transaction is entirely consistent with financing. And you should not expect a single decisive transaction. TF frequently appears as a pattern across accounts, over time, in amounts that individually look like nothing — which is precisely what Module 4 is about. 13 SCENARIO — THE SALARY THAT FUNDED AN ATTACK A customer of eleven years receives the same salary each month from the same employer, a legitimate engineering firm. Over four months he transfers €600, €450, €700 and €500 to an individual in a neighbouring country, described in the reference as family support. Every payment is funded from salary. There is no unexplained income, no cash, no structuring, no third-party credit. Question.  An AML analyst reviewing this file finds nothing. Are they wrong? Answer.  Not by AML standards, and that is the point. There is no predicate offence, no unexplained source, no layering and no unusual value — every AML question this file could be asked returns a clean answer. If the recipient is a facilitator for a proscribed organisation, this is terrorist financing throughout, and none of the money was ever criminal. The only things that would surface it are the identity of the recipient, which requires screening against designations, and the pattern of regular modest transfers to a jurisdiction of concern, which requires monitoring built for TF rather than for laundering. An AML function looking for dirty money will look at this file and correctly conclude that the money is clean. WHAT TO TAKE FROM THIS CHAPTER — Laundering makes dirty money look clean. Terrorist financing sends clean money to a dirty purpose. The direction is reversed. — TF has no predicate offence: the offence is complete on provision or collection with intent, before anything else happens. — The definition covers any means, direct or indirect — cash, goods, services and in-kind support, not only payments. — Funding an organisation's ordinary running costs is TF even where no specific attack is contemplated. — Intent is the legal element, and a financial institution cannot see it.
