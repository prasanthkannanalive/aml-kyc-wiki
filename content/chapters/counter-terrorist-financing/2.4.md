# Recommendation 8 and the non-profit problem

This chapter is about a recommendation that has caused more damage through over-application than through neglect, and about why an analyst should treat charitable sector risk with more care than almost anything else in this guide.


**2.4.1** What Recommendation 8 actually says Recommendation 8 concerns non-profit organisations, and its current wording is the result of a deliberate correction. The original formulation described the non-profit sector as particularly vulnerable to terrorist financing abuse, and was widely read as licensing blanket suspicion of charities. In practice it produced exactly that: account closures across the sector, refusal of remittance services to humanitarian organisations, and severe difficulty for aid agencies operating in conflict zones — the places where humanitarian need is greatest and where a bank account is hardest to keep. FATF revised the recommendation in response. It now requires countries to review the adequacy of their laws relating to non-profits, and to apply focused, proportionate and risk-based measures to the subset of organisations identified as at risk — not to the sector as a whole. The interpretive note is emphatic on this point, and FATF has said publicly that the recommendation does not require blanket de-risking of the sector and that over-application is itself a failure of implementation. That is an unusual thing for a standard-setter to say about its own standard, and it is worth knowing that it was said.


**2.4.2** Where the genuine risk sits None of that means the risk is imaginary. The diversion of charitable funds is a documented terrorist financing method and an analyst should understand its shape. Diversion at the point of distribution is the most common form. Funds are raised legitimately, from donors acting in good faith, and are diverted after collection — by a compromised local partner, an intermediary in the delivery chain, or an individual within the organisation. Abuse of an organisation's name and standing. A entity registered as a charity, or presenting itself as one, whose actual purpose is collection. The donors may still be genuine and the deception may be aimed entirely at them. Provision of logistical cover. Not money at all: the movement of people, goods or communications under humanitarian auspices. The pattern common to all three is that the risk concentrates in the delivery chain and in specific organisations, not in charitable activity as such. That is precisely what the revised recommendation says, and it is why proportionate measures target the chain rather than the sector.


**2.4.3** The cost of over-application This is worth stating plainly because it is the reason the recommendation was rewritten. When banks withdraw services from humanitarian organisations, the money does not stop moving. It moves through channels with less oversight — cash carried by hand, informal transfer 32 systems, third-party intermediaries — which is the opposite of the intended effect. A charity that cannot use a bank does not stop operating; it becomes harder to monitor. The humanitarian cost is direct and documented. Aid agencies have reported inability to pay staff in conflict zones, delayed procurement of medical supplies, and programmes suspended for want of a payment channel. Successive reports by the Financial Action Task Force itself, by the Financial Stability Board and by United Nations bodies have identified this as a systemic problem requiring correction. And there is an intelligence cost that analysts under-weight. A charity operating through the banking system is visible. Its payments are screened, its counterparties are known, its patterns are monitorable. Push it out and that visibility is lost, along with any prospect of detecting the diversion the exclusion was meant to prevent. So the over-cautious position is not the safe one. It is a decision with a body count and a monitoring cost, taken by people who believed they were avoiding risk.


**2.4.4** What proportionate looks like For an analyst reviewing a non-profit customer or a payment involving one, the practical questions are these. Is this organisation registered, and with whom. Registration status is checkable and its absence is a genuine question rather than an answer. Does the payment fit the organisation's stated activity and its usual pattern. A registered medical charity paying a medical supplier is unremarkable. The same charity making regular personal transfers to individuals is a question. Where is the money going, and does the delivery chain make sense. This is where the genuine risk sits, so this is where the enquiry belongs. Who are the counterparties, and do any of them appear on a terrorism designation. Screening does the work here that it does everywhere in CFT. And what is explicitly not a basis for suspicion: that the organisation is faith-based, that it operates in a particular region, that it serves a particular community, or that it sends money to a jurisdiction with a large diaspora. Those describe most of the charitable sector. An indicator that would flag a substantial share of legitimate organisations is not an indicator; Module 4 makes this argument at length and it applies here first.


## Scenario — THE MEDICAL CHARITY

A registered medical charity holds an account with your institution. It makes quarterly payments to suppliers in a conflict-affected country, and monthly payments of modest amounts to eleven named individuals in the same country. A relationship manager proposes exiting the relationship on the basis that the jurisdiction is high-risk and the payments to individuals are unexplained. **Question.** Is exit the right response? **Answer.** Not on this evidence, and the proposal contains an error worth naming. Payments to named individuals in a conflict-affected country are entirely consistent with paying field staff — which is what a medical charity operating there would necessarily do, and which is also one of the first things that becomes impossible when a bank withdraws.

The correct step is enquiry, not exit: ask whether the eleven individuals are employees, whether payroll records exist, whether the amounts correspond to salaries, and whether any of the counterparties appear on a terrorism designation. If the answers come back consistent, the file records a proportionate enquiry that found a legitimate explanation. If they do not, you have a real question. What is not defensible is exiting a registered humanitarian organisation because its work is in a difficult place — that is the over-application FATF revised the recommendation to stop, and it pushes the same payments into channels nobody can see.

- Recommendation 8 was rewritten because its original wording produced blanket de-risking of the charitable sector
