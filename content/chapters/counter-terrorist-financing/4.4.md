What indicators cannot do This chapter is the reason the credential is worth holding. Terrorist financing indicators are weak, generic, and drawn from characteristics shared by millions of people who have done nothing wrong. Applied without discipline they produce discriminatory outcomes and worse intelligence at the same time — and an analyst who does not understand that will cause harm while believing they are being thorough. 4.4.1  The base rate problem Start with arithmetic, because the arithmetic is not intuitive and it governs everything else in this chapter. Terrorist financing is extraordinarily rare. Against the population of an ordinary retail bank, the proportion of customers involved in it is a very small fraction of one per cent — vanishingly small compared with, say, the proportion who send remittances abroad. Now suppose an indicator that is right nine times out of ten: it fires on ninety per cent of genuine cases and on only ten per cent of innocent ones. That sounds like a good indicator. Apply it to a million customers among whom ten are involved, and it will identify nine of the ten — and roughly a hundred thousand innocent people. The overwhelming majority of everything it surfaces is wrong, and it is wrong by a factor of about ten thousand to one. This is not a flaw in the indicator. It is what rarity does to any test, and no amount of tuning escapes it. It means three things for practice: that almost every alert you see is a false positive and you should expect that; that an indicator which fires on a large population is worthless however sensible it sounds; and that combining indicators only helps when they are independent, because correlated indicators surface the same population twice. 4.4.2  Indicators that are really demographics Some commonly cited red flags describe populations rather than behaviour, and they should be named. Sending remittances to a high-risk jurisdiction. This describes tens of millions of people — migrant workers, diaspora communities, families separated by borders. It is the ordinary financial behaviour of a substantial share of the world. Frequent small transfers abroad. The shape of every remittance corridor in existence. Donations to religious or faith-based charities. The shape of ordinary charitable giving in most of the world's religious traditions. Use of a money service business. Covered in Module 3 — often the cheapest or only available channel, and the reason people use it is cost and access. Cash-intensive activity. The normal behaviour of many small businesses and of anyone in a cash economy. Nationality, place of birth, name, or travel to a region. These are not financial behaviours at all. 53 Each of these will appear in a genuine terrorist financing case, because terrorist financiers are also people who send remittances and give to charity. That is not what makes them indicators — it is what makes them useless as indicators, because so does everybody else. 4.4.3  The double harm Applying demographic indicators does two kinds of damage, and it is important that both are understood, because the second is what persuades institutions that the first is not a price worth paying. The harm to people is direct and documented. Account closures, refused transactions, remittances blocked, and businesses unable to bank — falling disproportionately on migrant communities, Muslim customers, charitable organisations and money service businesses. For an individual this means being unable to send money to a parent, or losing a bank account with no explanation and no appeal, because an institution acted on a pattern that describes their entire community. This is not hypothetical: it has been documented repeatedly by FATF itself, by the Financial Stability Board, by the World Bank and by human rights bodies. The harm to intelligence is less discussed and just as real. An alert queue full of demographic false positives has three effects. It buries genuine signals in noise — the case that mattered is somewhere in the hundred thousand. It exhausts analyst attention on files that could never have been anything. And it pushes the excluded activity out of the observable system entirely, into cash and informal channels where nobody can see it at all. So the choice is not between being careful and being fair. Demographic indicators are worse on both counts simultaneously — they harm people and they degrade detection. An institution using them is not trading accuracy for safety; it is losing both. 4.4.4  What to do instead Five disciplines, and they are the practical content of this chapter. Ask whether the indicator would flag a large population. If the honest answer is yes, it is not an indicator. Test it by completing the sentence: 'this is unusual because…' — if the completion describes a demographic rather than a behaviour, stop. Prefer behaviour to identity. What the customer did is evidence. Who the customer is, is not. Require independence before combining. Four descriptions of the same fact are one fact, and stacking them produces confidence rather than evidence. Ask before concluding. A question to the customer generates information that no amount of staring at the file will produce, and a refused or shifting explanation is a genuine indicator where the underlying activity was not. And write down what you did not establish. A file recording 'no basis for suspicion identified; enquiry made on X, explanation consistent' is a better record than one listing four demographic observations, and it protects the customer and the institution simultaneously. One further point that belongs here. If your institution's CFT escalation volume is concentrated in one nationality, one corridor or one community, that is a finding about the institution's indicators rather than about that community — and it is worth raising as such. 54 SCENARIO — THE QUEUE THAT TOLD YOU ABOUT ITSELF A quality review of six months of CFT escalations finds that 84 of 91 concerned customers of one nationality, and that 79 cited 'transfers to a high-risk jurisdiction' as a primary ground. None resulted in any action by the financial intelligence unit. A senior manager proposes tightening the corridor rule to reduce volume. Question.  Is that the right response? Answer.  No, and the proposal misreads the finding. The problem is not the volume; it is that the indicator is a demographic and reducing its sensitivity produces fewer wrong answers of exactly the same kind. Ninety-one escalations, none acted upon, concentrated on one nationality, is a description of a control that identifies a community rather than a behaviour — and it has been generating discriminatory outcomes and no intelligence for six months. The right response is to remove corridor and nationality as primary grounds altogether, rebuild around behaviour and sequence, and treat the concentration itself as a reportable control finding rather than a workload problem. Worth noting what the review got right: measuring escalation composition against outcomes is exactly how this becomes visible, and an institution that never looks at that will never know.
