Reporting a suspicion A terrorist financing report goes to people who hold information you do not. This chapter covers what makes a report usable, what makes one worthless, and the obligation that sits behind it. 5.2.1  The obligation, and how it differs Every jurisdiction requires a report where there are reasonable grounds to suspect terrorist financing. Three features distinguish it from ordinary suspicious activity reporting. The threshold is lower in effect, even where the words are the same. Because the consequence is severe and the financial signal is weak, an institution should report on materially less than it would need for a laundering suspicion. Waiting for a case that satisfies an AML instinct means never reporting. There is no de minimis. Module 1 made this point and it belongs here operationally: a two-hundred-unit transaction is reportable on the same grounds as a two-million one. And speed matters more. A laundering report concerns money that has already been earned by a crime already committed. A terrorist financing report may concern an act that has not happened, and its value decays quickly. Several jurisdictions also require reporting of attempted transactions, not only completed ones — a payment you refused is still information, and in a TF context frequently more informative than one you processed. 5.2.2  What a usable report contains The receiving unit holds what you do not: intelligence, law enforcement records, other institutions' reports. Your report is a fragment they will try to fit against the rest, and its value depends on how well it can be fitted. Identifiers, in full. Names as held, dates of birth, addresses, account numbers, identification documents. This is the joining material — the thing that lets your fragment be matched to theirs — and it is the part most often thinned out. The counterparties, with the same completeness. Who received the money, where, and through which institution. The activity, stated factually and in sequence. Dates, amounts, channels, in the order they occurred. A chronology is more usable than a summary. What you observed that caused the suspicion, separated from what you infer from it. The receiving unit needs to be able to tell your facts from your reading of them. What you asked and what the customer said. An explanation that changed, or a question refused, is evidence, and it is evidence the receiving unit cannot obtain. And what you could not establish. A report saying which enquiries were made and which came back inconclusive is more useful than one that presents only what was found. 5.2.3  What makes a report worthless Four failures, and the first is the one Module 4 was written to prevent. 59 Grounds that are demographic. A report whose stated basis is a corridor, a nationality or a remittance pattern gives the receiving unit nothing to work with and contributes to a body of reports that is itself discriminatory. Financial intelligence units have said publicly that defensive reporting on generic grounds is a significant problem, and it is worth understanding that a bad report is not neutral — it consumes analytical capacity that the useful reports need. Conclusions without the facts underneath them. 'Behaviour consistent with terrorist financing' is an assertion. The reader cannot assess it or match it against anything. Thin identifiers. A report naming a counterparty with no date of birth, no address and no account number may be unmatchable, which makes it unusable however good the underlying suspicion was. And volume as a substitute for judgement. An institution reporting everything reports nothing, because the signal it does hold is indistinguishable from the noise it also filed. 5.2.4  After the report Filing is not the end of the institution's involvement, and analysts frequently assume it is. The account decision is separate from the report. Reporting does not oblige you to close, freeze or restrict anything, and in many cases continuing the relationship under monitoring is what the authorities would prefer — a closed account stops producing information. Some jurisdictions have a consent or no-consent regime for proceeding with a specific transaction; know which applies to you. Monitoring should intensify rather than stop. The pattern that produced the report is likely to continue, and its continuation is itself reportable. Further reports may be required. New activity by the same customer generally warrants a new report rather than reliance on the first. And the file must be retained and readable. Module 4 made this point as a detection control: the next analyst sees only what you wrote. It is also a regulatory one, and the retention period is usually longer than people assume. Report A gives the receiving unit nothing it can act on and one thing it should not have been given: nationality as a ground. It cannot be matched to anything, because it names nobody and dates nothing, and its stated basis describes several million people. Report B is usable in a specific way — the named recipient with identifiers can be checked against records the institution cannot see, the chronology can be compared against other reports on the same recipient, and the changed explanation is evidence obtainable only by the institution that asked. Note also what Report B does that analysts often omit: it records that the designation check came back clean, and states plainly what could not be established. That is not weakness in the report. It tells the reader exactly how far the institution got, which is what allows them to judge how much further they need to go.
