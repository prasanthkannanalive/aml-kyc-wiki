# Network indicators and the small cell

Terrorist financing frequently involves several people whose individual activity is unremarkable. This chapter covers what network analysis contributes, why the small-cell problem defeats most of it, and where the boundaries of a financial institution's view actually lie.


**4.3.1** What network analysis can find Where a group of accounts is connected, some patterns become visible that no single account shows. Common counterparties. Several unconnected customers transferring to the same individual abroad, particularly where the amounts and timing are similar. This is the most productive network pattern in practice. Funnel behaviour. Multiple people depositing into one account, from which funds leave in consolidated form. Shared with AML mule typologies. Shared identifiers. Accounts sharing an address, a telephone number, a device or an email domain, where no relationship is declared. Sequenced activity. Several accounts performing the same sequence at the same time — a set of withdrawals in one week, a set of transfers the next. Each of these requires the institution to be able to see across accounts, which is an infrastructure question rather than an analytical one. An institution whose monitoring is account-by-account cannot perform any of it, whatever its analysts know.


**4.3.2** Why the small cell defeats it The difficulty is that much terrorist financing does not involve a network at all. A self-funded individual planning an attack alone has one account, ordinary income, and a handful of small transactions. There is no network to analyse because there is no network. Several of the most serious attacks in Europe over the past decade were financed this way, at costs of a few hundred euros. A cell of three or four people may share no financial relationship whatever. They may each fund their own part from their own income, meeting only in the physical world. Nothing connects the accounts because nothing needs to. And where a connection does exist, it is frequently one hop and entirely ordinary — a transfer between two people who genuinely know each other, which describes most transfers between two people. So network analysis is a real capability that addresses a minority of cases. An institution should have it and should not believe it covers the risk.


**4.3.3** Where the institution's view ends Being precise about this is what stops an institution overstating its own control, and it is the same discipline the sanctions module applies to screening. You see your own customers. You do not see accounts at other institutions, so a cell banking across four banks is invisible to each. You see financial connections. You do not see the physical, familial, religious or associational relationships that actually constitute a network, and those are what law enforcement holds. You see transactions, not intent. Two people sending money to the same recipient may be brothers supporting a parent. And you see the period you retain data for and can query efficiently, which in practice is frequently shorter than the period over which a pattern develops. The consequence is that the institution supplies a fragment. That is not a failure — it is the correct description of the role, and it is why the quality of what you report matters more than the volume.


## Scenario — THREE CUSTOMERS, ONE RECIPIENT

Three customers with no declared relationship each transfer between €300 and €500 monthly to the same named individual in the same country. The three live in different cities, bank at different branches, and have unrelated occupations. Their transfers fall in the same week each month. **Question.** Is this a network finding? **Answer.** It is a genuine one, and it is worth understanding what makes it different from the escalation in Chapter 4.2. The common recipient is a single fact that all three files share and none of them explains — it is not a description of a demographic, it is a specific individual receiving money from unconnected people in a coordinated rhythm. The timing correlation strengthens it, because coincidence does not usually keep a schedule.

What this does not establish is what the money is for: three brothers supporting a parent, three employees paying an agent, or three contributors to a fund would all look like this. So the escalation states the connection, the timing and the absence of a declared relationship, and asks for the recipient to be checked against designations and adverse media — which is a question the institution cannot answer and somebody else can. That is the correct shape of a network escalation: a specific, non-demographic connection, handed to whoever holds the missing half.


## What to take from this chapter
- Common counterparties across unconnected customers is the most productive network pattern in practice
- Funnel behaviour, shared identifiers and sequenced activity across accounts are the others worth knowing
- All of it requires cross-account visibility, which is infrastructure rather than analysis
- Much terrorist financing involves no network at all — a self-funded individual has nothing to connect
- A cell may share no financial relationship whatever, meeting only in the physical world
- You see your customers, their financial connections, and a retention window. Not other banks, not intent, not the real network
- The institution supplies a fragment, which makes the quality of the report matter more than the volume.
