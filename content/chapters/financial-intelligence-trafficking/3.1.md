The Global Legal Foundation A gainst Human Trafficking Human trafficking is not only a human-rights issue — it is a predicate offense to money laundering, meaning it directly falls under AML/CFT regulatory obligations. To detect and disrupt it effectively, compliance professionals must understand the international legal architecture that drives financial-sector responsibility. 1. United Nations Conventions A. UN Convention Against Transnational Organized Crime (UNTOC, 2000) Often referred to as the Palermo Convention, this treaty defines and criminalizes human trafficking as a form of organized crime. It obliges member states to: • Criminalize trafficking and related financial activities. • Enable asset freezing and confiscation. • Establish channels for cross-border cooperation and extradition. The Palermo Protocol, formally titled “Protocol to Prevent, Suppress and Punish Trafficking in Persons, Especially Women and Children”, serves as the backbone for all modern AML laws recognizing trafficking as a money-laundering predicate. B. UNODC’s Role The United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) acts as the technical secretariat to help governments implement anti-trafficking laws and supports FIUs and compliance regulators with typology guidance. UNODC’s “Human Trafficking and Migrant Smuggling Section” publishes annual trend reports identifying industries, payment routes, and laundering typologies. 2. Financial Action Task Force (FATF) Standards The FATF integrates human trafficking into its AML/CFT standards through a risk-based, data-driven approach.
