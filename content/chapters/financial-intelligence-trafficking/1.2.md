# The Scale, Sectors, and Economics of Exploitation

Trafficking generates an estimated USD 150 billion in profits every year, with over two-thirds originating from sexual exploitation, according to the International Labour Organization (ILO). The remainder stems from forced labour in agriculture, construction, manufacturing, hospitality, fishing and domestic work. Understanding where the money is made tells a compliance analyst where to look — different sectors produce different financial signatures.

## The economics of exploitation

Trafficking is unusual among crimes because the "product" is a person, and the revenue model is recurring. A drug shipment is sold once; an exploited person generates income daily for months or years. This changes the financial picture:

- **Low-value, high-frequency flows** — daily cash deposits, small remittances, wage deductions. None of it trips large-transaction thresholds, which is precisely why traffickers prefer it.
- **Multiple revenue streams per victim** — sexual exploitation, forced labour, begging, criminal activity (pickpocketing, drug running) and online exploitation can all run through the same control structure.
- **Upfront costs then long annuities** — recruitment, transport and document fraud are one-time costs; the exploitation phase amortises them over a long period, so even "low" per-victim returns are profitable at scale.

## Sector signatures

Each sector leaves a distinct financial pattern:

- **Agriculture and fishing** — seasonal cash economies, employer-controlled housing, wages paid in cash or into accounts the worker cannot freely access. Look for groups of accounts opened by the same "employer" contact, with near-identical transaction profiles.
- **Construction and manufacturing** — subcontracting chains that obscure the true employer; workers paid via intermediaries or "labour supply" companies. Layered corporate structures mirror the labour chain.
- **Hospitality and domestic work** — live-in workers with restricted movement; remittances sent home at unusual frequency or by third parties on their behalf.
- **Sexual exploitation** — online advertising payments, hotel and transport charges, and revenue consolidated through a small number of controllers rather than dispersed across victims.

## What this means for detection

The economics explain the detection problem: traffickers deliberately keep individual transactions small and routine, because the profit model does not require large movements — only persistent ones. That means the signals are rarely in single transactions. They are in **aggregation** (many small accounts under common control), **durability** (patterns that persist for months), and **dependency** (accounts where the holder has no independent economic life — every deposit matches a wage or fee schedule imposed by someone else).

## Key points

- Trafficking profits (~USD 150bn/year, ILO) are recurring, not one-off — the financial trail is long-duration and low-value.
- Over two-thirds of profits come from sexual exploitation; forced labour dominates the rest across agriculture, construction, hospitality, fishing and domestic work.
- Each sector produces a characteristic flow pattern — learn the sector to read the transactions.
- Detection lives in aggregation and durability, not transaction size — thresholds alone will miss almost all of it.
- The victim is often the account holder — the analyst's evidence must distinguish coercion from consent.
