# Defining Human Trafficking in the Context of Financial Crime

Deﬁning Human Trafficking in the Context of Financial Crime Human trafficking is not just a human rights violation — it is a multi-billion-dollar financial enterprise driven by profit and sustained by the global movement of illicit funds. According to the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC), trafficking involves the recruitment, transportation, transfer, harboring, or receipt of persons through coercion, deception, or abuse of power for the purpose of exploitation. From a financial perspective, these acts generate proceeds that must be laundered into the legitimate economy.

Every stage of the trafficking process — recruitment, transport, exploitation, and reinvestment — produces transactional data, payment traces, and behavioral footprints that compliance professionals can identify. The FATF classifies human trafficking as a predicate offense to money laundering, meaning that financial institutions and virtual asset service providers (VASPs) have a direct obligation to detect and report suspicious activity linked to it. Under Recommendation 20, any suspicion that funds derive from criminal activity — including human trafficking — must be reported as a Suspicious Transaction Report (STR) or Suspicious Activity Report (SAR) to the national Financial Intelligence Unit (FIU).

Despite these frameworks, traffickers continue to exploit financial systems due to the fragmented visibility across banks, fintechs, and crypto exchanges. Victims may be forced to open accounts, receive payments, or make withdrawals under direction of controllers, making the funds appear legitimate. For AML and compliance officers, the challenge lies in connecting seemingly normal patterns to hidden exploitation. An account showing modest daily transactions may, upon deeper review, belong to a small labor front controlling multiple victims’ wages. A remittance company might observe repeat transfers from various countries to a single recipient with no family relationship — a hallmark of trafficking payments.

Human trafficking is, at its core, a business — and therefore, it leaves a business trail. Recognizing this truth transforms the role of the compliance professional from a passive observer to an active participant in global protection.


## Topic 2 — The Scale, Sectors, and Economics of Exploitation Trafficking generates estimated USD 150 billion in profits every year, with over two-thirds originating from sexual exploitation, according to the International Labour Organization (ILO). The rest stems from forced labor in sectors such as agriculture, construction, manufacturing, hospitality, fishing, and domestic work.


> **Why it matters:** Trafficking is a financial crime before it's a human rights crime — the exploitation is monetised, and the proceeds must be moved, hidden and spent. That makes financial institutions the tripwire: victims' accounts are often controlled by traffickers, front businesses commingle illicit revenue, and the same AML/SAR machinery used for drug proceeds applies here. Recognising the financial indicators is what turns a compliance team into an enforcement partner.
