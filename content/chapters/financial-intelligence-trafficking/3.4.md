# Public–Private Partnerships (PPPs) and Collaborative Models
1. The Shift from Individual Oversight to Collective Intelligence Traditional AML frameworks focused on individual institutions detecting suspicious transactions. However, human trafficking’s complexity demands a networked response — one that transcends corporate, jurisdictional, and sectoral boundaries. Public–Private Partnerships (PPPs) bridge this gap by creating secure information-sharing environments between regulators, FIUs, and financial institutions. 2. Global Examples of PPPs in Action
- Project Protect (Canada) Launched by the Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP) and FINTRAC, this initiative engages banks, MSBs, and NGOs to combat human trafficking. Results include:
- Over 2,000 actionable STRs filed within one year.
- Detection of multiple transnational trafficking syndicates.
- Improved victim identification through financial linkage analysis.
- JMLIT (United Kingdom) The Joint Money Laundering Intelligence Taskforce unites the UK Home Office, NCA, and major banks. It pioneered structured data sharing via “intelligence cells,” enabling analysts to connect seemingly unrelated accounts across institutions.
- AUSTRAC-led Fintel Alliance (Australia) AUSTRAC’s Fintel Alliance coordinates between government agencies, big banks, and fintech companies. Its “Human Trafficking and Child Exploitation Working Group” produces typology guides now used globally.
- U.S. FinCEN PPP Framework The FinCEN Exchange facilitates public–private briefings where banks receive specific typology alerts, such as recruitment-fee schemes or online exploitation payments, based on ongoing investigations. 3. Benefits of PPP Participation
- Enhanced typology precision: Shared intelligence produces more accurate monitoring models.
- Reduced false positives: Collaborative data improves contextual risk understanding.
