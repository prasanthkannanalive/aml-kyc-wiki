## 2.1 Keys, addresses and transaction anatomy

To do compliance work on virtual assets you need to understand what a transaction actually is — not the trading-app version, but the ledger version.

### The key pair

Every virtual-asset holding is controlled by a **key pair**:

- A **private key** — a long random number that proves ownership. Whoever holds it controls the funds. It must never be shared, and losing it means losing the funds permanently. There is no "forgot password" on a blockchain.
- A **public key** — mathematically derived from the private key, safe to share. It is used to generate **addresses** — the strings you see on screen, like `bc1q...` for Bitcoin or `0x...` for Ethereum.

Think of the private key as the signature on a cheque and the address as the account number. You can receive money knowing only the address; you can only spend it with the private key.

### What a transaction contains

A blockchain transaction is a small structured record:

- **Inputs** — references to previous outputs being spent, each signed by the private key holder
- **Outputs** — new amounts assigned to addresses, plus optionally a change output back to the sender
- **Amount(s)** — the value being transferred (in the smallest unit — satoshis for Bitcoin, wei for Ethereum)
- **Fees** — a small payment to the network validators, which is why transactions confirm faster when fees are higher

For Bitcoin, amounts are **public**. For privacy coins and some newer chains, amounts may be hidden — but the transaction structure itself is always visible on a public ledger.

### Why this matters for compliance

An analyst looking at a blockchain explorer sees exactly what any other analyst sees: addresses, amounts, timestamps, and the transaction graph connecting them. There is no "account name" field, no "customer ID" — the only identity data is what the VASP attached at the edge. This is why **clustering and attribution** (Chapter 2.3) are the core analytical skills: they turn raw addresses into entities.

### Addresses are not accounts

A critical conceptual shift from banking: an address is not an account. There is no "balance" stored anywhere — the balance of an address is computed by summing all unspent outputs addressed to it. Users routinely generate new addresses for privacy, and exchanges use thousands of addresses to manage customer funds. **One address is not one customer.** This is the single most common source of error in naive blockchain analysis.
