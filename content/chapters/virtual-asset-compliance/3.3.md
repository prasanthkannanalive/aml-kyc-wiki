## 3.3 Following the fiat off-ramp

Crypto crime is ultimately about **fiat** — the dollars, euros and naira that criminals actually want. The off-ramp is where virtual assets convert back to spendable money, and it is where most compliance leverage exists.

### The off-ramp landscape

**Centralized exchanges** — the primary off-ramp. A user sells crypto on Binance, Coinbase, Kraken or a local exchange and withdraws fiat to their bank account. The exchange knows the customer's identity (KYC), the bank sees the fiat deposit, and the transaction is reportable at multiple points.

**Peer-to-peer (P2P) platforms** — LocalBitcoins, Paxful and successors connect buyers and sellers directly. The platform may have minimal KYC, and settlement often happens through cash, gift cards or money transfer services — creating gaps in the fiat trail.

**Crypto ATMs** — physical machines that exchange cash for crypto. Growing in number but still small volume; useful for small amounts and for avoiding banking channels.

**Payment processors** — BitPay, Coinbase Commerce and similar let merchants accept crypto. The processor converts to fiat and deposits to the merchant's bank — a regulated off-ramp embedded in commerce.

**OTC desks** — over-the-counter desks handle large trades for institutional and high-net-worth clients. Less transparent than exchange order books, more personalized service, and historically attractive to money launderers because of the discretion.

### The compliance leverage points

Each off-ramp type has different control strengths:

**Exchanges** — strongest controls. KYC at onboarding, transaction monitoring, Travel Rule data exchange, sanctions screening, and SAR filing obligations. When a criminal cashes out through a compliant exchange, there are multiple interception points.

**P2P platforms** — weakest controls. Minimal KYC, cash settlement, cross-border transfers through informal channels. FATF has flagged P2P as a significant gap.

**ATMs** — moderate controls. Operators are often MSBs with AML obligations, but transaction limits and geographic coverage vary.

**Payment processors** — strong controls at the merchant level but create a layer of indirection: the processor sees the crypto payment, the merchant's bank sees only the fiat deposit.

### Following the money backwards

For investigators, the off-ramp is where the trail gets interesting:

- **Exchange deposit addresses** are attributed — you can identify which exchange received the funds and request customer data through legal process
- **Withdrawal patterns** — a user who deposits crypto and immediately withdraws fiat is a cash-out pattern; one who deposits, waits and trades is different
- **Fiat destination** — the bank account receiving the fiat withdrawal is the ultimate identifier. Following it requires bank cooperation or legal process

### The stablecoin wrinkle

Stablecoins complicate the off-ramp analysis because they are crypto that behaves like fiat. A user can move USDT between wallets, off-ramps and DeFi protocols without ever touching a bank account. The "cash-out" moment may be a stablecoin-to-fiat conversion at an exchange, a payment to a merchant that accepts stablecoins, or a transfer to an unhosted wallet that never touches the regulated system at all.

This is why FATF's guidance treats stablecoins as virtual assets subject to the same obligations — they are the bridge between the crypto and fiat worlds, and the compliance perimeter must cover both sides.
