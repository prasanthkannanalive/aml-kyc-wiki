## 1.2 Why virtual assets attract financial crime

Virtual assets did not create financial crime; they inherited it and added new capabilities. Understanding which capabilities matter tells you which controls matter.

### Speed and irreversibility

A bank wire can be recalled, frozen or flagged mid-flight by the sending institution. A blockchain transaction, once confirmed, is final. There is no chargeback, no recall window, no "pending" state a compliance team can exploit. For a fraudster this is ideal: the victim's money is gone before anyone can react. For a compliance team it means **prevention is the only real defence** — by the time you see a transaction, it has already settled.

### Pseudonymity, not anonymity

This distinction is the most misunderstood idea in crypto compliance. Bitcoin addresses are not anonymous — they are **pseudonymous**. Every transaction is public and permanent; what is missing is the link between an address and a real-world identity. That link exists at the edges: exchanges that know their customers, payment processors, data breaches, law-enforcement seizures. Once an address is attributed to a person or entity, their entire transaction history — past and future — becomes visible.

This is why blockchain analytics works at all. The ledger is a complete, tamper-proof record; the only question is attribution. Sophisticated criminals understand this and try to break attribution through mixers, chain-hopping and layering across many addresses — which is exactly what Modules 2 and 3 teach you to detect.

### Borderlessness

A Bitcoin transaction from Lagos to London settles in minutes, costs cents, and touches no bank, no correspondent, no currency-control regime. Traditional laundering required moving physical cash or routing through complicit banks; crypto collapses distance and intermediaries. For terrorist financing and sanctions evasion this is transformative — value crosses borders faster than any legal process can respond.

### Accessibility to the unbanked — and to the sanctioned

Crypto wallets require no bank account, no ID, no credit check. This is genuinely liberating for people excluded from the financial system — and equally available to sanctioned regimes, terrorist networks and sanctioned individuals who cannot access dollar clearing. North Korea's Lazarus Group is the canonical example: estimated to have stolen billions in crypto to fund weapons programmes, operating entirely outside the reach of correspondent banking.

### Programmability and DeFi

Smart contracts — self-executing code on blockchains like Ethereum — enable **decentralised finance (DeFi)**: lending, trading and derivatives with no company, no CEO, no compliance officer. The code is the intermediary. FATF has struggled to bring DeFi inside the regulatory perimeter because there is often no legal entity to license — Chapter 5.4 covers where this is heading.
