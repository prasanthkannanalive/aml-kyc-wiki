## 5.4 The frontier: DeFi, unhosted wallets and what comes next

This chapter looks at where crypto AML is heading — the gaps that remain, the technologies emerging, and the regulatory responses taking shape.

### DeFi — the compliance frontier

**Decentralized Finance (DeFi)** protocols provide financial services through smart contracts without intermediaries. Lending, trading, derivatives and yield farming all happen through code, not companies.

The compliance problem: **there is no legal entity to regulate**. A Uniswap pool is a smart contract, not a company. A Compound lending market has no CEO, no compliance officer, no AML programme. FATF's guidance attempts to capture DeFi through the "front-end operator" concept — the website or interface that connects users to the protocol — but implementation is contested and incomplete.

**What this means for compliance**:

- VASPs integrating with DeFi protocols face unclear obligations — are they providing a service to a VASP? To an unregulated protocol?
- Users interacting with DeFi directly bypass VASP controls entirely — no KYC, no Travel Rule, no transaction monitoring
- DeFi protocols themselves are increasingly targeted by sanctions and enforcement (Tornado Cash being the landmark case)

### Unhosted wallets — the structural gap

Unhosted (self-custody) wallets are the single biggest gap in crypto AML. When users control their own private keys, no VASP sees the transaction, collects the data, or files the SAR.

FATF's response: require VASPs to collect information about unhosted wallet transactions above thresholds. But this only works when at least one side of the transaction touches a VASP — direct wallet-to-wallet transfers remain invisible.

**Emerging solutions**:

- **Wallet attribution services** — tools that link unhosted wallets to identities through OSINT, exchange deposit patterns, and behavioral analysis
- **Regulatory pressure on wallet providers** — requiring hardware wallet manufacturers or software wallet developers to implement AML controls (technically difficult, legally contested)
- **Travel Rule extension** — proposals to require unhosted wallet owners to register or identify themselves for transactions above thresholds

### Privacy-enhancing technologies

**Zero-knowledge proofs** — cryptographic techniques that allow verification without revealing underlying data. Zcash's shielded transactions and Ethereum's emerging privacy features use this technology.

**Fully homomorphic encryption** — allows computation on encrypted data without decryption. If realized for blockchain transactions, this could make even public ledgers opaque.

**Stealth addresses and viewing keys** — techniques that hide recipient addresses while allowing authorized parties to view transaction details.

These technologies are in early stages but represent a long-term threat to blockchain analytics. Compliance must prepare for a future where transaction data is encrypted by default.

### Cross-chain interoperability

**Bridges and atomic swaps** — protocols that move value between blockchains without centralized intermediaries. They are the primary chain-hopping mechanism and a major analytical challenge.

**Interoperability standards** — emerging standards for cross-chain communication will make chain-hopping easier and harder to trace simultaneously.

### AI and machine learning

**Automated tracing** — machine learning models that identify clustering patterns, detect mixers, and score risk faster than manual analysis.

**Anomaly detection** — behavioral analytics that identify suspicious patterns without predefined rules.

**Natural language processing** — analyzing forum posts, scam reports, and OSINT to identify wallet addresses and entity relationships.

**Predictive analytics** — forecasting which addresses are likely to be used for crime based on historical patterns.

### Regulatory convergence

The global regulatory landscape is converging toward FATF standards but with significant national variations:

- **EU** — MiCA provides a comprehensive framework; Travel Rule implementation through CASP regulations
- **US** — FinCEN MSB rules, SEC/CFTC jurisdiction, state-level requirements; Travel Rule through FinCEN guidance
- **UK** — FCA cryptoasset registration, Travel Rule through FCA rules
- **Asia** — Singapore's Payment Services Act, Japan's Payment Services Act, Hong Kong's VATP licensing
- **Emerging markets** — many jurisdictions still developing frameworks; some are becoming crypto hubs precisely because of regulatory gaps

### What compliance teams should do now

1. **Invest in chain-analytics capability** — whether in-house or through vendors, the ability to trace and score crypto exposure is table stakes
2. **Implement Travel Rule properly** — treat it as a core operational requirement, not an afterthought
3. **Monitor DeFi exposure** — understand which DeFi protocols your customers interact with and what risks they pose
4. **Prepare for privacy coins** — decide whether to delist them, restrict them, or apply enhanced monitoring
5. **Build cross-functional expertise** — crypto AML requires technical understanding that traditional AML teams may lack; invest in training
6. **Stay current** — the regulatory and threat landscape evolves monthly; compliance programmes must evolve with it
