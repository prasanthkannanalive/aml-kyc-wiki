## 2.3 The public ledger, clustering and attribution

The blockchain is public. That is simultaneously its greatest vulnerability for criminals and its greatest asset for investigators. This chapter explains how analysts turn a sea of anonymous addresses into attributed entities.

### What "public" actually means

Every confirmed transaction on Bitcoin, Ethereum and most major chains is recorded on a ledger that anyone can download and query. Blockchain explorers (blockchain.com, etherscan.io, mempool.space) provide free interfaces. There is no access control, no subscription, no warrant required — the data is simply there.

What the ledger does **not** contain: names, emails, IP addresses, or any link to real-world identity. The link is established externally — at exchanges, payment processors, or through law-enforcement action.

### Clustering heuristics

Since one person controls many addresses, analysts use heuristics to group addresses likely controlled by the same entity:

**Common-input-ownership heuristic** — if multiple addresses appear as inputs to the same transaction, they were probably controlled by the same entity (you can't sign inputs for addresses you don't own). This is the most reliable clustering method for UTXO chains.

**Change-address heuristic** — the output returning to the sender is usually the one not used before and not round-numbered. Identifying change addresses helps map wallet structure.

**Peeling chain detection** — a pattern where a large amount is split into many small outputs, each peeled off to a different address. Common in exchange hot-wallet withdrawals and in layering.

These heuristics are probabilistic, not certain. Sophisticated users deliberately break them — using CoinJoin transactions where multiple users' inputs are mixed, making common-input-ownership unreliable.

### Attribution — the real work

Clustering groups addresses; **attribution** names them. The primary attribution sources:

- **Exchange hot and cold wallets** — exchanges publish or are identified as controllers of specific addresses. When a user deposits to an exchange, the deposit address is attributed. Chain-analytics vendors maintain massive databases of attributed addresses.
- **Law-enforcement seizures** — when authorities seize exchange assets or dark-web market wallets, those addresses become attributed to specific entities.
- **OSINT** — forum posts, scam reports, ransomware notes that include wallet addresses directly.
- **Customer data** — a VASP knows which of its customers controls which deposit addresses.

### The analyst's workflow

A typical tracing engagement:

1. **Identify the starting point** — a flagged deposit address, a ransomware payment address, a sanctions-listed entity's known wallet
2. **Expand the cluster** — use heuristics to find all addresses likely controlled by the same entity
3. **Follow the flow** — trace outputs forward through the transaction graph, noting exchanges, mixers, and high-risk services
4. **Attribute the endpoints** — determine which addresses belong to regulated VASPs (where identity data exists) versus unhosted wallets (where it does not)
5. **Score the risk** — assess exposure to sanctioned entities, darknet markets, mixers and other high-risk services

This workflow is what chain-analytics vendors productize — Chapter 3.1 covers how their tools work.
