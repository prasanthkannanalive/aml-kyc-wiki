## 1.1 What a virtual asset actually is

The phrase "cryptocurrency" is a marketing term that has outlived its accuracy. The FATF definition that matters for compliance is broader and more precise: a **virtual asset** is a digital representation of value that can be digitally traded or transferred and can be used for payment or investment purposes — and it does not include digital representations of fiat currencies, securities, or other financial assets already covered elsewhere.

### The families that matter

**Native cryptocurrencies** — Bitcoin, Ethereum, Litecoin and hundreds of others. Each has its own ledger, its own rules for validating transactions, and its own community. Bitcoin pioneered the model; most others are variations on it.

**Tokens** — assets issued on top of an existing blockchain. Ethereum's ERC-20 standard made it trivial to create a token, which is why thousands exist. The critical distinction for compliance is **utility versus security**: a token that represents a claim on an enterprise (a security token) falls under securities regulation, not virtual-asset rules; a token that represents access to a service (a utility token) sits in the virtual-asset perimeter. Many tokens are deliberately ambiguous — that ambiguity is often the point.

**Stablecoins** — tokens designed to hold a stable value, usually pegged to a fiat currency. Tether (USDT) and USD Coin (USDC) dominate. Stablecoins are the settlement layer of crypto crime: ransomware demands, darknet market prices and cross-border fraud proceeds are overwhelmingly denominated in them because a "dollar" token moves like crypto but spends like cash.

**Non-fungible tokens (NFTs)** — unique tokens representing ownership of a specific digital item. Mostly collectible art and profile pictures, but also used for money laundering through wash trading — creating fake sales between controlled wallets to give illicit funds a veneer of legitimate trading profits.

**Central bank digital currencies (CBDCs)** — digital versions of sovereign money issued by central banks. Not virtual assets under the FATF definition because they are digital fiat — but they matter to this guide because they change the competitive landscape and raise questions about programmable money and surveillance.

### What "self-custody" means

The defining technical feature of virtual assets is that value can move **without any intermediary**. A bank transfer requires the bank; a Bitcoin transfer requires only the sender's private key and the recipient's address. This is what makes crypto attractive to criminals — and what makes compliance at the *intermediary* layer so important. When a regulated exchange processes a withdrawal, the exchange is the control point; when someone sends directly wallet-to-wallet, nobody is.

That last fact — unhosted wallets — is the single biggest structural challenge in crypto AML and occupies Chapter 5.4.

> **Exam trap:** The Travel Rule is the most-tested VASP concept. Like wire transfers, virtual-asset transfers above a threshold must carry originator and beneficiary information between VASPs. The exam trap is assuming crypto is exempt from AML — FATF Recommendation 15 and regimes like MiCA explicitly bring VASPs under the same obligations as banks. Pseudonymous does not mean anonymous: every on-chain transaction is publicly visible.
