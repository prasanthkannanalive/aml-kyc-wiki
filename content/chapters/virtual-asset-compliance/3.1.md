## 3.1 How chain-analytics vendors work

Chain-analytics is the commercial industry built on the attribution problem described in Chapter 2.3. Companies like Chainalysis, Elliptic, TRM Labs and CipherTrace productize the heuristics and attribution databases that individual analysts cannot replicate.

### What they actually do

**Address attribution at scale** — vendors maintain databases of millions of attributed addresses: exchange wallets, darknet market wallets, mixer contracts, ransomware addresses, sanctioned entity wallets, scam addresses. They combine public data (blockchain explorers, OFAC listings), proprietary intelligence (law-enforcement partnerships, undercover operations), and customer-contributed data (VASPs sharing their own attribution).

**Clustering algorithms** — automated versions of the heuristics in Chapter 2.3, refined with machine learning. They identify wallet clusters, entity groupings and ownership patterns across chains.

**Risk scoring** — the core product. Every address and transaction receives a risk score based on its exposure to high-risk services: percentage of funds traced to darknet markets, mixers, ransomware, scams or sanctioned entities. Scores range from low (clean exchange wallet) to severe (direct sanctioned entity).

**Investigation tools** — visual graph explorers that let analysts click through transaction flows, filter by service type, and export case files. These are the "Bloomberg terminals" of crypto compliance.

### How the data gets there

Vendor attribution comes from several channels:

- **Exchange partnerships** — major exchanges share wallet attribution data with vendors, creating a virtuous cycle: the exchange knows its deposit addresses, the vendor knows they're exchange addresses, and every transaction to those addresses is automatically attributed
- **Law-enforcement cooperation** — vendors work with FBI, Europol, Chainalysis's own law-enforcement clients to attribute seized wallets and identified criminal infrastructure
- **Undercover operations** — vendors have been known to operate their own exchanges or services to generate attribution ground truth
- **OSINT and scraping** — forum posts, scam reports, and public wallet addresses from ransomware notes

### The coverage problem

Chain-analytics is powerful but not omniscient:

- **Privacy coins** — Monero and shielded Zcash are largely opaque; vendors admit they cannot trace them reliably
- **Cross-chain complexity** — value moving through multiple bridges and DEXs creates gaps in coverage; vendor bridge datasets are incomplete
- **DeFi protocols** — smart-contract interactions create transaction patterns that don't map cleanly to the address-cluster model
- **New services** — attribution databases lag behind new exchanges, mixers and scams; a service launched last week is unattributed until the vendors catch up

For VASPs, the practical implication is that chain-analytics is a **screening tool**, not a definitive answer. A high-risk score triggers enhanced due diligence; a low score does not guarantee safety. Chapter 4.3 covers how to integrate these scores into monitoring rules.
